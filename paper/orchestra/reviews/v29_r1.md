ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer r1 (robot-hand designer; has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them).

The paper makes two claims about one planar servo-driven finger (PIP only, MCP held).

(C1) Room. Putting the PIP servo on the proximal phalanx and using a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness (3-9 mm) and required flexion (90/120/150 deg). The evidence is an optimisation study (GA/NSGA-II, three seeds) of four joints, with closed-form clearance and coverage laws (Props. 1-2) as intuition. The claim is stated in a falsifiable form ("one condition where 2R-DD matched it would refute this") and holds in all 10 conditions, by 2.4-14.6 points. It is well supported for the metric as defined. Its practical weight is less certain. Coverage is a cylinder-inscribed metric averaged over three poses, and nothing links a 2-15 point coverage gain to grasp outcomes. The comparison is also not uniformly in the CFL's favour: 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90 deg and at thin pads at 120 deg (Fig. 3a-b). The paper says so, but the bullet "remote servos and a CFL keep more contact surface" bundles two effects that Fig. 3 separates. The worst-case mechanical advantage of 0.41-0.58 means the PIP servo gives up roughly half its torque. For a small-servo hand that is a real cost, and here it is only reported, not traded.

(C2) Keep the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton. When a pad change forces a redesign, the redesign should keep the contact-surface path c(q) (Delta_contact) rather than the skeleton (Delta_skel). A fixed axis cannot keep it (Corollary 1, a clean and useful bound), and the CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound at almost no coverage cost. Both the kinematic part and the bound are convincing. The simulation part is weaker than the abstract suggests. Under a reused joint-impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured pad effect rank-correlates with Delta_contact (rho 0.80-0.86), across two simulators, two contact models and three tasks. The paper itself shows why: a face that moves changes how hard the reused setpoint presses (preload), and limit load scales with preload. Given that mechanism, the correlation is close to built in: Delta_contact is a geometric proxy for the penetration the same command produces. That is still a useful design rule, but it is a statement about one protocol, not about separating "interface from finger" in general. Table I makes the scope explicit. Under re-zeroing at touch, which is what I and most groups running servo hands actually do (current or contact threshold), and under an ideal torque command, the skeleton predicts the error better (0.88 and 0.71 vs 0.44 and 0.69). The paper calls these protocols ones that "need an ideal part". In my experience, current-threshold re-zeroing on Dynamixel-class servos is routine and needs no extra hardware, so that framing reads as minimising the counter-evidence. With 14-16 hands, Delta_contact's lead over Delta_skel is not significant. And even the best redesign (R) leaves 21% and 41% error at 7 and 9 mm, which is the same size as many pad effects one would want to measure.

On contact realism: the hydroelastic case deliberately uses the same 0.1 MPa, 3 mm compliant layer on every hand, so the thick compliant pad that motivates the paper is never simulated as thick and compliant. The pad-swap example (48% vs -41%) is in point contact, "given the soft pad's stiffness". The tactile-module motivation in the abstract and intro is not evaluated at all. Routing tactile wiring across a pinless crossed four-bar, and fitting a rigid sensor body under a moving virtual pole, are the hard parts in practice, and both go unaddressed.

Stage 2 plan: one finger with swappable pads and CFL links, O at 3 mm, and R, M, U at 7 mm with Delta_contact of 0.9/2.3/3.9 mm and falling Delta_skel. Markers measure Delta_contact, and a planar-slide pull-to-slip test with bare and textured covers (5x4x5) ends in a Page trend test. The design is good: Delta_contact and Delta_skel move in opposite directions across R, M and U, so the two hypotheses predict opposite trends, and the predicted 21/72/124% separation is large. Missing: (i) a direct preload or pad-force measurement, to confirm the mechanism and not just the end effect; (ii) any mention of servo deadband, backlash in the four-bar pins, and pad thickness tolerance (+-0.3-0.5 mm on cast silicone), which together are comparable to the 1 mm Delta_contact threshold; (iii) a run of the re-zero-at-touch protocol on the same hardware, which would show whether the rule matters under real practice; (iv) a check that bare vs textured cover is a large enough interface effect to measure against slide friction.

Presentation is compact but very dense. Many symbols are introduced in a few lines (S1, S2, T_i, Q_eval, z, Delta_t, c0, F1 vs Fp). The step from Prop. 3 to Eq. (8) is hard to follow. Fig. 2c's 3D surface is hard to read, Fig. 4c has overlapping labels, and the Results section packs too many numbers into each sentence. Calling c(q) the "contact Jacobian" is defensible (Eq. 5), but Delta_contact is a path difference, and the paper should say plainly that only one column, at one face point, is kept.

My scores rest on: C1 well supported but of moderate practical weight; C2's kinematic content sound and novel, but its empirical content close to a consequence of the chosen protocol, and reversed under the protocol many practitioners use; a falsifiable Stage-2 plan that needs mechanism and tolerance checks. Overall this is a reasonable Stage-1 submission with a clear falsification plan.
