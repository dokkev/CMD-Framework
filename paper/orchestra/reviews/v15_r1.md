THOUGHT:

My reading as someone who builds servo-driven research hands and runs grasp/tactile experiments on them.

What the paper is really about. Two linked claims about one planar PIP joint (MCP held at 0). (C1) Moving the PIP servo onto the proximal phalanx and replacing the pin with a crossed four-bar (CFL) leaves more usable pad surface at the joint than a servo-at-joint 2R finger, because the four-bar's virtual pole can sit at or near the contact face instead of inside the phalanx. (C2) When a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface, what should be preserved is the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal one (the authors call this the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, c(q)), not the fingertip/skeleton path; a fixed axis cannot do this without recessing the pad (closed-form lower bound, Eq. 8), and in simulation the change Δ_contact predicts the error in a measured interface effect (slip load change under friction/stiffness/shape changes) when one command or torque is reused.

What I like. The pole framing is clean and gives one picture for both problems. Eq. (2), (3), (6)–(8) are elementary but correct as far as I can check (Fig. 2 numbers, 2tan(q/2) per mm, √3Δh for the fixed-axis family at 120° all check out), and they are tied to the numerical evaluator (10^-13 on 51 designs, gap within 0.05 mm median). The hypotheses are stated with explicit failure conditions, the stats are pre-specified with Holm correction, and the authors include uncomfortable controls: the 2R-remote ablation (which beats CFL at 90°), the start-offset correction in Fig. 4d, a torque-reuse protocol, a hand-independent grid, robustness sweeps over solver parameters, and an explicit statement of load-step resolution. The Stage 2 protocol (same servos/bodies, swap pad modules + CFL links, R vs U with the same 7 mm pads so that real compliance differences cancel) is a well-chosen design. The question in C2 — measurement validity of interface studies across hand redesigns — is a real problem that I have seen in tactile-fingertip papers and is rarely stated this sharply.

Where I am unconvinced.

(1) The C2 result under the reused flexion command is close to built in. In the simulation, every pad thickness shares one contact model ("so E is what the redesign adds"), the finger is position-controlled through a joint stiffness K0, and the interface effect is a slip-limit change. Under those conditions the grasp force is set almost entirely by how far the faces penetrate the cylinder at the commanded angle, which is exactly what Δ_contact measures. That Δ_contact correlates with the error is therefore expected, and the paper itself shows the mechanism (distal force ratio ρ = 0.62, its deviation ρ = 0.94). The torque protocol is the more informative test, and it is the protocol most practitioners actually use (current-controlled grasp closure): there the error falls to 3–40%, the analysis is on 16 hands, and the partial correlation holding force fixed is labelled post hoc. The practical case for C2 is thus weaker than the abstract's "95–228%" headline suggests, which comes from the position-command protocol with a different (fixed-axis) joint type.

(2) Pads as geometry only. The motivating interfaces are thick compliant pads and camera-based tactile modules, whose defining property is compliance and conformation (patch growth, pressure distribution, viscoelasticity). Modelling all thicknesses with one penalty-contact model in MuJoCo-Warp removes exactly the physics that makes thick pads interesting, and "pad stiffness" is a solver contact-stiffness parameter, not a deformable pad. The authors acknowledge this for Stage 2, but it limits what Study 3 can say about real interface studies.

(3) The design population drives the correlations. The 15/14/16 hands are R, U, and author-selected redesigns at set Δ_contact that keep or change the skeleton. The Spearman ρ against each candidate depends on how this set was sampled; Δ_skel even comes out negative (−0.40, −0.51) because of the Eq. (7) coupling and the selection. "Ranks first among five quantities in every setting" rests on point estimates: in the direct comparison Δ_contact beats coverage/thickness/transmission in only 7 of 12 bootstrap intervals, and Δ_skel is "separated" only by adding the fixed-axis family, which is a different joint type (revolute) than the CFL redesigns, confounding joint type with skeleton preservation. Transmission is undone by construction (each hand's transmission table), so its ρ≈0 is not evidence. The pad-shape ρ = 0.94 is on six hands. Small n plus designer-chosen samples makes me want a pre-specified random sample of redesigns along the Pareto front.

(4) "Keep the contact Jacobian" is stronger than what is kept. J here is the single PIP column for planar motion with MCP fixed, and since φ = q on every design, J differs only through the pole path; Δ_contact is a max positional deviation of one face point, not a Jacobian difference. The authors themselves note the MCP column is not kept and that keeping c(q) does not keep contact mechanics. Calling this "the contact Jacobian" will be read by the manipulation community as something far more general (Montana/Murray contact Jacobian over all joints and contact frames). "Relative contact-face motion" would be honest.

(5) C1 is shown against a weak comparator and ignores costs a hand designer cares about. H1 is CFL vs 2R-DD only; the stronger competitor, 2R-remote, beats CFL at 90° at every thickness and at 3 mm/120°. The gain over 2R-DD is 2.4–14.6 "points" of a cylinder-inscribed coverage metric averaged over three angles — I don't know whether that matters for any grasp outcome. "Mechanical advantage is not constrained": the CFL optima have worst-case MA 0.41–0.58, i.e. roughly half the PIP torque of a direct-drive servo. On small servo hands grip force is often the binding constraint, and backlash/compliance in a crossed four-bar under load, link wear, and ROM to 150° with transmission angle ≥30° are real concerns. None of this is weighed against coverage. The hand in Fig. 1a is CAD only; there is no hardware evidence yet of the claimed room, of force capacity, or that links can be swapped without disturbing calibration.

(6) "It can be kept" is partial. CFL redesigns still change c(q) by up to 4.31 mm (10–51% of the bound), and Study 3 shows what that costs: R at 9 mm still errs 41%. So the CFL reduces but does not solve the problem at the thicknesses that motivate the paper; the 9 mm case deserves to be stated as a limitation of C2, not folded into "R errs least".

(7) Venue fit. Stage 1 requires hypotheses and methods to be complete and the insights stated even under negative outcomes. Hypotheses and methods are there. What is missing is the negative-result reading: if R does not beat U on hardware, is the principle wrong, is the sim contact model wrong, or is the effect below repeatability? The Stage 2 C2 test also only checks R < U on one pair; it does not test Δ_contact as a predictor against the other candidates on hardware, which is what C2 actually claims. A measurement of servo/linkage repeatability vs the predicted 21% vs 124% gap would also help.

(8) Presentation. The paper is extremely dense: many symbols (G_sym, S1, S2, x0, T_i, z(q), Δt, Δd), short declarative sentences that compress multi-step arguments, numbers packed into running text. A reader from manipulation will struggle to recover the Study 3 protocol (what exactly is O's "contacts" vs the grid; how E is aggregated; what Δ(O) magnitudes are). Fig. 5a mixes three changes and three shades in one small panel. Related work is acceptably focused per venue rules but misses the most relevant linkage-finger design literature (underactuated linkage fingers, e.g. Birglen/Laliberté/Gosselin; Yale OpenHand-style modular fingers) and soft-finger contact modelling (e.g. Xydas & Kao), both directly tied to C1 and to the contact-model assumption.

Decision reasoning. For Stage 1, the hypotheses are precise and falsifiable, the analytic part is sound, and the simulation is carefully controlled with honest ablations. The main threats (built-in correlation under position command, geometry-only pads, design-selection dependence, terminology overreach, unweighted MA cost) are serious but addressable within the Stage 2 plan and with rewording. I lean toward accepting as a Stage-1 abstract, with the expectation that the claims are toned down and the hardware test is strengthened.
