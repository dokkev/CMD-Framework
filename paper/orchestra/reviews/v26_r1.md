ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer r1 (robot-hand designer; has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them).

Central claims and evidence.

C1 (room for thick pads). Claim: moving the PIP servo onto the proximal phalanx and using a crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger (2R-DD) at every pad thickness (3-9 mm) and required flexion (90/120/150 deg). Evidence: a GA/NSGA-II design search, three seeds per condition, with CFL refined from neighbouring designs (Fig. 3). CFL wins all 10 comparable conditions by 2.4-14.6 points, and its worst seed beats 2R-DD's best seed in 9 of 10. The 2R-remote ablation is the most useful part for a designer. It separates "get the servo body off the joint" (enough at 90 deg) from "make the pole follow the face" (needed at 120-150 deg). The clearance law (Prop. 1, Eq. 2) explains it: the fixed-axis gap grows as 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of face depth. The geometry is correct and well tied to the numbers (3.46 mm/mm at 120 deg). Weak points: (i) mechanical advantage is left free, and the CFL optima transmit at 0.41-0.58 against 1.0 at the joint. On a servo-chain hand, where the per-joint torque budget is the binding constraint, halving distal-pad force is a first-order cost, not a footnote. Without at least one result under an MA floor, "keeps more contact surface" is a Pareto statement presented as a dominance statement. (ii) Coverage is a geometric score: the share of an inscribed cylinder's V that both pads cover, at three poses. It does not reward what thick pads are for, namely conformity and contact area. Even so, the C1 result is believable and of practical value. I would expect it to hold on hardware.

C2 (keep the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton). Claim: under a reused impedance setpoint, the redesign-induced error in a measured interface effect tracks Delta_contact (the change in the path of a distal-face point in the proximal frame), not skeleton motion, coverage, transmission or pad thickness. A fixed axis cannot keep Delta_contact without recessing the face (Cor. 1, a clean closed-form bound), and the CFL achieves 10-51% of that bound at almost no coverage cost (Study 2, Fig. 4). Study 2 is solid. The bound is simple and correct, the spacer alternative is costed honestly (8.6-18.2 points of coverage), and Fig. 4d's caveat about 2R-DD/RCJ falling below the bound is stated openly.

Study 3 carries the main empirical weight. The rank correlations are consistent across friction, stiffness and the grid (rho 0.80-0.86). They are bootstrapped over hands, Holm-corrected, and partly pre-registered, and Delta_contact survives with Delta_skel held fixed and with setpoint force partialled out (0.64). The negative and mixed results are reported: the reversed share does not rise with Delta_contact, and under the "touch" protocol the skeleton (0.88) and not Delta_contact (0.44) predicts. I appreciate that. Three things limit how far I can take C2.

(a) Partly by construction. Under position control at fixed stiffness, the grasp force is set by over-closure, i.e. by where the faces meet the object relative to q0. A predictor that measures exactly where the faces are will track the error. The interesting finding is the negative one: the skeleton does not suffice, and a Delta_skel = 0 revolute redesign errs 95-228%. The positive finding is closer to expected.

(b) Narrow scope. The result is tied to one protocol (reused setpoint). Under the touch protocol the ordering flips toward the skeleton. Under the ideal-torque protocol the transmission matters (-0.50). The authors argue that servo hands run reused setpoints, which is true of LEAP-style policies. Still, the title-level claim "redesigns that keep the contact Jacobian" reads more general than one protocol class.

(c) The contact model is point contact against meshes, with pad stiffness as a normal spring. For thick compliant pads this leaves out the patch growth, tangential compliance and rolling that motivate thick pads in the first place. The headline reversal (+48% reported vs -41% true for a softer, thicker pad on U) is a striking demonstration. But the "true" value is O carrying the soft pad's stiffness at 3 mm geometry. That counterfactual exists only in this simulator, and it depends on the contact model the paper flags as its main limitation.

Also, the "independent up to about 7 mm" conclusion is generous. R errs 21-25% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm, against a 20% tolerance that is not justified.

Stage 1 fit. The problem, hypotheses H1-H3 with explicit failure conditions, methodology and a concrete Stage 2 hardware protocol are all present. The Stage 2 protocol has exchangeable links and pads, O/R/M/U along a Delta_contact ladder, numerical predictions (21/72/124%) and a pre-stated Page trend test. That is the kind of falsifiable plan Stage 1 asks for. My concern with Stage 2 is measurement. Delta_contact steps of 0.9 / 2.3 / 3.9 mm must be resolved with markers on a servo finger that has link backlash, servo deadband and pad compression of 0.5-2.3 mm. Friction from textured covers also varies with normal load. Since R and U differ in contact force by about 4x (0.75x vs 3.3x O), friction-cover results may confound load-dependent friction with Delta_contact.

Presentation. The paper is dense but logically ordered (pole, then clearance, then motion, then Studies 1-3). The math is economical and correct as far as I checked (Lemma 1, Eqs. 2, 6, 8). Problems: Fig. 2 is too small to read in print. The Table I caption carries a paragraph of methods. "Contact Jacobian" is used for a single kinematic column in the proximal frame, which may confuse readers who expect the grasp-map sense. Several results are stated in compressed clauses that need two readings (e.g., the partial-correlation sentence, the "Other protocols" paragraph).

Score basis. C1 is solid and useful, though somewhat expected once the servo is moved off the joint, and it is under-qualified on mechanical advantage. C2 is a genuinely useful methodological point for anyone running interface comparisons on servo hands: redesign by face path, not by skeleton. It is backed by careful, partly pre-registered statistics, but only in one contact model and one protocol class. For a Stage 1 abstract the hypotheses, methods and falsification plan are complete. I lean accept at 6.
