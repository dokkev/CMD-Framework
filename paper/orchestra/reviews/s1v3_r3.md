ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The hypotheses as I read them:
- H1 (central): A finger is redesigned for a thicker contact interface, and the original hand's command (an impedance setpoint) is reused. The error the redesign adds to a measured interface effect, e_H = (D_H − D_O)/|D_O|, grows with the change in how the contact surfaces move (Δ^prox_distal, the distal contact point seen from the proximal pad). It does not grow with the change in the skeleton (Δ_skel, the fingertip path in the base frame).
- H2: This is not only a preload/contact-force artifact. Δ^prox_distal still predicts the error once the contact force is matched.
- H3: A policy learned on O keeps the most performance on the redesign that preserves the motion its task uses.
- Supporting design claims (C1/C2): a finite-displacement pole analysis (Eqs. 1–4) shows a fixed axis cannot keep contact motion as the interface deepens. A crossed four-bar (CFL) at the PIP can place its pole near the contact faces, so it gives more coverage and keeps more contact motion than at-joint, remote and RCJ joints.

Evidence and plan for each:
- H1: This is well supported in simulation. In Isaac Lab (MuJoCo-Warp, point contact): four tests at p<0.001 (Holm), partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δ_skel held fixed, and ρ = 0.94 over 27 grid redesigns. In Drake: four single-finger tasks under point and hydroelastic contact. The Skeleton-vs-CoDe contrast is clean: Δ_skel 0.3 mm and Δ^prox 6.6 mm against 4.8 mm and 1.1 mm. The sign-reversal anecdote (−41% on O vs +48% on Coverage) is compelling. Weak spots:
  - ρ falls to 0.43–0.53 in the dial and pivot tasks.
  - Section IV-B2 shows the predictive frame depends on what holds the object (Δ^base_distal when the object is palm-fixed). The principle is therefore really "keep the contact point's motion in the frame of whatever holds the object". It is not specifically "contact surface in the proximal frame".
  - There is no interface deformation. That is the defining property of the thick compliant/tactile pads that motivate the paper.
- H2: One partial correlation (ρ = 0.80 under an equal reused torque) and one re-referencing experiment (42 → 14%). This is thin but reasonable. "Preload" and the force-matching protocol are not defined well enough to judge.
- H3: The weakest hypothesis. Three PPO tasks with two seeds each. In one of three tasks (relay dial) the result goes the other way, which the authors handle by declaring fingertip-end contact "outside our claim". For cube rotation the predictive variable is a third metric (proximal pad position against the palm) that is not in Eq. 6, over 14 redesigns. As written, "the motion its task uses" is chosen per task after the fact. That makes H3 close to unfalsifiable unless the task-relevant motion is fixed a priori.
- Design claims: The coverage gains are modest (e.g., 73% vs 70% for remote at 120°/7 mm). The derivation of Eq. 3 assumes the same initial contact point and a pure translation of F_prox by Δt. CoDe's own coverage cost relative to the Coverage optimum is never reported, although it is the key trade-off of the principle.
- Hardware plan: Only one sentence long. It does not say how the ground-truth effect D_O will be obtained on hardware. In simulation, O "carries the new interface's contact stiffness on its own geometry", which is a counterfactual a real 7 mm pad cannot reproduce on O. A friction-only change (coating) seems to be the only clean hardware analogue, and this needs to be stated.

What my scores rest on:
- Value 3: The question "which kinematic property must be kept when the interface changes" is relevant to interface studies and to policy reuse across hand revisions. The pole framing is elegant. It is not 4 because:
  - The principle partially overlaps RCM fingers for thick skin [14,16].
  - The conclusion is somewhat expected once one notes that a joint-impedance command acts on the object through the contact surfaces.
  - The scope is narrow (planar, a single PIP, rigid pads).
- Feasibility 2: H1 is credibly supported in simulation. However, the main confound (rigid pads vs. deforming interfaces) is untested, the hardware protocol is unspecified, and H3 is post hoc with mixed results. Against the strongest competing submissions with hardware evidence, the current evidence is simulation-only for a hardware-design paper.
