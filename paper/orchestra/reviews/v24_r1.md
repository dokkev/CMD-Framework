ASSESSMENT NOTES:

I read the paper as someone who has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them. The problem is real. On a LEAP- or ROBEL-style finger the PIP servo body takes the space a proximal pad needs, and every pad swap becomes a re-print that changes where the faces sit. In my experience the second problem is the one that quietly breaks pad comparisons. The paper names it precisely, and that is its most useful contribution.

Central claims and evidence:

1. C1 / H1 (room for thick pads). Remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP gives more cylinder coverage C̄pad than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) in all 10 feasible (thickness, q_req) conditions, by 2.4–14.6 points (Fig. 3).
   - Search fairness is addressed reasonably well. CFL wins without the extra refinement, and its worst of six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10 conditions.
   - The 2R-remote ablation is the most informative part. A belt-driven fixed axis already matches or beats CFL at 90° and at thin pads. The polycentric joint pays off only as flexion deepens. Eq. (2) explains why: the gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm.
   - The result is credible but expected. Taking the servo body off the joint is bound to free room. The real price of the CFL is a worst-case mechanical advantage of 0.41–0.58 against 1 for direct drive, roughly halving available PIP torque. That price sits outside the comparison ("left free"). For a servo hand with a fixed actuator budget, this is a first-order design variable, not a footnote.
   - C1 is purely geometric (an inscribed-cylinder coverage proxy). Its Stage-2 falsification test only checks that pads can be swapped without disassembly. Coverage itself is never measured physically.

2. C2 theory (Sec. III-C). The PIP column of the contact Jacobian is set by the pole path relative to the face. The theory has three parts:
   - Eq. (6), ‖Δc‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖.
   - Eq. (7), a lower bound showing that a thicker pad cannot keep both the contact-surface motion and the skeleton motion.
   - Corollary 1, a fixed-axis bound.

   All three are elementary but correct planar kinematics, and I checked Lemma 1 and Eq. (6). Their value lies in the framing: keep the contact-surface path, not the skeleton. Study 2 (Fig. 4) shows the CFL reaching 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound while staying within 0.2 points of the best coverage. A fixed axis allowed to slide its contact point stays at 0.59–0.79 of the bound, which is a good control.

3. C2 / H3 (an interface can be studied on its own). Under a reused impedance setpoint, the redesign-induced error in measured friction and stiffness effects tracks Δcontact (ρ = 0.80–0.86). Δcontact is the only candidate whose bootstrap interval excludes zero, and it survives Holm correction. Holding Δskel fixed does not remove it. A Δskel = 0 revolute errs by 95–228%.
   - The statistics are careful for a Stage-1 paper: pre-registration is marked, within-thickness resampling is reported, partial correlations hold setpoint force fixed, and Fig. 5 sweeps robustness over stiffness, friction and substeps.
   - The headline example (U reports +48% where the truth is −41%) shows convincingly why this matters to people running pad studies.

My main concerns:

(a) The result is largely tied to the protocol, and the protocol is close to the mechanism. With an over-closure of q0+5° at stiffness K0, contact force is set mostly by how far the face penetrates the object. That penetration is roughly what Δcontact measures, so a strong correlation is close to built in. The partial correlation of 0.64 helps but does not remove this. Under the torque and touch protocols, Δcontact weakens (0.69, 0.44) and the skeleton takes over (0.71, 0.88).

The authors argue that servo hands run position-at-stiffness and stay under the current limit (≤25% of stall). In practice, though, many LEAP/Dynamixel users close past the object exactly so that the current limit caps the grip. In that regime the hand behaves like the torque protocol, and C2's predictor is weaker. The paper should state more plainly that C2 holds for unsaturated position-impedance grasps. It should also explain why that regime is representative.

(b) The contact model is too thin for a claim about pads. Pads are meshes in point contact with a normal stiffness. A "7 mm soft pad" therefore differs from a 3 mm pad only by geometry and a contact-stiffness number. There is no area growth, no conformation and no shear compliance, and those are exactly what a pad comparison measures.

This creates two problems:
- The "true" interface effect is defined on O's 3 mm geometry with the soft pad's stiffness. That separation of thickness from material exists only in this model.
- The abstract's "holds 41% less" carries no caveat, although the body correctly says "in this contact model".

The Stage-2 bench test (planar slide, markers) is the right next step. Even so, the sim-to-real gap for thick compliant pads is the largest open risk.

(c) The spacer baseline is dismissed in one sentence. Shims, or one finger sized for the thickest pad with thinner pads raised to the same face, give Δcontact = 0 exactly. They cost 8.6–18.2 coverage points, and they need the largest interface known in advance. For an experimentalist, that is usually a cheaper and more exact way to isolate an interface than swapping CFL links. Stage 2 also swaps links. The paper needs to argue why a near-optimal-coverage reference matters for an interface study, or report spacers as a fair comparison arm in Study 3.

(d) The tolerance is generous. "Independent up to about 7 mm" comes with 21–25% error at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. Fig. 1d at 9 mm also seems to show R well short of the true change. For pad effects of a few tens of percent, a 20% error is not "independent". The 20% threshold also needs a justification.

(e) Clarity. The prose is very compressed. Many coined terms (pole, link line, face, mount depth, S1/S2, O/R/U/M, grid/touch/torque protocols) arrive in quick succession. The Table I caption carries most of the statistical method, and Figs. 2–4 have very small fonts. A reader from the hand-design community will need two passes. The methodology is complete, but it is hard to extract.

Stage-1 fit: the problem, hypotheses (H1–H3 with explicit failure conditions), methods, preliminary simulation evidence and a falsifiable Stage-2 protocol (Page trend test, 43/48 ordered blocks predicted) are all present. The required negative-result insight is also there: the reversed-share prediction failed, and the paper reports it. Related work is focused, as the guidelines ask.

Overall, this is a sound and useful Stage-1 submission. The concept is original (contact-surface motion as the invariant for interface studies), and the simulation work is careful. It is held back by a single point-contact model, the dependence on the protocol, an under-argued spacer alternative and dense writing. I lean accept at 6.
