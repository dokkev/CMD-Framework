ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology; I check whether each experiment tests what it claims and whether the statistics hold up.

Central claims and evidence:

(1) Room. A CFL PIP joint with remote actuation keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint (2R-DD) finger at every pad thickness and flexion. The evidence is an optimization over 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions with a GA/NSGA-II and three seeds (Fig. 3a-c). The comparison is fair (one requirement set, each joint at its own best design) and the falsification criterion is stated. Support is adequate, with three caveats. (i) "Every" means the 10 conditions where 2R-DD is feasible. At 150 deg, 2R-DD has no design past 5 mm. The abstract does not say this. (ii) 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90 deg and in several 120 deg cells (Fig. 3a,b), so the advantage over 2R-remote appears only as flexion deepens. The text says this, but the abstract's framing ("remote actuation and a crossed four-bar") blurs which factor does what. (iii) Coverage is a purely geometric, inscribed-cylinder metric. It says nothing about pressure distribution or the effective contact area of a compliant pad under load. That is acceptable for a room argument, but the label "contact surface" claims more than the metric measures. The spread across the three seeds is not reported, so I cannot tell whether a 2.4 pp margin exceeds optimizer variance.

(2) Kinematics. A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion when the face deepens, and the paper gives a closed-form lower bound (Eq. 8-9). The CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. The planar kinematics (pole, Eq. 7) are classical and correct as far as I can check. The bound depends on modelling assumptions: the pin cannot sit below the pad mount, and the roller radius is fixed. RCJ already breaks the second one in the optimization, which the paper notes. This part is sound.

(3) Experimental claim. Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact and not Delta_skel. This is where my concerns are.

(a) The paper's own mechanism makes the result close to an identity. Sec. IV-B1 says E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1| to first order. The limit load in a Coulomb pull-out scales with preload. Under a stiffness setpoint, preload is K0 times the angular interference, which depends on where the face sits at q0. Delta_contact includes the start offset and the face position along the path, so it is close to a proxy for the preload change. The experiment therefore mainly shows that a stiffness-controlled grasp with a different face position presses differently, and that Coulomb limit loads scale with normal force. That is true and useful to state, but it is a much smaller claim than "the contact Jacobian is what matters." Table I is missing the obvious candidates: face displacement at q0 (or the interference at the setpoint) and the measured preload ratio itself. If either matches or beats Delta_contact, the full-path contact Jacobian is not the operative quantity.

(b) Protocol dependence weakens the general message. When each finger is re-zeroed at touch, which the paper calls what "practice does," the skeleton predicts the error instead (Table I: Delta_skel 0.71/0.88 vs Delta_contact 0.69/0.44). Matching contact force leaves only 3-7%. So "keep the contact Jacobian" holds for one control protocol, and the paper itself says a common practical protocol removes most of the effect. The conclusion states this, but the abstract and contributions read as protocol-independent.

(c) Statistics. Per setting n = 14-16 hands, and the authors admit the lead over Delta_skel is not significant there. Only the 45-hand grid, at one thickness (7 mm, with 27 added redesigns), separates the two. The redesigns were chosen to "reach a set Delta_contact while keeping or changing the skeleton most." That is a designed sample, not a random one, so a Spearman correlation computed on it depends on the sampling design. Hands are also not independent, because they share thickness groups and Delta_contact covaries with thickness. Bootstrap over hands and Holm correction are good practice. Still, a mixed model or a within-thickness analysis would show whether Delta_contact predicts beyond the thickness grouping. The "Pad thickness" row (rho 0.11-0.20) partly answers this but does not stratify. The partial correlation at the setpoint with force deviation held is post hoc. "Pre-registered: fixed in a dated log before the runs" cannot be verified by a reviewer; a timestamped public registration (e.g., OSF) would make it count.

(d) Simulation fidelity for the interface effects themselves. In Isaac Lab, pad "stiffness" 2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m under point contact is a penalty or solver parameter, not a pad. A 9 mm compliant pad conforming around a 15-20 mm cylinder has distributed contact, a changing contact patch, and rolling of the contact point. Point contact represents none of these. The Drake hydroelastic check is the right step, and with pads "as soft as they are thick" the error grows sharply (R: 104% at 9 mm). This suggests that the 20% figure from point contact is optimistic exactly where compliance matters. The 0.1 MPa hydroelastic modulus is reasonable for silicone, but the paper does not say whether the friction model (regularized Coulomb in SAP), the stiction tolerance, or the time step was checked for convergence. No noise floor for E is reported: the run-to-run or perturbation variance of Delta(O) is missing. Without it, errors such as 3-6% cannot be read.

(e) The headline reversal ("U reports +48% when it holds 41% less") compares against U. U is the coverage-only design that presses 3.3x as hard. Nobody running a careful interface study would reuse a setpoint on such a hand without at least re-zeroing. The example illustrates the risk well, but it is the extreme case, not a typical one.

(f) The E metric divides by |Delta(O)|. For small reference effects it explodes, and errors of 100-370% may partly reflect a small denominator. Please report the absolute Delta values alongside E.

Presentation. The writing is very compressed. Many terms are introduced inline and used once later: "O's placements," "the grid," R, U and M, the "mounting rule," and the S1/S2 conditions. Reading Sec. IV needs several passes. In Fig. 4b, the panel title and the 5/7/9 mm labels overlap data points, and three marker families plus three blue shades are hard to tell apart. Fig. 2c's 3D surface is hard to read quantitatively; a 2D contour or line plot would be clearer. Fig. 3 is clear. The planned hardware test is specific and falsifiable (Page trend test, load-cell preload, backlash measured against R's 0.9 mm), which I appreciate.

What my scores rest on: the design and kinematic results are sound and modestly novel. The experimental part is carefully run (two simulators, three tasks, Holm, bootstrap, stated refutation criteria). Its central causal claim, however, reduces largely to preload sensitivity under stiffness control, it holds for one protocol only, and it is not separated from simpler predictors at the per-setting sample sizes. For a Stage-1 extended abstract with a concrete hardware plan, this is acceptable but not strong.
