THOUGHT:

I read this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methods reviewer. The paper makes two claims. C1 is kinematic and geometric: a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger. C2 is the more interesting one. When a servo hand reuses its impedance setpoint (a target flexion at stiffness K0) across redesigns, the error in a measured interface effect follows the change in contact-surface motion, Delta_contact. Neither the skeleton change nor any of the other candidates explains that error. C1 rests on closed-form propositions (pole, clearance, coverage, fixed-axis bound) and on optimization in Studies 1 and 2. I have few concerns there: the theory is clean, the bound in Eq. (8) is checked against optimized fronts, and the ablation through 2R-remote separates remote actuation from the polycentric joint. My review therefore concentrates on Study 3, where the contact simulation and the statistics carry the claim.

What is done well in Study 3:
- Each hypothesis has an explicit failure criterion.
- A hand-independent 27-design grid was pre-registered, and on it rho = 0.94 [0.84, 0.97].
- Bootstrap intervals are taken over hands, which is the right unit. Permutation tests with Holm correction are used, and post-hoc analyses are labelled as such.
- Other protocols (ideal torque, close-to-touch) are reported honestly even where Delta_contact loses: under touch, the skeleton wins.
- The scope is narrowed to "the setpoints servo hands run". This is the right framing, and it matches Table I.

My main concerns:

1. The mechanism may make the correlation close to tautological. The paper itself says that at a reused position setpoint, a face sitting in a different place over- or under-closes, so the hand presses harder or softer: R presses 0.75x as hard and U 3.3x. For a friction change from mu 1.3 to 2.0 under an elliptic cone, the holding load scales roughly as mu*N. So E for the friction intervention is essentially |N_H - N_O|/N_O, the relative change in normal force at the setpoint. For a stiffness intervention, the same over-closure enters through the spring. If so, Study 3 mainly shows that Delta_contact, taken at q <= q_req, predicts the normal-force change at q0. Two things follow. First, the relevant geometric quantity is the face's position near the setpoint relative to O's. Second, "the interface can be studied on its own" reduces to "the grasp force is preserved". The partial correlation with the normal offset held fixed (0.69 on the grid) and the post-hoc rho = -0.99 between error direction and signed normal offset push me further toward this reading. I would like to see the following, per hand:
   - the contact normal force at the setpoint before the load ramp;
   - the correlation of E with |N_H/N_O - 1|;
   - whether Delta_contact explains anything beyond that.
   If it does not, the claim should be framed as a geometric predictor of preload change. That is still useful, but it is a different claim.

2. Contact model fidelity. Isaac Lab 3 / MuJoCo-Warp contact is a penetration-based soft constraint on rigid geometry. As I understand the paper, a "7 mm compliant pad" is rigid geometry plus an equivalent contact stiffness (2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m). Patch growth, pressure distribution, thickness-dependent stiffness and the nonlinear, hysteretic response of real elastomers are not modelled, yet those are exactly what make thick soft pads interesting. Fig. 1d's headline (the soft thick pad "holds 48% more when it holds 41% less") depends on how the solver maps pad stiffness to normal force under over-closure. The 2N/s ramp, the 10 mm/s slip threshold and the solver parameters (solref/solimp or their Warp equivalents, timestep, cone, impratio) all affect hold load and should be stated or swept. One contact model with no sensitivity analysis is thin support for a sign-reversal claim. The 100-fold K0 sweep helps but does not replace a contact-parameter sweep.

3. Sample construction and selection.
   - Most hands in the reused-setpoint columns are optimized designs chosen to span Delta_contact (R, U, and redesigns at set Delta_contact). A sample chosen to span the predictor inflates rank correlation relative to a population of plausible redesigns. The pre-registered grid mitigates this, which is good, and it should be the headline number.
   - Hands that cannot hold the cylinder are excluded. Counting them as the largest errors lowers rho to 0.81 and 0.67, and this belongs in the main result rather than only in a figure caption.
   - E is a median over placements and directions within a hand. Please report the within-hand spread too, since placements share geometry and are not independent.
   - E = |Delta(H) - Delta(O)|/|Delta(O)| is unstable when Delta(O) is small (errors of 95–228% appear). An absolute error is mentioned in the Fig. 5 caption (rho 0.82–0.89) and should appear in the text.

4. The "design tolerance" of about 1 mm for E < 20% comes from fits whose threshold ranges from 0.35 to 0.96 mm across settings, a factor of nearly 3. It is specific to one cylinder, one K0 range, one contact model and one task. It should be stated as such and not as a general design rule.

5. The task is narrow: a planar two-DoF finger with the MCP held, a single cylinder between proximal and distal pads, and a quasi-static pull-out. The C2 statement in the abstract and introduction reads more generally than this evidence supports. The paper does acknowledge that fingertip-only contact is excluded.

6. Stage 2 protocol. It is good that this is fixed in advance with simulation predictions (21, 72, 124%) and a failure criterion. However, with three hands (R, M, U), a one-sided Page trend test is a test on three ordered conditions. The placements, directions and repeats are within-hand replicates, so the effective n for "error rises with Delta_contact" is 3. The authors should say what the replication unit is and do a power or sensitivity check given real-pad hysteresis and repeat-to-repeat variability. They should also say how the "true" reference will be obtained physically: O cannot carry the 7 mm pad, which is the point of C1, so the "true" change in Fig. 1d is itself a simulated counterfactual. This deserves a sentence.

Minor points:
- Delta_contact is a max over q <= q_req, but the error arises at a specific setpoint q0 in 85–115°. Would a Delta_contact evaluated at q0 predict better? Pointwise versus worst-case matters for practical use.
- Delta_contact uses a single point c(q) below the fingertip. For the cylinder, the contact point moves along the face. Please clarify how much the choice of c matters.
- Table I omits the signed normal offset as a candidate column, although the text uses it; including it, marked post hoc, would make the comparison transparent.
- Presentation is very dense. The abstract alone holds about six numbers and four concepts. Terms such as "keeps the contact Jacobian" versus "contact-surface motion" versus Delta_contact are used interchangeably. Several key quantities, such as E's definition and K0, appear only deep in Sec. V.

Overall: for a Stage 1 extended abstract, the problem, hypotheses with falsification criteria and methodology are fully laid out. C1 is well supported. C2 is supported in simulation within a scope the authors mostly state correctly. My central methodological concern is that the C2 effect may be explained largely by setpoint preload change, and that it rests on a single contact model. I would accept, conditional on the authors addressing the normal-force analysis and contact-parameter sensitivity before Stage 2.
