THOUGHT:

Reviewer background: mechanism design and planar kinematics. I read the text closely and checked every closed-form result by hand.

**What the paper is.** The paper argues that a robot finger redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface should keep the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal contact face, not the motion of the skeleton. In the plane, the finite pole of the distal phalanx sets that relative motion. The paper derives four results:
- a clearance law (Prop. 1);
- a coverage law (Prop. 2);
- a pole-shift law for the contact-surface motion, with a "sum rule" (Prop. 3, Eq. 7);
- a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1).

It then shows on four joint types that a crossed four-bar (CFL) changes the contact-surface motion least under re-optimization. A simulation study finds that, when a flexion command is reused, Δcontact predicts the error in a measured interface effect.

**Checking the mathematics (my main job).**
- Lemma 1. (I − R_q)^{-1} = ½[I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}] is correct. det(I − R_q) = 2(1 − cos q), and sin q/(1 − cos q) = cot(q/2). The finite (displacement) pole is classical (Bottema–Roth), which the paper acknowledges. Using the finite pole relative to q = 0, not the instantaneous centre, is the right choice for both clearance and Δc. That is a nice, correct observation.
- Prop. 1. The proof idea is correct and elegant. Write rotation about P as two reflections: the vertical line through P, then the line at q/2. If the distal pad at q = 0 is the mirror of the proximal pad about the vertical through P, the rotated distal pad is the mirror of the proximal pad about the bisector line. A convex set meets its mirror image iff it meets the mirror line, because points on the line are fixed. I checked the binding corner. For z < t and for z > t, the face-side joint corner (depth t) is closer to the line than the back corner (depth d), so "joint-side corner crosses first" holds. G = 2(t − z) tan(q/2) per side pair is correct. Fig. 2's numbers are consistent: 5 tan 60° = 8.66, 9 tan 60° = 15.59, 4 tan 60° = 6.93; pads 32 → 25.1 → 33.7. The [·]^+ clamp agrees with G ≥ 0 at q → 0.

  However, the hypotheses are stronger than stated:
  - (i) An exact mirror needs equal pad cross-sections near the joint (equal d and h, not just equal face depth t, which is all S1 states).
  - (ii) S2 must hold at every q, because the mirror is about the vertical through P(q). For a CFL, whose finite pole moves in x as well as in depth, S2 then forces the pole's x coordinate to stay at the gap midpoint for all q. That is generally false. So Eq. 4 is exact for a fixed axis and an RCJ, but only approximate for the CFL. The paper validates it with a *median* error of 0.05 mm. The maximum error, and a general asymmetric condition, are missing.
- Prop. 2. I re-derived the distance between the phalanx ends. The vectors from P are (∓G/2, z), and the mirror-line normal is (cos q/2, −sin q/2). The distance is 2|w·n| = G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). The denominator is therefore correct. The remark "the best joint turns about a point on the contact face" holds for constant z when Prop. 1 binds at q_req: lowering z trades 2 tan(q/2) in the numerator against 2 sin(q/2) in the denominator, and gains nothing once G is clamped at 0. With z(q) varying and G set by a max over q, it is a heuristic. It should be labelled as such.
- Prop. 3. c = R c0 + (I − R)p, so Δc = (I − R_q)Δp and ‖Δc‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖. This is correct, and it holds for every distal-face point, not just c, which justifies "one point sets it". The sum rule is also correct. The link frame and the face frame differ by a translation of the proximal face depth, so Δp − ΔP = (0, Δt₁), and the triangle inequality gives Eq. (7). But:
  - (a) Δt must be defined as the change in proximal face depth relative to the link line, i.e. Δh − Δd.
  - (b) "A thicker pad cannot keep both" is false as stated. A recessed pad (Δd = Δh) has Δt = 0 and can keep both. The corollary itself admits this. The sentence should be conditioned.
- Cor. 1. The algebra is fine, but the phrase "wherever the new axis is placed" clashes with the hypothesis "faces move Δh − Δd away from the axis". If the axis could be placed anywhere, moving it palmar by Δh − Δd would give Δp = 0. The real content is a *packaging* constraint: the pin must lie inside the phalanx, above the pad mount. That constraint is never stated. Once it is, the corollary follows from Prop. 3 in one line.
  - The same logic shows that a four-bar translated rigidly by Δt has Δcontact = 0. The interesting design question is therefore which constraint blocks that translation, and why the CFL escapes it. Its virtual pole is the crossing point of the links and can lie palmar of all physical pivots. This is the actual mechanism insight, and the paper only implies it.
- Rolling joint. z = r tan(q/4) is correct. I derived it from two equal circles rolling without slip: the pole stays at x = 0 at depth r tan(q/4). The caveat about the placement of the rolling line applies here too.
- Numbers I checked: the bound 2 sin 60°·6 = 10.39 matches 2R-remote at 9 mm and 120° in Fig. 4b. 0.35/3.46 = 10% and 4.31/8.49 = 51% match. Fig. 1: 3.4 + 3.9 ≥ 6.93 and 7.7 + 0.9 ≥ 6.93 satisfy Eq. 7. 17.4/4.5 = 3.9×.

**Concerns with the empirical argument.**
1. *The Study 3 correlation is close to tautological.* The flexion command is reused through each hand's transmission table, and stiffness is imposed in q-space (K0 = 5 N m/rad). Under that protocol, the face poses at the commanded q differ from O's by exactly (I − R)Δp. The object touches only the faces. So the over-closure and contact locations at the commanded pose are, to first order, functions of Δcontact. Several of the four rival "candidates" are set up to fail:
   - The skeleton never touches the object.
   - Transmission is modelled away twice: by the table and by specifying stiffness in q rather than in servo angle (K_q = K_θ (dθ/dq)²).
   - Thickness has no mechanical effect if the pads are a contact model on rigid geometry.

   The torque-reuse result (ρ = 0.75) is more informative and deserves more weight.
2. *The error metric is only a proxy for the stated problem.* The stated problem is comparing interface A in hand O with interface B in redesigned hand R. The experiment instead measures how a *third* variable (μ, contact stiffness, crown) changes the limit load on each hand, and compares that with O. In simulation, thickness has no mechanical consequence, so O's effect is a valid ground truth. On hardware, the effect of friction on a 9 mm compliant pad legitimately differs from that on a 3 mm pad, so E conflates kinematic distortion with real mechanics.
3. *The practical payoff is weak relative to protocol alternatives.* R still errs 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm. In Fig. 1d, a 9 mm hand with Δcontact ≈ 3.5 mm errs about as little (≈40%) as R. Reusing O's joint torque brings *every* hand, U included, to 3–40%, and force matching gives 3–7%. The data therefore say the protocol matters at least as much as the redesign. The paper should discuss this trade-off instead of dismissing it in one sentence. Joint-torque reuse does not need interface-side sensing.
4. *Overclaiming "keeps".* The CFL reduces Δcontact to 0.35–4.31 mm, or 10–51% of a bound derived for a *different* joint class. It does not keep it. Δcontact is never driven to zero, and the 90°/9 mm case is half the bound.
5. *C2 switches metric against the fairest baseline.* 2R-remote (also remote-actuated) beats the CFL on C̄pad at 90° and at 120° for 3–5 mm. The CFL's advantage is then argued on a different metric (pad length / L_f). The coverage objective rewards a contour that shortens when flexed (Prop. 2), which is itself a modelling choice that affects the ranking.
6. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, which matters for a remotely driven CFL with a moving pole. "2R-DD: none found beyond 5 mm" at 150° does not say whether the optimizer failed or the design is infeasible.
7. Fig. 4d shows Δc(0) ≠ 0 for 2R-DD and RCJ (≈9.3 mm for RCJ), so Prop. 3's precondition (same c0) fails for those designs. The RCJ's Δcontact is dominated by an offset at extension. It is unclear why the point below the fingertip moves along the face when L_f is fixed. This needs explanation, because it drives the "least of four joint types" comparison.
8. Study 3 contains only CFL redesigns, 14–16 hands per test, Spearman with bootstrap CIs over 5 candidates × 3 conditions, and a hand set chosen by the authors to decorrelate Δcontact from Δskel. That is good for identification, but the CIs are wide, and "interval contains zero" is not evidence of no effect (thickness ρ = 0.50 under torque reuse).

**Presentation.** The text is dense but mostly precise. Terminology: "contact Jacobian" is really the body Jacobian of the distal-face frame relative to the proximal-face frame. A contact Jacobian normally depends on contact locations and maps into contact frames. Some symbols are under-defined (x0, r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, T_i). "3.9×" (Fig. 1, one placement) versus "3.3×" (median) can confuse. The title is long. C5 is listed as a contribution but is future work.

**Overall judgement.** For a Stage-1 RSS extended abstract, the problem, hypothesis and methodology are fully laid out. The kinematic analysis is correct and cleanly presented. The simulation is carefully controlled, with pre-fixed gates and robustness checks. The Stage-2 plan is concrete, and its negative outcome is informative. The weaknesses are conceptual sharpness and significance: the theory is elementary planar kinematics under hidden assumptions, and the main empirical correlation is largely built in. I lean accept, provided the assumptions are stated honestly and the protocol trade-off is discussed.
