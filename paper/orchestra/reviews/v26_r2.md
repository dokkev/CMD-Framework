ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Reviewer perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology (does each experiment test what it claims, and are the statistics adequate).

Central claims and evidence.

(C1 / H1) A remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. Evidence: Study 1, GA + NSGA-II over all design parameters, 3 seeds, 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions; CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both have a design (by 2.4-14.6 points), and the worst of six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10. The clearance law (Eq. 2) explains the trend mechanistically (fixed-axis gap grows 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of face depth; the CFL pole follows the face). Idealized upper bounds (CFL ideal, RCJ ideal) bracket the result. This claim is well supported within the stated model and the ablation (2R-remote) cleanly separates "remote actuation" from "polycentric joint". It is a geometric claim, so the simulation is the right instrument; the caveat that mechanical advantage is left free (0.41-0.58 vs 1) is stated honestly.

(C2 / H2) A redesign can keep the PIP column of the contact Jacobian (Delta_contact) far better than a fixed axis can. Evidence: Proposition 3 and Corollary 1 give a closed-form lower bound for a fixed axis (2 sin(q_req/2)|Delta h - Delta d|); Study 2 shows the CFL reaches 10-51% of it at near-best coverage, and stays below even a fixed axis free to slide along the face (0.59-0.79 of the bound). The kinematics are correct and elementary (planar rotation about a pole); the bound is tight for the cases where c0 is kept, and the paper is careful to say 2R-DD and RCJ can fall below it only because their contact point moved. Supported.

(C2 / H3) Under the impedance setpoint a servo hand runs, the error that a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact, not the skeleton motion, coverage, transmission or pad thickness. This is the claim my perspective cares most about. Evidence: Study 3, Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp, 15/14/45 hands for friction, stiffness and grid settings, cylinder pull-out in 4 directions over many placements; Spearman rho 0.80-0.86 for Delta_contact, hand-level bootstrap, one-sided permutation tests with Holm correction, a pre-registered grid of 27 new hands, and partial analyses (Delta_skel held: 0.86-0.91; setpoint force held: 0.64; face normal offset held: 0.69). The statistical reporting is better than typical for a robotics abstract: the unit of analysis is the hand, pre-registered vs post hoc tests are labelled, and the negative case (keeping the skeleton exactly, Delta_skel = 0, errs 95-228%) is a strong discriminating test. The "other protocols" columns of Table I are an honest scoping: under reused torque both Delta_contact and Delta_skel predict, and under touch-closure the skeleton predicts better (0.88 vs 0.44). So C2 is explicitly a claim about the impedance-setpoint protocol, which the paper argues is what servo hands actually run.

Main concerns my scores rest on.

1. Contact model fidelity relative to the effect sizes. Pads are rigid meshes in point contact with the cylinder; the interface's "stiffness" is the solver's normal contact stiffness; friction is a point Coulomb model. Real thick compliant pads have a distributed patch, torsional/patch friction, viscoelasticity and a contact location that migrates under load. The paper's own design tolerance (E ~ 20% at Delta_contact 0.35-0.96 mm) is of the same order as the simulated pad compression at the grasps (0.5-2.3 mm). Delta_contact is defined on the unloaded faces; under load the effective contact point and normal move by a comparable amount, and that motion depends on the very stiffness being studied. Whether Delta_contact evaluated at the loaded configuration predicts better, or whether the ranking survives a patch/compliant-layer contact model, is the key unknown before hardware. The authors acknowledge "one contact model", and the Stage 2 hardware protocol is the right remedy, but a second simulator contact formulation (e.g. a soft-layer or elastic-foundation pad) would be cheap and would strengthen H3 considerably.

2. Possible inflation of rho by design of the candidate set. The 18 redesigns were chosen to "keep or change the skeleton most" at set Delta_contact, i.e. at extremes. Sampling at extremes of a predictor inflates rank correlations relative to the population of plausible redesigns. The within-thickness intervals ([0.41, 0.99], [0.35, 0.98]) are wide, consistent with only ~5 hands per thickness, and much of the pooled rho could come from between-thickness spread (Delta_contact grows with thickness and so does error). The grid of 27 random-ish 7 mm hands (rho 0.86, pre-registered) partially answers this, which is good; it should be stated how the grid was sampled.

3. The error metric. E(H) = |Delta(H) - Delta(O)| / |Delta(O)|, median over placements and directions "where both hands hold". (a) The ratio is unstable where Delta(O) is small; the paper should say whether such placements are excluded or how often they occur. (b) Conditioning on both hands holding is a survivorship filter: hands that change the contact surface most may also lose more grasps, which removes their worst errors from the median. The fraction of excluded placements per hand, and a sensitivity analysis that scores a lost grasp as a large error (the caption says this was done: rho 0.81/0.67) belongs in the main text, not only the figure caption.

4. The headline reversal example. The abstract says the soft thick pad "holds 41% less" in truth while U reports 48% more; the body says the true change on O is -37% on the grid and "41% less" in the U paragraph; Fig. 1d shows roughly -40% at 7 mm. These are probably different subsets (grid vs. the U placements), but the paper should make the numbers trace to one definition. Also, the "true" effect is O with a 3 mm geometry and a 7 mm pad's stiffness, a counterfactual only available in simulation; that is fine as a ground truth for the methodology, but the Stage 2 hardware protocol can only test friction (bare vs textured covers), not stiffness. The paper should say plainly that the stiffness half of H3 will remain simulation-only.

5. Stage 2 protocol. Clear falsification criteria (C1 fails if exchange requires disassembling the articulation; C2 fails if a one-sided Page trend test finds no rise of error with Delta_contact). The three hands R, M, U have Delta_contact rising (0.9, 2.3, 3.9 mm) while Delta_skel falls (7.7, 4.6, 3.4 mm), so the ordering discriminates between the two candidates, which is good design. However, three hands give a single ordinal trend; with 5 placements x 4 directions x 5 repeats the within-hand noise is well sampled but the between-hand degrees of freedom are minimal, and any unmodelled hardware difference between the three printed fingers (backlash, print tolerance) is confounded with Delta_contact. Measuring Delta_contact with markers (rather than using the design value) is the right call. The simulated power (43 of 48 blocks ordered) is reported, which is appreciated.

6. Clarity. The paper is extremely compressed. Table I's rows ("Delta_contact, Delta_skel held") and its caption are hard to parse; Fig. 5's caption carries results that belong in the text; several sentences in Sec. V ("A fixed axis free to slide its contact point along the face still stays above 0.59-0.79 of the bound") need a second reading. The theory (Sec. III) is clean.

Overall: a well-reasoned, honestly scoped Stage 1 submission. The hypotheses, methodology and falsification criteria are fully specified as Stage 1 requires, the kinematic results are sound, and the statistical treatment of H3 is careful. The main risk is that H3's evidence rests on a single point-contact model at a scale where pad compression is comparable to the tolerance being claimed; that is a limitation for Stage 2 to resolve rather than a flaw that invalidates the Stage 1 claims. I lean accept.
