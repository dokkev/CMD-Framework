ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and the evidence for each.

(1) Room. Claim: remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar PIP joint (CFL) keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger at every pad thickness and required flexion. Evidence: a pole-based clearance law (Prop. 1) and coverage law (Prop. 2), and an optimization study over 4 joints x 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions (Fig. 3a-c). I checked the algebra that I could: the fixed-axis gap 2 t tan(q/2) (3.46 mm/mm at 120 deg) is right, and in Eq. (3) lowering the pole by delta does add 2 delta tan(q/2) to both numerator and denominator, so the monotonicity argument holds under S1-S2. The claim as worded (CFL vs 2R-DD, 10 of 10 conditions, 2.4-14.6 points) is supported, within a self-defined metric. What it does not show is that the four-bar is needed for room: in Fig. 3a-b the belt-driven revolute (2R-remote) is at or above the CFL at 90 and 120 deg for most thicknesses, and the CFL only wins clearly at 150 deg. So the "room" contribution is mostly remote actuation, which is not new, and the CFL's own share is confined to deep flexion. No seed variance is given, although the smallest gap is 2.4 points and the search is a GA with three seeds.

(2) Redesign. Claim: a fixed axis cannot preserve the contact-surface motion, a CFL can. Evidence: Eq. (6)-(8), which are correct and elementary (a chord of a rotation), and the Pareto study (Fig. 3d-g). The bound is conditional on a mounting rule (the pin cannot sit below the pad mount, no lugs, no spacers) that is asserted in one clause. It also does not bind the main baseline: 2R-DD sits below the bound because its servo already holds the face deep, RCJ falls below it by enlarging the roller, and a fixed axis that may slide c0 can reach 0.59-0.80 of it. The CFL reaches 10-51% of the bound, so in its worst cell the margin over the relaxed fixed-axis floor is small (51% vs 59%), and without the start offset it is lowest in 7 of 9 cells, not all. "Can preserve" in the abstract is stronger than "0.35-4.31 mm residual".

(3) Measurement error. Claim: under a reused impedance setpoint the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact and not Delta_skel. Evidence: Spearman correlations over 14-45 simulated hands in Isaac Lab, repeated in Drake with point and hydroelastic contact and on two further open-loop tasks; partial correlations; Holm correction. This is a careful simulation study for an extended abstract, and the direction of the result is convincing. Its weight is limited by four things. First, the mechanism is close to a tautology of position-type control: Delta_contact includes the static offset of the face at q = 0, the same joint target then presses more or less, and the limit load scales with preload. The partial rho with the face offset held (0.69) says there is more than this, but the paper does not separate the static shift from the path shape in the headline numbers. Second, the result is specific to the protocol. Table I shows that under torque and re-zero-at-touch protocols the error follows the skeleton (0.71, 0.88) more than the contact surfaces (0.69, 0.44), and force matching leaves 3-7%, better than the proposed redesign's ~20%. The paper argues the reused setpoint is the protocol of interest with one citation; a reader may equally conclude that the cheaper remedy is to re-zero or match force. Third, the "true" interface effect is a simulation-only counterfactual: a 7 mm pad on the 3 mm hand O does not fit, so Delta(O) for a thicker pad is defined by giving O the soft pad's stiffness. Thickness is part of an interface; the paper needs to say plainly which properties of the pad are "the interface" and which belong to the finger. Fourth, the first-order model predicts 230% where 124% is measured, and R itself errs 41% at 9 mm, so "near 20%" holds up to 7 mm only (the conclusion says this; the abstract and contributions do not).

No hardware exists yet; the title names a hand, the evidence concerns one planar finger's PIP column in simulation. The planned hardware test is concrete and falsifiable, which is appropriate for this format.

What the scores rest on. The idea of taking the contact-surface motion, rather than the skeleton, as the invariant of a redesign is original and well motivated, and the pole gives one compact explanation for both room and invariance. The analysis is correct as far as I can verify, and the experiment is unusually disciplined for a design abstract (stated refutation conditions, partial correlations, multiple simulators and contact models). Against this: all evidence is computational, the benefit of the CFL over the simpler belt-driven revolute for coverage is modest outside 150 deg, the measurement claim is protocol-dependent and partly follows from the definition of Delta_contact, the practical costs of the four-bar (mechanical advantage 0.41-0.58, link strength, lateral stiffness, backlash, width taken by crossed links) are not discussed, and the paper is very dense, with the acronym CFL never expanded and the experimental design hard to reconstruct. I place it at the acceptance threshold for an extended abstract with preliminary experiments.
