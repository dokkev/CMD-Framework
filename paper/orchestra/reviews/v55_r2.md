ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper asks which kinematic property a finger should keep when it is redesigned for a thicker contact interface. Its answer is the motion of the contact surfaces relative to each other (the paper calls this the "contact Jacobian" and measures it with Δ_contact), not the skeleton (Δ_skel, the fingertip path). It makes three groups of claims.

(1) Analytical (Eqs. 1–5). Eq. (3) is the standard finite-displacement identity. Eq. (4) uses the triangle inequality on the pole shift seen from two frames that differ by a translation of Δt. Both derivations are correct under the stated assumptions: the same initial contact point, and the frame F_p translating rigidly by Δt. They are elementary, but they are useful and clearly framed. The Eq. (5) bound for a fixed axis depends on the claim that "its pin and lugs must sit above a full-width pad's mount". This is a packaging argument, not a kinematic one, and it is asserted rather than shown. A revolute joint whose axis sits below the link line (a pin in a clevis offset toward the palm, for example) is not obviously excluded. The Fig. 2(a) "revolute stays on the link line" result follows from that same assumption.

(2) Design studies (Fig. 3). CoDe covers more than the at-joint finger in all 10 feasible conditions, by 2.4–14.6 pp, and preserves Δ_contact best. These are believable outcomes of the optimization. However, the four-bar has many more design variables, and the extra freedom (pivot heights, F′) is granted only in the contact-preserving problem. So "best preservation" partly reflects the size of the design space rather than the principle. The paper partly addresses this: at 7 mm, freeing the pivots without Δ_MCP gives E = 17%, versus 21% with them fixed.

(3) Simulation evidence. This is the core claim. The error in a measured pad effect is defined against O, the original hand fitted with the new pad's contact properties on its own geometry. That error is shown to rank with Δ_contact (ρ = 0.94 over the grid; partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δ_skel held fixed) and not with Δ_skel. My main concern is circularity in this setup. Δ_contact measures how closely a redesign reproduces O's contact-point trajectory, and the "error" measures how closely the redesign's outcome reproduces O's outcome under the same command. With pads that do not deform and a thicker pad modelled as a friction or stiffness change, a redesign that reproduces O's contact kinematics will reproduce O's contact outcomes almost by construction. The finding therefore shows that the simulators are consistent, not that a new principle holds. The more interesting result is the negative one: Skeleton errs as much as Coverage. Even this is expected once one accepts that the object touches the pads and not the fingertip path. The task where the skeleton should matter (the relay dial at the fingertip end) is, appropriately, reported as outside the claim.

The reference itself is a counterfactual. The thicker pad cannot be fitted to O, which is the premise of the paper. So "the true pad effect" is defined as a friction or stiffness change on a 3 mm geometry, and the real physical consequences of a thicker pad (conformity, contact-patch growth, contact-point migration under load) are absent from both simulators. The limitations section acknowledges this. Still, it means the headline contrast in Fig. 1(d) (−41% vs. −39% vs. +48%) compares two kinematic variants of one contact model, not two measurements of a pad.

The "not only preload" analysis (partial ρ = 0.80 with the force deviation controlled) is a welcome control. The re-referenced setpoint result, which cuts the error from 42% to 14%, also shows that a simple practical fix (retuning the setpoint) removes most of the problem. This weakens the motivation for a hardware redesign principle, and the paper should discuss it as a competing approach rather than as a robustness check.

Statistics are reported without sample sizes. The number of redesigns in the correlation set, the number of placements, and the n behind ρ = 0.94, ρ = −0.98 and the Fig. 4 ρ values are not given. Two of the four Drake tasks have only ρ = 0.43–0.53, which is weak support for "keep this ordering". "Pre-registered" is claimed without any reference to a registry or a timestamped protocol. The policy-transfer results rest on two seeds with large seed-to-seed variance: for cube rotation, Skeleton keeps 65% vs. 20% and Coverage 70% vs. 22%.

The hand is presented as a platform ("Design of a Contact-Centric Robot Hand"), but there is no hardware: the hand exists only as CAD, and the conclusion says hardware tests come next. The modular-finger contribution is evaluated only through coverage.

Overall, the idea is original and clearly argued, the kinematic analysis is sound, and the study is internally careful (multiple simulators, contact models, partial correlations). But the central empirical claim is close to tautological under rigid, non-deforming contact. The reference is a counterfactual that cannot be built. Retuning the setpoint already removes most of the error. And no physical validation is provided. I lean towards rejection.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. Naming is overloaded. "CoDe" denotes the hand, the contact-preserving redesign, and (in the Fig. 3 legends) the crossed four-bar architecture ("labelled CoDe in the figures"). Fig. 1(c, d) calls the coverage-only redesign "Conventional", while the rest of the paper calls it "Coverage". Use one name per object throughout.
2. Fig. 1(c) says "Conventional ... keeps neither fully", but Fig. 1(c) right shows Conventional below CoDe on skeleton motion. Clarify what the reader should conclude from the right panel.
3. Δ_MCP is first used in Sec. III-A ("Δ_MCP, Eq. (7)") before it is defined in Sec. III-C. Define it at first use or move the forward reference.
4. Eq. (1): introduce the complex-plane notation (y, b(q), e^{iq}) explicitly, since the sentence reads oddly in the PDF ("y ↦ e^{iq}y+b(q) in C").
5. Eq. (2): "pole at depth z(q) midway between their joint-side corners" is hard to parse. A small inset in Fig. 2 showing t, z and the gap would help.
6. Eq. (9) is a three-objective minimization, but the selection rule ("keeps both best at near-best coverage") is vague. State the exact rule used to pick CoDe from the Pareto front.
7. Sec. III-D's subsection numbering (III-D1, III-D2) is cross-referenced as "Sec. III-D2" for the CoDe hand definition. Defining the hands once, in a table, would be clearer.
8. Table I: the median signed error is printed alongside E, the median |error|, in the text, and the caption does not explain how the two are computed. Many entries are exactly "0%", which needs explanation (ties, zero-effect placements?). The bold convention is not explained.
9. Table I groups a "compl. pad" column under MuJoCo-Warp and another under Drake hydro., but not under Drake point. Explain the missing cells or make the layout regular.
10. Fig. 4: the per-panel annotations (E point / E hydro / Δ / ρ) are very small and hard to read in print. The ρ values are given without n. The legend markers (circles vs. diamonds) are barely distinguishable.
11. Fig. 3(c): the explanation of at-joint points lying above the fixed-axis bound ("starts from a different one") is confusing. The y-axis "contact motion kept (%)" going to −100 also needs a sentence in the text.
12. Fig. 3(a) right: the marker shapes and colors for the four architectures are small, and infeasible "×" marks overlap the data points.
13. Fig. 5: the quantity under each task changes (Δ_contact, Δ_skel, palm shift), which is easy to miss. Label each row explicitly and state the seed variance in the text.
14. "Contact Jacobian" is used as a synonym for Δ_contact, which is a positional deviation over a flexion range. Either justify the term or call it the contact-point path.
15. The abstract and introduction are dense with qualified clauses. For example, "a fixed axis above the pad's mount cannot keep that motion from the same initial contact point" packs several assumptions into one line. Simplify the abstract.
16. The "pre-registered" sentence (Sec. IV-A) needs specifics or should be removed.
17. Sec. IV-A ("Hands") mentions "further redesigns, each held to a prescribed Δ_contact while Δ_skel varies" without saying how many there are or what their range is.
18. Sec. IV-B1 mixes Isaac Lab, Drake, Table I and Fig. 1(d) numbers in one paragraph. Splitting it by simulator or claim would aid readability.
19. Fig. 2(b): name the link and pivot clearance values, or state that the figure is schematic. The "dashed: pivot clearance" circles are hard to see.
20. References: several are cited with "et al." and lack pages or venues. Reference [12] (ICLR 2026) and the arXiv entries should be checked for final venues.
