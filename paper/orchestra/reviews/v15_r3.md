THOUGHT:

Reading as a mechanism-kinematics and synthesis reviewer. The paper argues two things about the PIP joint of a servo-driven finger. (C1) Where the relative pole of the distal phalanx lies sets how much pad length a thick pad can keep near the joint, and a crossed four-bar (CFL) can put that pole near the contact face. (C2) When a finger is redesigned for a thicker pad, what should be kept is the relative motion of the two contact faces, written in a frame attached to the proximal face. The paper calls this the "contact Jacobian". The skeleton (link-line) motion is not the right thing to keep. The kinematic core is classical planar displacement geometry (finite poles, mirror lines, chord lengths). The interesting part is using it as a design invariant for interface studies.

Checking the mathematics (I re-derived each statement):

- Lemma 1. (I - R_q) has determinant 4 sin^2(q/2), and its inverse is (1/2)I + (1/2)cot(q/2) R_{pi/2}. So P = (1/2)(b + cot(q/2) R_{pi/2} b) is correct. "Finite pole = instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is also correct: if P(q) = p_hat(q) for all q, then (I - R_q)P' = 0, so P' = 0 for q != 0.
- Prop. 1. Rotation about P after a reflection in the vertical through P equals a reflection in the line tilted by q/2. The clearance condition at the face corner, (-G/2, t - z), gives G/2 >= (t - z) tan(q/2), which matches Eq. (2). The convex-set-and-mirror-image argument is fine (in fact connectedness is enough). The Fig. 2 numbers check: 5 tan60 = 8.66, 9 tan60 = 15.59, 4 tan60 = 6.93, and the slope is 2 tan60 = 3.46 mm/mm. The "2.0 mm at 90 deg, 7.5 mm at 150 deg" figures also check.
- Prop. 2. The distance from the link-line corner (-G/2, -z) to the mirror line is (G/2)cos(q/2) + z sin(q/2), so the chord is G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). The denominator in Eq. (3) is therefore right. The claim that lowering the pole by delta adds the same amount 2 delta tan(q_req/2) to numerator and denominator at q_req is correct: tan(1 - cos) + sin = tan. For q < q_req the numerator gains at least as much as the denominator, because tan(Q)cos(x) - sin(x) = sin(Q - x)/cos(Q) >= 0. So the monotonicity claim even holds over the whole of Q_eval. The authors could say so.
- Prop. 3 / Eq. (6). c = p + R_q(c0 - p) follows from b = (I - R_q)p. (I - R_q) is a scaled rotation with norm 2 sin(q/2). Correct. A nice consequence the paper only half states: with a shared c0, Delta_contact = max_q 2 sin(q/2)|Delta p(q)| does not depend on which face point is chosen. It is purely a weighted distance between finite-pole paths.
- Eq. (7). The triangle inequality on (I - R_q)(Delta p_face - Delta p_link), with Delta p_face - Delta p_link = Delta t e_z, holds. The Fig. 1 numbers satisfy it (3.4 + 3.9 = 7.3 >= 6.93; 7.7 + 0.9 = 8.6 >= 6.93).
- Corollary 1. Correct for a fixed axis. The rolling-joint claim z = r tan(q/4) is correct. For two equal rollers the finite pole sits at the initial contact abscissa, at distance r/cos(q/4) from the proximal roller centre, so its depth is r tan(q/4). The contrast family's Delta_contact = sqrt(3) Delta_h = 2 sin(60 deg) Delta_h also checks. The statement that exactly keeping c(q) requires conjugating the motion by a Delta_h translation, i.e. translating every pivot into the pad, is right and well put.

So the mathematics is sound as stated. My concerns are about scope and hypotheses, not errors.

(1) Props. 1-2 need S2: the joint-side corners stay equidistant from the pole in x. The corners are fixed in their bodies, so S2 for all q forces the pole abscissa to be constant. That holds for a fixed axis and for equal-roller RCJ. It does not hold for a crossed four-bar, whose finite pole moves in x. The CFL, the paper's own mechanism, therefore lies outside the hypotheses of the "iff". The paper admits this numerically (Eq. 2 is within 0.05 mm median and 1.6 mm worst on CFL designs), but the text should say plainly that S2 fails for the CFL. Also, 1.6 mm is about half a mm-of-thickness at 120 deg, which is not negligible. The S1 mirror argument also implicitly needs equal mount depths/thicknesses near the corner, which Prop. 1 does not state (Prop. 2 adds "equal pads").

(2) H2 is benchmarked against the fixed-axis bound. A four-bar has four pivot locations instead of one axis, so beating a fixed-axis lower bound is close to expected. "Fails if it reaches the bound" is a weak falsification criterion. The interesting kinematic question is left to NSGA-II: what is the smallest Delta_contact a four-bar can reach when all its pivots must stay above the mounting depth d? That is a constrained approximate motion-synthesis problem: match the Delta_h-shifted finite-pole path at precision positions, with pivot regions bounded. Burmester or least-squares pole-matching theory could give either a bound or at least a principled explanation of the 10-51% figure. Note also that 51% occurs at q_req = 90 deg, 9 mm, so the four-bar's advantage is weakest at shallow flexion. The paper does not discuss this.

(3) "Contact Jacobian" is a stretch. What is kept (approximately) is the planar relative displacement of the distal face with respect to the proximal face, parameterised by q. Equivalently, it is the finite-pole path, or the pair of centrodes, relative to the faces. The MCP column is explicitly not kept. Delta_contact is a position difference, not a Jacobian difference. The CFL reduces the change; it does not keep it. The title ("Redesigns That Keep the Contact Jacobian") and C2 overstate this. Classical language (relative motion / centrodes / pole path relative to the face) would be more precise, and it would link to the synthesis literature, which is barely cited (only Bottema-Roth).

(4) Study 3 statistics. Eq. (7) makes Delta_contact and Delta_skel trade off along near-optimal redesigns. So within R/U-type designs the two are anti-correlated by construction, and Delta_skel's negative rho (-0.40, -0.51) is a consequence of the design set, not evidence. The authors know this: they add a fixed-axis contrast family (Delta_skel = 0) and report a partial rho of 0.88. But with n = 14-16 hands, the contrast family's large errors (95-228%) probably dominate the rank correlation. I want rho without the contrast family, and within R/U only. The other candidates are weak competitors. Pad thickness takes three values. The protocol cancels the transmission change by construction. "Coverage" is an absolute quantity, not a change relative to O. A mechanistic competitor would be fairer: the change in the contact angle/normal on the object, the overclosure angle at first contact, or the squeeze force. The paper itself shows that most of the error is force-mediated: under reused torque the errors fall to 3-40%. The C2 claim is therefore strongly tied to a position-controlled, compliant-servo protocol. That is a legitimate protocol, but it should be framed as such.

(5) C1 depends on the servo size. "CFL beats 2R-DD" at all 10 conditions is close to a foregone conclusion once a servo body sits at the joint. The informative comparison is CFL vs 2R-remote, which isolates the polycentric part. There, the CFL loses at 90 deg for 3-7 mm and wins only at >= 120 deg / thicker pads (they cross between 5 and 7 mm at 120 deg). That part of C1 is condition-dependent, and C1's wording hides it. The optimizer results are "best of three seeds", the CFL got extra refinement, and some margins are 2.4 points. Seed spread is needed. Other virtual-pivot mechanisms (circular guide / RCM, cross-axis flexural pivot, geared rolling joints with unequal radii) are not discussed. The text argues "a pole on a deep face must be virtual", but that argument does not single out the crossed four-bar.

Stage-1 fit. Hypotheses H1-H3 are explicit, each with a stated failure condition and pre-fixed thresholds, with Holm correction and separately flagged post-hoc analyses. The Stage-2 protocol is concrete (R vs U at 7 mm, paired one-sided Wilcoxon). This is above average for Stage 1. The Stage-2 C1 test (exchange without taking the articulation apart) does not test the coverage claim itself, though. Presentation is very dense: short sentences, but many undefined bits ("O's contacts" vs "grid" columns, hand counts 15/14/45/16, x0, "impedance ratio", "start offset"). The abstract is overloaded with numbers.

Overall: correct and elegant planar kinematics, a novel framing of a real methodological problem (confounds in interface studies), and an honest, well-structured hypothesis design. The weaknesses are scope (assumptions vs the CFL, a weak H2 benchmark, confounding in H3) and terminology. None is fatal for Stage 1. Score 6, Accept, conditional on the clarifications.
