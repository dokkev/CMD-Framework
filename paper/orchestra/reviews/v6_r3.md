THOUGHT:

My background is mechanism design and planar kinematics, so I worked through Sec. III line by line before judging the studies.

**What the paper argues.** When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the designer should keep the relative motion of the two contact faces, not the skeleton motion. Because the relative rotation is phi = q on every design, that relative motion reduces to the path c(q) of one distal-face point in the proximal-face frame. In turn, that path reduces to the finite pole of the displacement 0 -> q. A fixed axis cannot move its pole relative to the face, so it pays a closed-form penalty. A crossed four-bar (CFL) has a virtual pole that can move, so it pays less. Study 3 then shows in simulation that, under a reused flexion command, the error in a measured interface effect tracks Delta_contact and not Delta_skel.

**Checking the mathematics.**

- *Lemma 1.* (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) R_{pi/2}). I verified this directly from I - R_q = [[1-c, s], [-s, 1-c]]. With b = r_C(q) - R_q r_C(0) the displacement is correctly written. The lemma is textbook (Bottema and Roth), and the paper cites it as such. The remark that "the finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is loosely worded. The finite pole always tends to the IC at q = 0 as q -> 0. The intended meaning is "for all q".
- *Proposition 1.* The key step holds: a closed convex set and its mirror image are disjoint iff the set does not meet the mirror line. If it meets the line, the points on the line are fixed by the reflection. The binding corner is the joint-side face corner at depth t = d + h for any z. So G >= 2(t - z) tan(q/2) is right, and it is negative when the pole lies below the face, in which case G = 0 binds at q = 0. Fig. 2 checks out: 5 tan 60 = 8.66, 9 tan 60 = 15.6, 4 tan 60 = 6.93, and the pad lengths 32/25/34 mm are consistent with these.
  - However, S2 is stronger than it reads. For the rotated distal pad to be the mirror image of the proximal pad for every q, the distal pad at q = 0 must be the reflection of the proximal pad in the vertical line through P(q). That holds only if the x-coordinate of the finite pole is constant and equal to the gap midpoint for all q. S2 is therefore a condition on the pole path, not on the pads.
  - The RCJ satisfies it: I computed its finite pole as (0, r tan(q/4)), which matches the paper's z = r tan(q/4). A general four-bar does not satisfy it. The 0.05 mm median agreement on CFL designs suggests near-compliance, but the maximum error and the spread of P_x(q) should be reported.
- *Proposition 2.* I re-derived it. The distance between a point and the rotation of its mirror image is twice its distance from the mirror line, so ||r2s - r1e|| = G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). The denominator follows, given L_vp = G under the "ends meet the phalanx ends" assumption.
  - I also checked the design conclusion. With G = G_sym, dN/dz = dD/dz = 2 tan(q/2). Hence dC/dz = 2 tan(q/2)(D - N)/D^2 > 0 until G hits 0 at z = t. Beyond that, only the denominator grows. So "the best joint turns about a point on the contact face" is correct. This is a nice result, but only a single-q argument is given. With q-dependent z and a max in G_sym, the averaged objective needs a sentence.
- *Proposition 3.* Correct and one line: Delta_c = (I - R_q) Delta_p, and ||I - R_q|| acts as 2 sin(q/2) on vectors.
  - Useful corollary the authors should state: when c0 is shared, Delta_c(q) is the same for every distal material point. This is what makes Delta_contact well defined independent of the choice of "point below the fingertip".
  - Eq. (7): the two pole descriptions differ by the translation of the frame origin Delta_t e_z, and the triangle inequality gives the bound. Correct.
  - The bound is close to definitional, though: it is the same pole seen from two frames offset by Delta_t. It also requires Delta_t != 0, so a recessed pad (Delta_d = Delta_h) escapes it. The sentence "A thicker pad cannot keep both" should carry that qualifier. My numerical checks against the figures pass: R 0.9 + 7.7 >= 6.93, U 3.9 + 3.4 >= 6.93, and the 5 mm skeleton-keeper at Delta_contact ≈ 3.06.
- *Corollary 1.* The algebra is right. Equality also needs q_req <= pi so that the max over q sits at q_req; this holds for the studied set but should be stated. 2R-remote meeting the bound exactly at 90°/120° is a good sanity check.
  - The statement is a kinematic result only under a hardware premise: that the axis cannot move palmar with the face. Kinematically, a fixed axis can sit anywhere. A circular guide or remote-centre mechanism can put it on the face, and lateral clevis pins can put a pin at face depth in a planar model that ignores width. In each case Delta_contact = 0.
  - The paper should say explicitly that the bound is for pin/roller joints whose axis is constrained to lie dorsal to the pad mount. The abstract's "what a fixed axis must" should read "what a fixed axis with unchanged mount depth must". 2R-DD falls below the "bound" in Fig. 4 for exactly this reason.

**Missing kinematic framing.** Keeping c(q) under a face offset Delta_t means producing the conjugated motion T D(q) T^{-1}. For any linkage, the exact solution is to translate every pivot palmar by Delta_t, that is, to move the mechanism into the pad. This is the same obstruction the fixed axis faces. The four-bar's advantage is that its virtual pole lets it approximate the shifted pole path with pivots kept dorsal. That is a motion-generation problem. By Burmester theory, a four-bar matches a prescribed rigid motion exactly at no more than five positions.

Framing it this way explains why CFL Delta_contact grows with Delta_h (0.35 -> 4.31 mm). It also explains why "keeps" is really "reduces". The paper should cite Burmester / precision-position synthesis, which is the literature most directly tied to its redesign problem.

**Metric concern (Study 2).** Fig. 4d shows that 2R-DD and RCJ start 5.3 and 9.3 mm apart at q = 0, because their reference point moved along the face. Corollary 1 assumes a kept c0, and these designs violate it. Much of their Delta_contact is a tangential offset at extension. For a flat face meeting a cylinder, tangential sliding of the material reference point matters much less than the normal offset and orientation of the face line.

The "CFL changes it least in every cell" claim therefore partly reflects the metric choice. I would like Delta_contact split into components normal and tangential to the face, or re-anchored at q = 0. Study 3's own mechanism points the same way: it is the distal face "retreating", which is a normal displacement.

**Study 1.**
- The headline comparison is against 2R-DD, which carries its servo at the joint. Against the fair baseline, 2R-remote, the CFL loses at 90° and at thin pads at 120°.
- The text then switches to a second metric (pad length over L_f) on which the CFL wins. Eq. (5) shows why: C_pad's flexed-contour denominator penalizes a palmar pole. The authors explain this, but it reads as metric selection. Which metric is the objective should be settled once.
- MA_wc is "reported" but no number appears in the text. That matters, because the CFL absorbs thickness partly through its transmission.

**Study 3** is the strongest part, and it is executed with care: pre-fixed gates, partial correlation, worst-case inclusion, solver robustness checks. Still:

- The mechanism is close to expected given the protocol. The cylinder is placed where O touches both pads at q0, and the finger is then position-commanded 5° further through a stiff servo, so the squeeze depends on the touch-angle shift, and Delta_contact proxies that.
- The five candidates include obvious straw men (coverage, transmission, thickness). The natural competitor, the change in touch angle or the normal face offset at the touch configuration, is not tested.
- The negative rho for Delta_skel is partly built into the sample. By Eq. (7), designs near the bound have Delta_skel ≈ k|Delta_t| - Delta_contact, so the two are anti-correlated by construction. The partial correlation of 0.88 helps, but this should be said.
- n = 15 with a CI of [0.41, 1.00] is wide, and "positive at each thickness" rests on about 5 points each.
- Practical significance is mixed. R still errs 41% at 9 mm, while force matching reaches 3–7% and touch-referenced closure about 20%. Joint torque can be sensed without depending on the interface. The argument that force-based protocols "use signals the interface changes" needs more care.
- C4's claim that "softer actuation weakens the dependence" rests on one K0 plus the torque limit. A stiffness sweep is needed.

**Presentation.** The writing is dense and symbol-heavy, and several symbols clash:
- t is used both for the distance from X and for face depth.
- z is introduced as "dorsal", yet z(q) is a depth below the link line.
- L_vp vs G is unclear.
- "three CFL fingers" in the Study 3 setup is confusing.

The title is very long. The Lemma appears out of order in the flow.

**Stage-1 fit.** Problem, hypotheses and methodology are well fleshed out, and the Stage-2 plan is concrete (O, R and U on one finger, with face tracking). This is above the bar for a Stage-1 abstract.

**Verdict.** The theory is correct but modest: the lemma is textbook, Prop. 3 is one line, and the corollary depends on a hardware premise. The framing, keeping contact-face motion instead of the skeleton, is the real contribution and is useful for the hand-design community. The overclaims ("keep", "a fixed axis must"), the metric issue and the missing synthesis framing are fixable. I lean weak accept.
