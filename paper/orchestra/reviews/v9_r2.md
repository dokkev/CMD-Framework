THOUGHT:

My reading as someone working on manipulation and contact modelling. The paper argues that when a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the property to keep is the motion of the distal contact face in the proximal-phalanx frame (Δ_contact, set by the finite pole of the PIP joint), not the skeleton motion. It gives (i) elementary finite-pole results (clearance gap Eq. 4, coverage Eq. 5, Δc = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| Eq. 6, a fixed-axis lower bound Eq. 8), (ii) a GA/NSGA-II comparison of four PIP joints for coverage (Study 1), (iii) a redesign study showing the CFL changes Δ_contact by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound (Study 2), and (iv) an Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp study in which the error in a measured interface effect under a reused command correlates with Δ_contact and not with four other candidate invariants (Study 3).

What I like. The framing is new and useful: confounding of interface comparisons by the redesign the interface forces is real, rarely stated, and the paper turns it into a measurable quantity. The theory is simple but clean, and Study 2 has a nice internal check (2R-remote lands exactly on the bound of Corollary 1). Each contribution has a stated failure condition, thresholds were fixed before the runs, the paper reports a torque protocol and a hand-independent placement grid to answer obvious objections, and it gives the alternative protocols (touch-triggered closing, force matching) instead of hiding them. For a Stage-1 extended abstract this is a well-developed piece of work.

Where I get stuck is C4, the claim that makes the whole paper matter, and I looked at it closely.

1. Study 3 covers only one architecture. All 18 redesigns are CFL fingers. The claim is that Δ_contact, not skeleton/coverage/thickness/transmission, governs the error for redesigns in general, and Study 2 compares architectures by Δ_contact. But the link from Δ_contact to measurement error is never tested on 2R-DD, 2R-remote or RCJ redesigns, where Δ_contact is larger and arises differently (e.g., Fig. 4d: RCJ's Δ_contact is dominated by a 9 mm offset at q = 0, i.e. the contact point moved along the face, not a change in motion). Whether an offset along the face does the same damage as a change in pole path is an open question that the single-architecture sample cannot answer.

2. The statistics are too weak for the "only predictor" claim. n = 14–18 hands (6 for shape), Spearman ρ, and a verdict based on whether each candidate's 95% interval excludes zero. That is a thresholding argument, not a comparison. Showing that ρ(Δ_contact) is larger than ρ(other) needs a test on the difference of dependent correlations (Steiger/Williams, or a paired bootstrap of the difference), or a multivariable model. The borderline values make this matter. Under the torque protocol coverage has ρ = −0.52 and thickness ρ = 0.50 with n = 16. A Fisher-z 95% interval for |ρ| = 0.52, n = 16 is roughly [0.03, 0.81], which excludes zero. So with a standard parametric interval, two other candidates would also "predict" the error under that protocol, and Δ_contact (0.75) would not be clearly better. The CI method (presumably a bootstrap over hands) is not stated, and the [0.41, 1.00] friction interval hits the ceiling, which points to a small-sample bootstrap with ties. There are 20 intervals (5 candidates × 4 columns) with no multiplicity correction. The skeleton correlation is negative (−0.40, −0.51), so skeleton change predicts lower error. That is an artefact of the designed sample and of Eq. (7) (a thicker pad cannot keep both), which shows the candidates are collinear by construction. The partial correlation given Δ_skel (0.88) helps, but partials with n ≈ 15 are fragile and their intervals are not reported. "It holds at each thickness" with about 5 hands per thickness cannot carry a correlation claim.

3. Sample construction and censoring. The 18 redesigns were not sampled from any natural distribution of redesigns. They were constructed at set Δ_contact values with the skeleton pushed to extremes, so the correlation structure among candidates is whatever the authors built. That is a legitimate intervention design, but then the right analysis is the dose-response under intervention, not a population correlation. Hands that drop the cylinder are excluded per protocol (15/14/18/16). These are likely the high-Δ_contact designs ("distal face retreats 1.5–2.6 mm"), so the outcome is censored on a variable related to the predictor. Treating failures as worst-rank would be more honest, and it would probably strengthen the authors' case.

4. Outcome metric and noise floor. E(H) = |Δ(H) − Δ(O)|/|Δ(O)| is a relative error and is unstable wherever O's effect is small (some load directions, some placements). The median helps, but the distribution of |Δ(O)| over placements/directions is not shown, and there is no absolute-error version. More importantly there is no noise floor: what is E for O against a perturbed copy of O (small parameter jitter, a different solver seed or initial pose)? Under the torque protocol errors are 3–40% and R vs U is 5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%. Without a floor I cannot tell whether those gaps exceed solver variability. The robustness check (R < U in 20–24 of 22–24 pairs) covers only R vs U, not the correlation claim.

5. Contact model fidelity. The motivating interfaces are thick compliant pads and camera-based tactile modules. Pad stiffness is varied as a penalty contact stiffness (2.5 to 1.0 kN/m), and friction runs from 1.3 to 2.0 (high). A 9 mm compliant pad conforms, shifts its contact patch and changes the effective pole of contact, which a rigid-geometry soft-contact model does not capture. Since the paper's quantity is literally where the faces meet the object, patch distribution under deformation matters. The limit-load test (slip, loss of a pad, collapse) in a soft-constraint solver is also sensitive to solver impedance. Only one robustness sweep on this is reported.

6. Over-closure and protocol choice. Under the reused command, U presses 3.3× (Fig. 1: 3.9×) harder, so much of the error is a force effect of position control with K0 = 5 N·m/rad. The torque protocol cuts error to 3–40%, and force matching cuts it to 3–7%, better than the best R redesign under the reused command (21% at 7 mm, 41% at 9 mm). The dismissal that force matching "needs sensing that the interface itself changes" is not convincing: joint torque or motor current sensing does not depend on the pad. So the practical significance of keeping Δ_contact is partly undercut by the paper's own numbers. Even the best contact-keeping redesign leaves 41% error at 9 mm, so "comparable" studies are not actually achieved by the method.

7. Smaller points on theory and clarity. Eq. (2) calls [c'; 1] the PIP column of "the contact Jacobian", but it is a relative face-point velocity in the proximal frame, not the usual grasp/contact Jacobian mapping joint rates to contact-frame velocities at the actual object contact. The actual contact point on the cylinder moves along the face with the object, and Δ_contact tracks a single fixed face point below the fingertip. Why one material point summarises contact for objects of different sizes needs an argument; Eq. (6) answers it only for equal c0. The results are mostly textbook finite-displacement kinematics (the authors cite them). The novelty lies in applying them to pad clearance, not in the math. S1/S2 are strong symmetry assumptions, and their validity for the optimised designs is checked only via the 0.05 mm median. Study 1 gives CFL extra refinement from neighbouring designs, an asymmetric search budget, although the authors say the result holds without it. Minor inconsistencies: Fig. 1 says 15 redesigns and 3.9×, the text says 18 redesigns and 3.3×. The text never says how the 95% intervals are computed. The table is hard to read (column headers "grid", "O's contacts").

8. Stage-2 plan. Per the venue, methodology should be fully fleshed out. The hardware plan (one finger, O/R/U, "track both faces", two pad surfaces) is one paragraph, with no sample size, no number of trials, and no account of how Δ_contact will be measured to sub-mm accuracy or how the reused-command protocol and the pull test will be instrumented. A power estimate from the simulation effect sizes would be easy and valuable.

Overall: an original and well-motivated idea with careful kinematics. But the central empirical claim (C4, "only Δ_contact predicts the error") rests on a single-architecture, n ≈ 15 constructed sample, threshold-based CI comparisons whose verdict depends on the interval method, outcome censoring, no noise floor, and a contact model that cannot represent the interfaces the paper is about. The paper's own force-matching result also weakens the practical case. I lean to reject in its current form, but these problems can be fixed and I could be persuaded, since Stage 1 permits preliminary evidence.
