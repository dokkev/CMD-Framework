ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper makes two claims about a servo-driven finger, both tested in simulation. C1: remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more room for proximal and distal pads than a servo-at-joint revolute PIP (2R-DD). C2: for a pad (interface) effect measured on a redesigned finger to match the effect on the reference finger, the redesign should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, rather than the skeleton (fingertip path). The theory rests on the finite pole. Prop. 1 gives the clearance gap, Prop. 2 gives coverage, Eq. (6) gives ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||, and Corollary 1 is a fixed-axis lower bound on Δcontact. I checked the main relations. Eq. (6) follows from c = R c0 + (I−R)p. The 2 tan(q/2) gap growth and the Fig. 2 numbers (5 tan 60° = 8.7 mm, 4 tan 60° = 6.9 mm) agree. Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality. The analysis is simple and correct, and it explains the design choice: a deep face needs a pole below the pivots, which only a virtual pole can give. That point is useful to a hand designer.

Evidence for C1 (Study 1) is good for a simulation study. Coverage was optimized with GA/NSGA-II over three seeds. CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 feasible conditions by 2.4–14.6 points, and its worst run beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10. The 2R-remote ablation separates remote actuation from the polycentric joint. The ideal-joint upper bounds are a nice touch. The weak points:
- 2R-DD is a fairly easy baseline. Anyone who has built a LEAP-style hand knows the servo body takes the pad space.
- Mechanical advantage is "left free." The CFL optima give only 0.41–0.58 of a direct-drive PIP. For a servo hand, PIP torque often sets grasp strength, so this is a real cost. It is only acknowledged in the conclusion and backed by a "≤ 25% of stall torque" observation for one task.
- Coverage is a cylinder-inscribed-in-V metric at three poses. It is a reasonable proxy but narrow.
- Nothing is said about the practical costs of four-bar PIPs: link backlash, compliance of printed links under load, and pin wear. All of these move the pole by amounts comparable to the 1 mm tolerance the paper later relies on.

Evidence for C2 (Studies 2 and 3) is the more interesting part, and the statistics are unusually careful: pre-registration, bootstrap intervals, permutation tests, Holm correction, partial correlations, and robustness to contact stiffness, friction impedance and substeps. ρ = 0.80–0.86 between the error E and Δcontact over 14–45 redesigned hands is consistent, and the honest report of a failed prediction (the reversed share does not rise) adds credibility. My main concerns are about the validity of the proxy, not the statistics:
1. Pads are meshes in point contact, and one normal stiffness is shared by all pads. In this simulation, pad thickness is therefore purely geometric. The things that make real thick compliant pads interesting are a larger contact patch, distributed pressure, torsional friction and indentation of several mm under load. None of these is modelled, yet they are exactly what interface studies measure. The paper names "one contact model" as a limitation, but the C2 claim is phrased about interfaces in general.
2. The ~1 mm Δcontact tolerance is smaller than the compression of a soft 7–9 mm pad and probably than the assembly backlash of a printed CFL. It is not clear the tolerance is meaningful on hardware.
3. The C2 result depends on the protocol. With a reused impedance setpoint (q0 + 5° over-closure), Δcontact predicts the error. With "touch" closing, which is easy on a servo hand using current or load readback, Δcontact is not significant (0.44) and Δskel predicts (0.88), with errors of 6–61%. The paper argues the reused setpoint is what servo hands run. That is partly true, but it means the principle is "keep the contact Jacobian *if* you reuse joint-space targets," and the abstract and C2 should keep that conditional prominent. Table I already shows it; the prose sometimes reads more universally.
4. The definition of the "true" interface effect is a counterfactual: O with the soft pad's stiffness but O's 3 mm geometry. This is coherent within the framework, but it is a choice. One could argue that a thicker pad's true effect includes its geometry. The headline "48% more vs 41% less" depends on this definition, and it should be argued explicitly.
5. The hand set includes designs constructed "at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton most." Correlations over a designed set can be inflated by its spread. The 27 pre-registered grid hands mitigate this, but the within-thickness intervals are wide ([0.41, 0.99]).

Stage 1 fit: the problem, hypotheses with explicit failure conditions (H1–H3) and the Stage 2 protocol are laid out, with physical predictions (21, 72, 124%) and a Page trend test. This meets the Stage 1 bar well. Gaps in the Stage 2 plan:
- The C1 physical test ("fails if an exchange needs the articulation taken apart") tests swappability, not coverage.
- Nothing says what will be concluded if the hardware Page test fails, for example from pad deformation versus backlash.
- Measurement noise on |Δ(O)| for bare versus textured covers can blow up the relative error E. An expected effect size or power argument is missing.

Clarity is the weakest dimension:
- The writing is extremely compressed: many symbols, captions that carry results (Table I, Figs. 3–5), and short declarative sentences that drop needed context.
- Notation is overloaded. M is a finger design and then the middle Stage 2 hand. O, R and U appear before they are defined in the Study 3 setup.
- Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 text is very small.
- Phrases such as "degenerate 2R-remote designs (0.21, 0)", "CFL ideal" and "spacers keep Δcontact = 0 at 8.6–18.2 points" need a reader to reconstruct a lot.
- The related work is appropriately narrow for Stage 1.

Overall: a sound, original framing (the pole as the single design quantity for both pad room and pad-motion invariance), a correct small theory, and careful simulation statistics. It is held back by a contact model too simple for the pad claims, protocol dependence and dense presentation. It is above the Stage 1 bar, so I recommend acceptance at 6.
