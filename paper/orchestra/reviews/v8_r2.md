THOUGHT:

My background is manipulation and contact modelling, so I read Sec. III for correctness and spent most of my time on Sec. V (Study 3) and its statistics. Study 3 is what carries the "why it matters" claim (C4), and that claim turns a kinematics paper into a methodology paper about interface studies.

What the paper claims. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker pad or tactile module, the quantity to preserve is the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame, i.e. the path c(q) of a distal-face point. The skeleton (fingertip path from the link line) is the wrong target. A finite-pole analysis gives clearance (Eq. 4), coverage (Eq. 5) and a contact-motion law (Eq. 6). It also gives a sum rule Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q/2)|Δt| (Eq. 7) and a fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 8). A crossed four-bar (CFL) is shown by optimization to keep coverage (Study 1) and to keep c(q) at 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound (Study 2). A MuJoCo-Warp/Newton simulation (Study 3) shows that, when a flexion command or joint torque is reused, the error in a measured interface effect rank-correlates with Δcontact and not with four other candidate invariants.

Theory check. Lemma 1 is the standard finite-pole result; (I − R_q)^{-1} = ½(I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}) is correct. Eq. (6) follows directly: Δc = (I − R_q)Δp, so ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||. Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality between pole shifts measured from two reference lines that differ by Δt. Corollary 1 is immediate for a fixed axis. The mirror argument for Prop. 1 is neat, but S2 (joint-side corners equidistant from the pole in x) is strong. It is validated only by a median error of 0.05 mm on CFL designs, with no maximum reported. Overall, Sec. III is correct, compact and genuinely useful. Its main novelty is framing, not mathematics: the finite pole is classical, and the new part is turning it into a statement about pads. I count that as a real, if modest, contribution.

Studies 1–2. These are reasonable for a Stage-1 abstract. The authors are candid that 2R-remote beats CFL on C̄pad at 90° and at thin pads at 120°. Two things bother me. First, CFL alone gets a refinement step from neighbouring designs. The paper says the 2R-DD result holds without it, but not whether the 2R-remote and RCJ comparisons do. Second, three seeds per condition is thin for a GA/NSGA-II search over different-dimensional spaces, and no seed spread is shown. The CFL "10–51% of the bound" figure compares an optimized four-bar with a bound that assumes the mount depth is kept. That is fair, and Fig. 4d is informative.

Study 3 is where I have serious concerns.

(1) Built-in correlation between Δcontact and Δskel. By the paper's own Eq. (7), for a given thickness change Δcontact and Δskel cannot both be small. Across redesigns at a fixed Δt they are forced into a trade-off. So if error tracks Δcontact, Δskel must correlate negatively with error. The reported ρ = −0.40 / −0.51 is exactly that. "Skeleton does not predict" is therefore largely a consequence of "contact does predict" plus Eq. (7). It is not an independent finding. The partial correlation of Δcontact with Δskel held fixed (0.88) is reported. The partial for Δskel with Δcontact held fixed is not, and that is the number that would test whether the skeleton carries any information.

(2) The protocol matches the metric. The cylinder is placed "where O touches it", in a frame anchored on the proximal contact face. Δcontact is defined in that same frame (Fp, origin on the proximal face). The error mechanism the authors identify is over-closure from face offset: U presses 3.3–3.9× harder, and the face offset at the grasp alone gives ρ = 0.92. So under a position-controlled closure against an object placed relative to the reference contact face, error tracks face offset. That is close to true by construction. The more interesting question is whether Δcontact still matters for an object placed in a palm- or world-fixed frame (as in a scripted pick, or a policy whose object pose comes from vision), or under a contact-referenced closure. The reused-torque protocol partly addresses this (ρ = 0.75, error 3–40%). But the closing-on-touch and force-matched protocols (median 20% and 3–7%) are reported without saying whether Δcontact predicts their residual error.

(3) Weak and asymmetric competitor candidates. The transmission dθ/dq is compensated by the protocol's own lookup table under reused command, and bypassed entirely under reused joint torque. It cannot predict anything here, so it is a strawman, and it is also unclear how a function was reduced to a scalar. Pad thickness takes only three values with heavy ties across 15 hands, so its Spearman ρ is close to a between-group test with n = 3 groups. Coverage is reported as a raw C̄pad value, but the other candidates are changes from O. The comparison is not like for like.

(4) Sample size and dependence. There are n = 14–16 hands, and the count differs by column with no explanation. The hands form three thickness families, each built against the same O, plus deliberately chosen R/U/extreme redesigns. The sample is designed, not random. Spearman ρ and a bootstrap "interval over hands" on a designed, clustered sample of 15 do not support population-level statements. Five candidates × three effects with no multiple-comparison correction makes it worse. Under reused torque, coverage (−0.52) and thickness (0.50) are borderline at n = 16. "Δcontact is the only candidate" there rests on interval widths that the reader cannot see. "Positive at each thickness" uses n ≈ 5 per family. The robustness section checks only paired R-vs-U ordering, not the correlation claim.

(5) Contact modelling. Pad "material stiffness" is a contact stiffness of 2.5 → 1.0 kN/m, i.e. solver impedance. A 9 mm pad is presumably a rigid geometry with soft-constraint contact. Thickness then changes only geometry, not compliance, patch size or pressure distribution, and those are exactly the interface properties such studies measure. µ of 1.3–2.0 is high, and limit-load detection under MuJoCo's soft constraints (creep, regularization) needs thresholds that are not stated. The paper says the thresholds were fixed beforehand but never gives them. The grasp itself is under-specified. A free cylinder rests on one finger's proximal pad, gets loaded in four in-plane directions, and the hand has three fingers: what reacts the load, is it a single-finger hook grasp, and is the palm involved? The relative error |Δ(H) − Δ(O)|/|Δ(O)| is undefined or unstable when Δ(O) is small, and no Δ(O) magnitudes are reported. The effect is also strongly impedance-dependent: at K0/10, U's error drops from 119% to 44%. So the magnitudes are conditional on one actuator stiffness, which is not related to real servo stiffness.

(6) Scope. The principle is stated for multi-phalanx grasps, with the MCP held, in the plane, with cylinders only. That is fine for Stage 1. But C4 is phrased generally ("follows the change in contact-surface motion, not the change in skeleton motion"), and the evidence is one planar protocol family.

Presentation. The prose is very terse; each sentence is one claim. That reads well at the top level but hides key setup details: what the grasp is, which solver produced the main numbers, how dθ/dq becomes a scalar, and the thresholds. Fig. 1 is a good teaser. The title is long and awkward. The C1–C4 failure conditions are welcome for Stage 1, though C3's ("fails if it reaches the bound") is a low bar.

Overall. The theory is clean and the framing is valuable to the tactile-hand community. Studies 1–2 are adequate. The headline empirical claim (C4) is partly circular: the protocol, the frame and Eq. (7) together make its main result largely expected. Its statistics are thin and its competitor candidates are weak. The contact model does not represent the interface physics the paper is ultimately about. For Stage 1 the methodology must be fully fleshed out. Here it is mostly present, but the key confound (object placement frame) is not addressed. I lean towards rejection but could be persuaded, since Stage 1 permits preliminary experiments.
