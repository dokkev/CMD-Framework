ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology (does each experiment test what it claims, and are the statistics adequate).

Central claims. (C1) A remote-actuated finger with a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint (2R-DD) finger at every pad thickness and required flexion. (C2) When a finger is redesigned for a thicker pad, the thing to keep is the contact-surface motion c(q), which is the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton. A fixed axis has a closed-form lower bound on the change (Eq. 9), and the CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. (C3) In simulation, when an impedance setpoint is reused across redesigns, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect (friction or stiffness change -> change in limit load) tracks Delta_contact (Spearman 0.80-0.86). It does not track Delta_skel. This holds in a second simulator, with hydroelastic pads, and in two more tasks, and the error can reverse the sign of a conclusion.

Evidence for C1. The geometry is clean. Prop. 1-2 give interpretable laws for clearance and coverage under the idealizations S1-S2, and the paper says plainly that the optima satisfy S1-S2 only approximately. The comparison is an optimization result: a GA/NSGA-II with three seeds per cell. No convergence evidence is shown, such as seed spread or best-so-far curves. Each joint's "best" design therefore depends on the optimizer, and "in all 10 conditions" relies on every competitor being optimized equally well. The margins (2.4-14.6 points) are large enough that I believe the ranking against 2R-DD. Still, a seed-spread number per cell is needed to support a universal "every condition" statement. The coverage metric (cylinder-inscribed overlap of unoccluded faces) is a reasonable proxy. The paper does not link it to any contact outcome, such as grasp stability or pad load sharing.

Evidence for C2. Prop. 3 and Corollary 1 are correct for finite-displacement poles, and Eq. 9 is a useful, checkable bound. Fig. 3d-g is consistent with it. Two gaps:
- Delta_contact is defined at one material point (the face point below the fingertip). In a real grasp the object touches elsewhere, and the contact point rolls or slides over a compliant surface. Calling c(q) "the contact Jacobian" is defensible kinematically (Eq. 5 and the text after it). How much the single-point metric represents the actual contact locations in the experiments is not quantified.
- 2R-DD and RCJ "include a start offset at q=0". Without that offset the CFL is least in only 7 of 9 cells. Which baseline is fair should be stated and justified, not left to a parenthesis.

Evidence for C3. This is the core of the hypothesis-driven part, and it is where my main concerns sit.

1) Mechanism near-tautology. The paper itself identifies the pathway: under a reused position-impedance setpoint, a face that moves changes the preload, and limit load scales with preload, so E ≈ |N_H/N_O - 1|. A finger whose contact surface sits differently relative to the object at the commanded q will press with a different force. That is close to a direct consequence of tau = K0 (q_cmd - q_contact), and Delta_contact is the natural geometric proxy for (q_cmd - q_contact). The partial correlation with each hand's force deviation held fixed (0.79 under reused torque, pre-registered; 0.64 at the setpoint, post hoc) is the right control and the most informative number in the section. It deserves more space. The post hoc setpoint partial should not carry inferential weight.

2) Is the compliant thick pad actually modelled? The motivating object is a thick, soft pad (5-9 mm). In Isaac Lab, contact is point contact with a stiffness parameter, and the stiffness effect is 2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m. The hydroelastic Drake run uses "0.1 MPa over 3 mm on every hand, so the interface itself is unchanged". The soft-pad swap uses O "given the soft pad's stiffness". So no experiment simulates a 7-9 mm compliant layer deforming under load. Under realistic preloads, such a layer moves the effective contact point and patch centroid by millimetres. That is the same order as the ~1 mm Delta_contact threshold the paper recommends. The claim "keep Delta_contact below ~1 mm and the error stays near 20%" is therefore made at a length scale the contact models do not resolve. A hydroelastic or FEM run with the actual pad thickness and modulus on each redesign is needed before C3 can be stated for thick compliant pads rather than for kinematic geometry.

3) Design of the hand set and the statistics. The candidate hands are curated: R (minimizes Delta_contact), U (ignores it), and redesigns built to reach a set Delta_contact "while keeping or changing the skeleton most". This extreme-group design pushes Delta_contact and Delta_skel apart on purpose, which is good for discriminating between them. It also inflates Spearman ρ relative to what a random population of redesigns would show. Fig. 4a shows that between 1.5 and 3.5 mm the error ranges from about 10% to about 200% at similar Delta_contact, so the predictive value inside the practically relevant range is weaker than ρ ≈ 0.83 suggests. With 14-16 hands, the paper concedes that Delta_contact's lead over Delta_skel is not significant. Only the 45-hand grid separates them. The headline should rest on a direct paired comparison (for example a Steiger/Williams test or a bootstrap of ρ_contact - ρ_skel) on the larger set, not on "interval excludes zero" per candidate. The predictors are also correlated with each other (thickness, Delta_contact, Delta_skel, transmission), so a joint model (for example a rank regression or partial correlations for all candidates) would say more than single-variable ρ values.

4) Outcome definition. E is the median over placements and directions "where both hands hold". Conditioning on success is a selection that can hide the largest errors: a redesign that drops the cylinder in some placements loses exactly those cases. The fraction of excluded cases per hand should be reported and analysed. Treating a non-hold as maximal error would be a sensible sensitivity check. The relative error |Delta(H) - Delta(O)|/|Delta(O)| also becomes unstable when Delta(O) is small, for example for the stiffness effect.

5) Simulator dependence. The Drake replica gives ρ = 0.67 with point contact, and the hands "rank only loosely alike across the two models (0.39)". So the trend survives, but the per-hand error is not a robust quantity across contact models. No sensitivity to solver parameters is reported (time step, contact regularization or MuJoCo soft-contact parameters, loading rate for "load rises until the grasp gives way"). Friction-limited breakaway is exactly where compliant-contact solvers are least reliable, through creep and regularized friction. The command-sensitivity check (0.05° -> <1%) is welcome. A matching check on the contact parameters is missing.

6) Scope of the protocol. Table I shows that under touch re-zeroing, a common real protocol, Delta_skel (0.88) predicts the error better than Delta_contact (0.44), and under ideal torque both are about 0.7. The thesis therefore holds for one actuation protocol: a reused position-impedance setpoint. The abstract and contributions state it more broadly ("a redesign should keep the contact Jacobian"). The scope belongs in the claim itself.

7) The sign-reversal example (U: +48% vs truly -41%) is a single hand chosen as the coverage-only design. It is illustrative, not evidence of frequency. The numbers are also hard to reconcile: O measures -37%, while the "truth" is -41%. Which hand or measurement defines the ground truth?

Stage 2 falsification plan. A plan is given (O, R, M, U; markers for Delta_contact; planar slide; bare vs textured covers; 5 placements x 4 directions x 5 repeats; Page trend test). The unit of inference for the hypothesis is the redesign. Here that is three 7 mm hands, so the trend test runs over n = 3 ordered levels, and the 100 trials per hand are repeated measures, not independent replicates. A Page test that finds "no rise" over three levels is a weak refutation criterion. Stating a quantitative tolerance against the predicted 21/72/124%, with an interval, would make the test sharper. Marker-based measurement of Delta_contact must resolve differences of about 1 mm on a deforming pad, so its accuracy should be specified. Using bare vs textured covers mainly changes friction. A stiffness or thickness swap, the motivating case, is not in the hardware plan.

Presentation. The writing is extremely compressed. Many results are packed into single sentences with parenthetical qualifiers ("without which the CFL is least in 7 of 9", "partial 0.79, pre-registered ... 0.64, post hoc"). Key experimental definitions are scattered: what "grid", "torque" and "touch" mean, how the hands in each Table I column were chosen, and how "pre-registered" was implemented. Fig. 4 is dense but readable. Fig. 2c needs its caption to be understood.

Overall. The kinematic analysis (Prop. 1-3, Corollary 1) is a genuine and clean contribution. The simulation study is carefully controlled for a Stage 1 paper: two simulators, pre-registration, Holm correction and a mediator control. Its main methodological limits are that the compliant thick pad is not actually simulated, that the hand set is curated with only modest n, and that the claim's generality exceeds the one protocol under which it holds. These are fixable in Stage 2, and the hypotheses and falsification plan are explicit, so I lean towards acceptance at the Stage 1 bar.
