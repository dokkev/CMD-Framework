CoDe Hand: Same Hand, Different Contact — Keep
How the Contact Surfaces Move, Not the Skeleton,
for Comparable Contact-Interface Studies
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—Contact interfaces such as thick compliant pads
and tactile modules decide how a robot hand touches an
object. To compare two interfaces, the finger must usually
be redesigned, because servos and joints occupy the space an
interface needs. The redesign changes the hand, so a measured
difference cannot be attributed to the interface alone. We
propose that a redesigned finger keep how its contact surfaces
move relative to each other, not its skeleton. In the proximalphalanx frame this motion is one column of the finger’s contact
Jacobian, and one point sets it: the pole about which the distal
phalanx turns. We show that a fixed-axis joint keeps it only if
its contact faces keep their distance from the axis, and give a
closed-form lower bound on the change otherwise. A remoteactuated crossed four-bar finger keeps the change within 10–
51% of the fixed-axis bound at near-best contact surface,
less than three other joint types in nearly every condition.
In simulation, when one flexion command or joint torque is
reused across redesigns for 5, 7 and 9 mm pads, the error in
measured friction, stiffness and shape effects follows the change
in contact-surface motion (Spearman ρ = 0.60–0.94; 6 to 45
redesigns per setting). Changes in skeleton, coverage, thickness
and transmission do not predict it consistently, and a fixedaxis finger that keeps its skeleton exactly distorts the friction
effect by 95–228%. Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand
is the next stage of this work.

I. Introduction
Contact is central to dexterous manipulation, and the
contact interface sets that contact. Compliant fingertips
change how an object is held [17, 12]. Vision-based
tactile sensors turn the contact patch into geometry and
force [20, 11]. Robot hands have become experimental
platforms [15, 16, 6], and most are built around servos
for maintainability. Servo bodies and joints occupy the
space an interface needs, above all around the joint. A
large or thick interface therefore costs range of motion or
contact surface, and carrying it is a redesign, not a module
swap [21, 4].
The redesign changes how the contact surfaces move
relative to each other. For a joint that turns about a
fixed axis this change is unavoidable when the pads sit
at their own depth. Interface studies often reuse the same
commands across hands: scripted grasps, frozen policies,
the same actuator targets. A measured difference then
mixes the interface with the hand’s change. One could
keep the skeleton instead, but the object does not meet
the skeleton. It meets the contact surfaces.
In this work we propose CoDe (contact-centric design),
a servo-driven hand whose redesign target is how its

contact surfaces move relative to each other. Its servos
act remotely through a linkage, which frees the space
under the joints. Its PIP joint is a crossed four-bar linkage
(CFL), whose pole moves with flexion, so a thick pad
can sit under the joint. When a new interface asks for
a redesign, the finger is re-optimized to keep its contactsurface motion, not its skeleton motion (Fig. 1). This paper
analyses one planar finger of that hand. Each contribution
is a hypothesis with a stated way to fail:
C1 A bound. Finite-pole laws for the clearance, coverage
and contact-surface motion of planar finger joints. A
fixed axis keeps its contact-surface motion only if its
faces keep their distance from the axis, with a closedform lower bound otherwise (Sec. III).
C2 A finger. At every pad thickness and required flexion,
the CFL keeps more contact surface than a servo-atjoint finger. It fails if the servo-at-joint finger matches
it in any condition (Sec. IV-B). A belt-driven fixed
axis keeps as much at shallow flexion but pays in C3.
C3 A redesign that keeps contact. Re-optimized for
thicker pads, the CFL keeps its contact-surface motion well inside the fixed-axis bound at near-best
coverage. It fails if it reaches the bound (Sec. IV-C).
C4 Why it matters. In simulation, with reused flexion
commands or joint torques, the error in measured
friction, stiffness and shape effects follows the change
in contact-surface motion, not the change in skeleton
motion. It fails if another design quantity predicts the
error in every setting (Sec. V).
Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand (C5) is the
next stage. The principle concerns grasps that use more
than one phalanx; in fingertip-only contact the object
meets the fingertip path. Keeping the contact-surface
motion does not keep the contact mechanics: those change
with the interface, and they are what an interface study
measures.
II. Related Work
Contact interfaces. Vision-based tactile sensors need an
elastomer, a camera and optics behind the surface [20, 11],
so fingers and hands are designed around them [21, 4].
Thin skins can be replaced without redesign [3]; thick
interfaces come with their own finger, so comparing them
compares fingers.

Finger joints. Open, low-cost hands use fixed-axis servo
joints [16, 6]. Rolling contact joints are compact [9, 18].
Crossed four-bar joints give polycentric finger motion [2,
1], and linkage-driven hands integrate the drive [10]. Polycentric four-bar knees make the path of the instantaneous
centre the design target [14], and finite-position synthesis
places poles to guide a body through given poses [5]. None
of these is analysed for what it takes from the pads, or for
whether its contact-surface motion survives an interface
change. Co-design methods let the morphology change
freely for a task [19, 8]. We instead hold the contact-surface
motion fixed, so that a difference can be attributed to the
interface.
III. What Is Kept: The Pole of the Contact Surfaces

K, pads Ci = (ℓi , hi , di ), actuation A and a structure S
that supports each pad over its full length. A pad has
length ℓi , thickness hi and mount depth di , with i = 1
proximal and i = 2 distal. Pad thickness and width are
requirements. We study a planar two-DoF finger with the
MCP held at 0. At q = 0 the phalanx axes are collinear,
and the finger length is Lf = L1 + Lvp + L2 , where

IV. Keeping the Contact-Surface Motion by Design
A. Architectures and setup
We compare four PIP joints. 2R-DD is a revolute joint
with the actuator at the joint. 2R-remote is the same joint,
belt-driven from an actuator on the proximal phalanx.
RCJ is a rolling contact joint of two equal rollers [9]. CFL
is CoDe’s crossed four-bar [2], driven through its proximal
pivot. All share one goal: Lf = 90 mm, the same actuators,
pad requirements and collision rules. CFL ideal and RCJ
ideal omit the joint actuator’s body and serve as upper
bounds. Mechanical advantage is not constrained.
Pads are 3, 5, 7 or 9 mm thick, and qreq ∈
{90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ }. Study 1 maximizes C̄pad over all design
parameters with a genetic algorithm and NSGA-II [7],
three seeds each, and refines CFL from neighbouring
designs. On 51 designs the pole-based coverage matches
the evaluator within 10−13 ; on the CFL designs Eq. (4)
gives the joint gap within 0.05 mm (median).
B. The CFL keeps more contact surface (Study 1)
Fig. 3 shows the best coverage per condition. CFL
exceeds 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both have
a design, by 2.4 to 14.6 points, and also without the
refinement. From 3 to 9 mm, CFL loses 0.085–0.096 of
C̄pad . 2R-remote loses 0.182 at 120◦ , and at 150◦ its 7
and 9 mm designs cannot hold a cylinder. 2R-remote, a
belt-driven fixed axis, leads at 90◦ , the two cross between 5
and 7 mm at 120◦ , and CFL leads at 150◦ . Eq. (5) explains
the split: CFL’s palmar pole lengthens the contour, while
2R-remote’s gap shortens it when flexed. 2R-remote pays
instead in contact-surface motion, which it changes by the
full bound (Sec. IV-C).
C. The CFL keeps its contact-surface motion (Study 2)
From each architecture’s 3 mm Study 1 design M0 , we
minimize ∆contact and maximize C̄pad for 5, 7 and 9 mm
pads with NSGA-II. We report the coverage end of each
front, which gives up no coverage (Fig. 4). CFL and 2Rremote keep their mount depth, so the dotted line is
Eq. (8) with the faces moved by ∆h. 2R-DD falls below
it because its servo body already holds its proximal face
deep, so a thicker pad barely moves that face; it pays
in coverage. RCJ changes its roller radius, which moves
its pole. Spacers keep ∆contact = 0 when every interface
is known in advance: the finger is built for the thickest
pad, and thinner pads lose 8.6–18.2 points of coverage.
We study the redesign a new interface forces.
2R-remote meets the bound exactly at 90◦ and 120◦ .
CFL changes the contact-surface motion least among the
physical joints in every cell: by 0.35–4.31 mm, or 10–51%
of the bound. Without the start offset of 2R-DD and RCJ

(Fig. 4d), the CFL still changes the motion least in 7
of 9 cells; the two exceptions cost 2.4 and 17.8 points of
coverage. Its C̄pad stays within 0.2 points of Study 1’s
best. The other joints cannot close the gap by trading
coverage: giving up 2 points along their fronts, 2R-DD
and RCJ stay above the CFL in every cell where they
have a design. CFL absorbs the thicker pad by changing
its skeleton motion and its transmission, which control
compensates.
V. Why the Contact-Surface Motion Matters
Why keep the contact-surface motion, and not another
property of the hand? Interface studies compare hands
under one protocol, and many reuse one set of joint
commands or torques, as scripted grasps, joint-space
policies and current-controlled servos do. We study these
two protocols. We reuse the flexion command through each
hand’s own transmission table, which undoes the transmission change (Sec. III), and test five candidates a designer
might hold: skeleton motion, coverage, transmission, pad
thickness and contact-surface motion.
Setup. We simulate the CoDe hand model’s three CFL
fingers (qreq = 120◦ , MCP held) in Isaac Lab 3 with
Newton’s MuJoCo-Warp solver, at one flexion stiffness,
K0 = 5 N m/rad. A cylinder of radius 15 or 20 mm rests
on each hand’s proximal pad, free in the sagittal plane,
at the place along the face where the reference O (3 mm
pads) touches it with both pads at q0 (q0 = 20◦ to 120◦
in 5◦ steps). The PIP closes to q0 + 5◦ , the cylinder is
released, and a load rises in one of four directions until
the cylinder slips (10 mm/s), loses a pad or collapses
the grasp. Three interface changes are measured: pad
friction (µ from 1.3 to 2.0), pad material stiffness (contact
stiffness from 2.5 to 1.0 kN/m) and pad shape (flat to
2 mm crowned; ρ = 0.94 with ∆contact over six hands).
For a hand H, ∆(H) is the change of the limit load, and
the error is E(H) = |∆(H) − ∆(O)|/|∆(O)|, the median
over placements and directions. Pads of every thickness
share one contact model, so a hand that held the cylinder
as O does would show O’s effect: E is what the redesign
adds. All thresholds were fixed before the runs. The 18
redesigns for 3 → 5, 7 and 9 mm include, per thickness,
R, which keeps the contact-surface motion, U , redesigned
for coverage alone, and redesigns at set ∆contact that keep
or change the skeleton as much as possible.
The contact-surface motion is the consistent predictor.
Over the redesigns that hold the cylinder, the error follows
∆contact for both pad friction and pad stiffness (Table I,
Fig. 5a; friction [0.41, 1.00]). It is the only candidate whose
interval excludes zero in every setting, and its four p-values
(≤ 0.0006) survive Holm correction over all 20 tests. It
holds at each thickness and with ∆skel fixed (partial 0.88).
R errs least at each thickness: 3, 21 and 41% at 5, 7 and
9 mm, against 52–124% for U .

joint type, a revolute PIP at the centre of O’s joint gap,

ratio ×0.3 or ×3, or doubled substeps.
VI. Conclusion and Next Stage
A redesigned finger should keep how its contact surfaces
move, not its skeleton; a crossed four-bar does so at nearbest contact surface.
Stage 2 protocol. One CoDe finger keeps its servos and
bodies; swapping the pad modules and CFL links gives
O (3 mm), R and U (7 mm). C5: we record the parts,
time and recalibration needed. C3: markers on both faces,
tracked over repeated slow sweeps, give ∆contact and its
tolerance. C4: the reused command closes the finger on a
cylinder on a planar slide, which is pulled in four directions
until slip, with bare and textured pad covers (5 placements
× 4 directions × 5 repeats per hand and cover). Real 3 and
7 mm pads differ in compliance, so the test is R against U ,
which carry the same pads. C4 fails if R errs no less than
U : pad compliance or friction scatter would then outweigh
the geometry.
Limitations and future work. Our evidence is computational, with pads represented by a contact model.
Extending the principle to the MCP calls for a polycentric
MCP, and thicker camera-based tactile modules are the
next interfaces.
