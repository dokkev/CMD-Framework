THOUGHT:
I read this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methodology reviewer. The paper makes two claims: (C1) a remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint leaves more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger, and (C2) a redesign that carries a new interface should preserve the contact-surface motion c(q) ("contact Jacobian") rather than the skeleton, because the error in a measured interface effect tracks Delta_contact when an impedance setpoint is reused. For a Stage 1 abstract the problem, hypotheses (H1-H3, each with a stated failure condition), methodology and a Stage 2 hardware protocol with predicted numbers are all present, which is more than the format requires. The kinematics (finite pole, Eq. 2, 6, 7, 8) look correct to me: Eq. 6 holds pointwise since Delta c = (I - R_q) Delta p, and the fixed-axis bound follows.

My concerns are about whether Study 3 tests what it claims.

(1) Mechanism vs. predictor. The "true" effect is a counterfactual in which O keeps its geometry and only the pad stiffness/friction parameter changes. So pad thickness enters the result only through where the faces sit at a reused position target. Under impedance control at q0+5 deg with stiffness K0, a face that is nearer the object simply produces more preload. The authors themselves report that the direction of the error follows the signed normal offset of the distal face with rho = -0.99 (post hoc), and that their own prediction (reversed share rises with Delta_contact) failed. That signed normal offset at the contact point is a simpler, one-dimensional quantity than Delta_contact (an unsigned max over q of a point displacement), and it is not among the five candidates in Table I. The paper needs to show Delta_contact adds anything beyond "preload changed because the face moved along its normal at the grasp pose". The torque-protocol column is offered as that control, but there Delta_contact (0.69) is not better than the skeleton (0.71), and under the touch protocol the skeleton wins (0.88 vs 0.44). So the principle is specific to the reused-position-setpoint protocol; the title and C2 read as general.

(2) The proposed design does not meet the paper's own tolerance. The tolerance for E<20% is Delta_contact below about 1 mm (0.35-0.96 mm by setting). The CFL redesign R for 7 mm is at 0.9 mm and errs 21-25%; for 9 mm the CFL is at about 2.3 mm (Fig. 5 dotted line), where the scatter gives roughly 50-100% error, and Fig. 1(d) at 9 mm shows R reporting roughly -15% against a true value near -50%. "Lets the pad be studied on its own" is therefore true only up to about 5-7 mm; beyond that CFL is less wrong, not right. Spacers (Delta_contact = 0) are the only exact option and are dismissed in one sentence.

(3) Contact model fidelity. Pads are a lumped compliant-contact model in MuJoCo-Warp (stiffness in kN/m, elliptic cone, mu 1.3-2.0). Thickness and stiffness are independent parameters there, contact patch growth/conformity and thickness-dependent shear compliance are absent, and those are exactly what a thick soft pad changes. The paper says "pads share one contact model, so E is what the redesign adds", which is honest, but it means Study 3 measures a geometric preload artefact of a rigid-body simulator, not an interface effect in a continuum sense. No sensitivity to solver parameters (time step, contact softness, cone type, load ramp rate), and robustness is one sentence on K0.

(4) Statistics. Units are "hands", but the 18 redesigns are clustered by thickness and architecture and the 27 grid hands are all 7 mm and built on a grid of placements, so they are not exchangeable; a hand bootstrap and permutation test treat them as independent. Hands that do not hold are dropped (15/18, 14/18) and E is a median only over placements/directions "where both hands hold" - a survivorship filter correlated with the predictor. E is normalised by |Delta(O)|, so it inflates where O's effect is small; the load step is 2.5-5.6% of that effect. 25 one-sided tests without a multiplicity statement. Redesigns were selected "at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton most", which is good for decorrelation but Eq. 7 couples Delta_contact and Delta_skel at fixed thickness, so the negative skeleton correlations (-0.40, -0.51) are partly by construction. "4 places x 7 targets per radius adds 27" is not explained (28? minus one?).

(5) H3's failure criterion is ambiguous: "fails if Delta_contact misses in any setting" - it does miss under touch (0.44, not bold). The authors apparently mean the three reused-setpoint settings; say so.

(6) Study 1: three seeds, mechanical advantage unconstrained, so CFL optima sit at MA 0.41-0.58 vs 1 for the baseline. Coverage is a geometric proxy; no evidence that 2.4-14.6 points of coverage changes any grasp outcome. Fine for Stage 1 but H1 is a comparison of optimiser outputs under an objective that favours the proposed design.

(7) Stage 2 protocol: one finger, three redesigns, friction covers only. A Page trend test over three ordered conditions has very little resolving power and the blocking unit is not stated; covers add thickness themselves; no stated accuracy for marker-based Delta_contact against a 0.9 mm level.

Positives: explicit falsifiable hypotheses, an admitted failed prediction, a pre-registered grid, an ablation separating remote actuation from the polycentric joint, closed-form bounds checked numerically, and candidate comparisons under alternative protocols rather than only the favourable one. Presentation is very dense; many numbers per sentence and several terms (grid, places, targets, "reversed share") defined only implicitly.

Overall: sound kinematics and an interesting, useful methodological point for interface studies, with a simulation study that is more careful than most but whose central statistic may be standing in for a simpler preload variable and whose proposed joint only partly delivers the claimed independence. For a Stage 1 abstract I lean accept, at 6.
