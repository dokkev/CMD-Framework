ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper makes three linked claims about a servo-driven finger whose PIP joint is a crossed four-bar linkage (CFL) driven by a servo on the proximal phalanx.

(1) Room. A remote-actuated CFL PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. The support is an optimization study: 4 thicknesses x 3 flexion limits, a GA/NSGA-II with three seeds, and each joint compared at its own best design. The CFL wins all 10 conditions where both joints have a feasible design, by 2.4-14.6 points. Two closed-form results back this up: a clearance law (Prop. 1) and a coverage law (Prop. 2), both under symmetry assumptions S1-S2. The evidence is adequate and the result is plausible, but it is also fairly expected: taking the actuator and pin away from the joint frees space. The more interesting part is the analysis of where the pole sits (the best joint turns about a point on the contact face). The comparison with 2R-remote shows that much of the gain at 90 deg comes from remote actuation alone.

(2) Invariant. A redesign for a thicker interface should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), which is the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, rather than the skeleton or fingertip path. A fixed axis cannot do this: there is a lower bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt| (Cor. 1), and Eq. (8) shows that no redesign keeps both motions. I checked the math and it is clean. Lemma 1 is classical. Prop. 3 follows from (I - R_q)Δp with |I - R_q| = 2 sin(q/2), and Eq. (8) is a triangle inequality on the depth shift. The CFL reaches 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound while losing less than 0.2 points of coverage. This is the most original and transferable idea in the paper. However, the paper switches between the finite pole P(q) (Lemma 1, Prop. 3) and the instantaneous centre p̂ (the Jacobian column) and does not say clearly that they are different objects. Also, the "keep the contact Jacobian" framing is stronger than what is shown: Δcontact is a max positional deviation of one point. It is not a measure of the Jacobian column over the face.

(3) Experiment. The experiment reuses an impedance setpoint across redesigns. Under that protocol, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect (pull-out load change under friction or stiffness changes) follows Δcontact (Spearman 0.80-0.86, Holm-corrected, partly pre-registered). It does not follow Δskel, coverage, thickness or transmission. The result is replicated in Drake with point and hydroelastic contact and in two more open-loop tasks. A worked example shows a sign reversal: +48% reported vs -41% true. The care taken here is unusually high for a 5-page abstract: pre-registration, bootstrap intervals, partial correlations, and a second simulator. My main concerns follow.
 (a) The mechanism the authors identify is preload. A face that sits elsewhere changes how hard a reused position/stiffness target presses, so E ≈ |N_H/N_O - 1|. That is almost tautological once stated. Δcontact includes the start offset, so part of the correlation may simply be "initial face displacement sets preload".
 (b) The finding depends on the protocol. With re-zeroing at touch or an ideal torque, the skeleton becomes the predictor. With force matching, the error falls to 3-7%. So the practical recommendation is conditional on a protocol that experimenters could fix in other ways. The paper should say up front why the reused-setpoint protocol is the one that matters (the scoping to servo-hand setpoints in the intro and Sec. IV-B4 is brief).
 (c) The sample sizes are small (14-18 hands per setting). The hands come from optimizers deliberately chosen to span Δcontact and Δskel, and correlations over them depend on that sampling. Δskel's negative ρ under friction and stiffness hints at structure in how the set was built. The paper itself notes that the lead over Δskel is not significant at 14-16 hands.
 (d) "Near 20% up to 7 mm" is still a large confound for an interface study, and it rises to 41% at 9 mm.
 (e) Everything is computational. Stage 2 is a well-specified pre-registered plan, but there is no hardware data yet. That is acceptable for this venue but caps significance.

Clarity is the weakest dimension. The prose is extremely compressed. Several terms are used before they are defined or never fully defined: O/R/U/M, "goal", "mounting rule", "front", and the "torque"/"touch" protocols in Table I. The abstract packs in numbers from three studies. Fig. 2c (3D surface) and Fig. 4b (many overlapping marker types on a log axis) are hard to read at print size. The section heading "Stage 2" under the Conclusion is unexplained. A reader outside linkage kinematics will struggle to follow the argument from pole to coverage to Jacobian to preload.

My scores rest on these points. The work is original in reframing the invariant (C2), with a sound bound and a carefully controlled simulation study. Contribution 1 is less novel. The empirical claim is real but protocol-conditional and preload-mediated, and there is no physical validation. Presentation density reduces accessibility. For an extended abstract where preliminary experiments are allowed, the hypotheses and methods are complete and the evidence for the main claims is sufficient, so I lean accept.
