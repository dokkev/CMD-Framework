ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology (does each experiment test what it claims, and are the statistics adequate).

Central claims and the evidence for each.

(1) A remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. The evidence is a planar-model optimization: GA/NSGA-II, three seeds per optimizer, 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions, with an ablation (2R-remote, RCJ, idealized bounds). The paper states a falsification condition before the result ("one condition in which 2R-DD matched it would contradict this"), and the robustness statement that CFL's worst of six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9/10 conditions is the right kind of check. The pole/clearance analysis (Prop. 1-2) explains the trend, and the stated agreement of Eq. (3) with exact clearance (0.05 mm median) is useful. Weaknesses: coverage is a geometric proxy (the length of pad facing a partner across an inscribed cylinder at three poses). Nothing in the paper shows that this proxy predicts grasp outcomes. The result also leans on a mounting rule ("shallowest depth clear of joint hardware, no spacers") that the authors chose, and that rule is what penalizes the fixed axes. Within this model I find the claim well supported. Its practical meaning depends on the proxy.

(2) A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion; the closed-form bound is Eq. (9), and the CFL redesign reaches 10-51% of it at near-best coverage. The kinematics (Lemma 1, Prop. 3, Eq. (7)-(8)) are classical and correct as stated. Applying them to redesign under a pad-depth change is neat. The metric is a concern. Delta_contact is the max over [0, q_req] of the displacement of a single point c(q), "below the fingertip", and assumes c0 is kept. For 2R-DD and RCJ, c0 is not kept: Fig. 4d shows offsets of 5-9 mm at q = 0, so their Delta_contact mixes a start-point shift with the motion change. The authors acknowledge this ("7 of 9 cells" without the offset). In the experiments the cylinder touches the distal face at a placement-dependent point, not at c. The paper never shows that the one-point, max-over-q summary is the right statistic, rather than, say, the face-pose deviation at the setpoint q0 over the actual contact patch.

(3) Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact (rho 0.80-0.86), and keeping the skeleton does not help. This is the central empirical claim and it needs the closest look. The protocol commands each hand to q0 + 5 deg at K0 = 5 N m/rad, so the pre-load comes from over-closure in joint space. If a redesign moves the distal face, the face meets the cylinder earlier or later, which changes the normal force. Under Coulomb point contact the friction-limited holding load scales with that normal force. So a friction-change effect Delta(H) scales with N_H, and E roughly reduces to |N_H - N_O| / N_O. The paper's own numbers fit this reading: R presses 0.75x as hard as O and U 3.3x, and U's error is 124-254%. The finding is therefore largely mechanistic and almost expected: Delta_contact predicts setpoint-force mismatch, and the force mismatch drives E. The post hoc partial correlation with setpoint force held (0.64) is the right control and partly answers this, but it is post hoc, and the paper does not say how "setpoint force" is defined (per pad, sum, normal only). I would like the force-mediated and the residual (kinematic) parts separated in a pre-specified analysis. The "matching contact force leaves 3-7%" result points the same way: most of the effect is force. That is still a useful message for practitioners. It also means the contact Jacobian matters here mainly through the pre-load it implies, which is narrower than "the contact Jacobian is what matters".

Statistics. The design of the analysis is better than usual: one-sided permutation tests, a hand-level bootstrap, Holm correction for the primary candidate, and pre-registration claims. Several issues remain.
(a) "More than any of four other design quantities" is a comparative claim. A CI excluding zero for one candidate and not the others does not establish it. A paired bootstrap of rho differences (or Steiger's test) is needed. Under the torque protocol, Delta_contact (0.69) and Delta_skel (0.71) are indistinguishable.
(b) The hands are constructed, not sampled. Redesigns were chosen "at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton most", so the spread and the decorrelation of the predictors come from the authors' choices, and rho depends on that design. With n = 14-18 the intervals will be wide; they should be reported, not only bolded.
(c) E is a ratio with |Delta(O)| in the denominator, and the median is taken "where both hands hold". Directions where O's effect is small inflate E. Dropping placements where a redesign fails to hold is selection that may favour poor redesigns. Hold rates per hand should be reported.
(d) Pre-registration cannot be checked from the paper. Many headline details are labelled post hoc: the partial correlation, the face-normal-offset sign explanation, and "reverses at up to 40% of placements". Saying what was registered, and when relative to the earlier experiments, would strengthen the paper.
(e) The "Hands" row mixes 15, 14, 45, 27 and 18, and it is unclear why the counts differ (failures to hold? different subsets?).

Contact simulation. Point contact in Isaac Lab "with Newton and MuJoCo-Warp" is ambiguous: one solver, or both with agreement? Pad "stiffness 2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m" under point contact presumably means a penalty/solref parameter. The paper should say so, together with the friction model (pyramidal vs elliptic cones, regularization). Soft-constraint solvers allow creep under tangential load, which interacts with normal force and could itself make E scale with pre-load. In the Drake check, the hydroelastic layer is "0.1 MPa over 3 mm on every hand". So a thicker pad is not modelled as a thicker compliant layer, and the compliance difference that is the interface's physical effect is absent, while pad thickness enters only through face depth. The "softer, thicker pad" case study (48% more vs 41% less) is therefore a statement about geometry and pre-load under a stiffness parameter change, not about real thick-pad mechanics (distributed pressure, large deformation, change in effective friction with area). The cross-model rank agreement of 0.39 matters. It means the two contact models disagree about which redesigns err most, so the quantitative Stage 2 predictions (21/72/124%) are fragile. The pre-registered ordinal Page trend test is the appropriate response, and I credit it.

Falsification plan (Stage 2). It is concrete: O, R, M, U on one finger with link swaps; Delta_contact is measured with markers including backlash and pad compression; there are 5 placements x 4 directions x 5 repeats and a one-sided Page test, and the refuting outcome is stated. Missing pieces: a power argument (only three ordered levels, and real friction variance between covers can be tens of percent); how cover wear and friction drift across repeats will be controlled (randomized order, re-measuring O); and a definition of M, which appears only here. Measuring the pre-load at the setpoint on hardware should also be pre-specified, so that the force-mediated pathway can be tested directly.

Scores. Claims (1) and (2) are well supported within the planar model. Claim (3) is supported as a correlation and is replicated across simulators and two of three extra tasks; the negative rolling result is reported honestly. Its interpretation (contact Jacobian vs pre-load mismatch) and the comparative claim over other quantities need tighter analysis. The presentation is dense and terse, with undefined symbols (M), an ambiguous simulator setup and a placeholder Fig. 1. For a Stage 1 submission the hypotheses, methods and falsification plan are laid out well enough that I lean towards acceptance.
