CoDe Hand: Same Hand, Different Contact — Keep
How the Contact Surfaces Move, Not the Skeleton,
for Comparable Contact-Interface Studies
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—Contact interfaces such as thick compliant pads
and tactile modules decide how a robot hand touches an
object. To compare two interfaces, the finger must usually
be redesigned, because servos and joints occupy the space an
interface needs. The redesign changes the hand, so a measured
difference cannot be attributed to the interface alone. We
propose that a redesigned finger keep how its contact surfaces
move relative to each other, not its skeleton. In the proximalphalanx frame this motion is one column of the finger’s contact
Jacobian, and a single point sets it: the pole about which the
distal phalanx turns. We prove that a fixed-axis joint cannot
keep it when thicker pads are mounted at their own depth,
and give a closed-form lower bound. A remote-actuated crossed
four-bar finger keeps it within 10–51% of that bound at nearbest contact surface. In simulation, across 15 redesigns for 5, 7
and 9 mm pads, the error in a measured friction effect follows
the change in contact-surface motion (Spearman ρ = 0.83).
The change in skeleton motion does not predict it. Exchanging
interfaces on one physical hand is the next stage of this work.

I. Introduction
Contact is central to dexterous manipulation, and the
contact interface sets that contact. Compliant fingertips
change how an object is held [17, 12]. Vision-based
tactile sensors turn the contact patch into geometry and
force [20, 11]. Robot hands have become experimental
platforms [15, 16, 6], and most are built around servos
for maintainability. Servo bodies and joints occupy the
space an interface needs, above all around the joint. A
large or thick interface therefore costs range of motion or
contact surface, and carrying it is a redesign, not a module
swap [21, 4].
The redesign changes how the contact surfaces move
relative to each other. For a joint that turns about a
fixed axis this change is unavoidable when the pads sit
at their own depth. Interface studies often reuse the same
commands across hands: scripted grasps, frozen policies,
the same actuator targets. A measured difference then
mixes the interface with the hand’s change. One could
keep the skeleton instead, but the unchanged finger cannot
carry a thicker pad, and the object does not meet the
skeleton. It meets the contact surfaces.
In this work we propose CoDe (contact-centric design),
a servo-driven hand whose redesign target is how its
contact surfaces move relative to each other. Its servos
act remotely through a linkage, which frees the space
under the joints. Its PIP joint is a crossed four-bar linkage
(CFL), whose pole moves with flexion, so a thick pad

can sit under the joint. When a new interface asks for
a redesign, the finger is re-optimized to keep its contactsurface motion, not its skeleton motion (Fig. 1). This paper
analyses one planar finger of that hand. Our contributions
are:
C1 A bound. Finite-pole laws for the clearance, coverage
and contact-surface motion of any planar finger joint.
A fixed axis cannot keep its contact-surface motion
when the pads thicken, with a closed-form lower
bound (Sec. III).
C2 A finger. The CFL keeps more contact surface than a
servo-at-joint finger as pads thicken and the required
flexion deepens (Sec. IV-B).
C3 A redesign that keeps contact. Re-optimized for
thicker pads, the CFL keeps its contact-surface motion well inside the fixed-axis bound at near-best
coverage (Sec. IV-C).
C4 Why it matters. In simulation, with reused flexion
commands, the error in a measured interface effect
follows the change in contact-surface motion, not the
change in skeleton motion. The dependence weakens
as the actuation softens (Sec. V).
Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand (C5) is the
next stage. We do not claim a better hand or a best interface. Keeping the contact-surface motion does not keep
the contact mechanics: those change with the interface,
and they are what an interface study measures.
II. Related Work
Contact interfaces. Vision-based tactile sensors need
an elastomer, a camera and optics behind the contact
surface [20, 11]. Fingers are therefore designed around
them: GelSight Svelte shapes a finger around one camera [21], and a hand has been built to carry large-area
tactile sensing [4]. Thin skins can be replaced without
redesign [3]. Fingertip materials have been compared [17]
and modelled [12]. Thick interfaces come with their own
finger, so comparing them compares fingers.
Finger joints. Open, low-cost hands use fixed-axis servo
joints [16, 6]. Rolling contact joints are compact [9, 18].
Crossed four-bar joints give polycentric finger motion [2,
1], and linkage-driven hands integrate the drive [10].
Prosthetic knees place a four-bar’s instantaneous centre
for stability [14]. None of these is analysed for what it
takes from the pads, or for whether its contact-surface

III. What Is Kept: The Pole of the Contact Surfaces
A. Finger model
A finger morphology M = (K, C, A, S) has a mechanism
K, pads Ci = (ℓi , hi , di ), actuation A and a structure
S that supports each pad over its full length. A pad
has length ℓi , thickness hi and mount depth di , with
i = 1 proximal and i = 2 distal. Pad dimensions are
requirements. We study a planar two-DoF finger with the
MCP held at 0. At q = 0 the phalanx axes are collinear,
and the finger length is Lf = L1 + Lvp + L2 , where
Lvp is the vacant length between the phalanges. Each
pad is mounted at the shallowest depth clear of its joint
hardware. Spacers are excluded (Sec. IV-C). A design is
feasible if it assembles without overlap and is collisionfree for q ∈ [0, qreq ]. A four-bar must keep its transmission
angle in [30◦ , 150◦ ].

IV. Keeping the Contact-Surface Motion by Design

We compare four PIP joints. 2R-DD is a revolute joint
with the actuator at the joint. 2R-remote is the same joint,
belt-driven from an actuator on the proximal phalanx.
RCJ is a rolling contact joint of two equal rollers [9]. CFL
is CoDe’s crossed four-bar [2], driven through its proximal
pivot. All share one goal: Lf = 90 mm, the same actuators,
pad requirements and collision rules. CFL ideal and RCJ
ideal omit the joint actuator’s body and serve as upper
bounds. We report the worst-case mechanical advantage
MAwc and do not constrain it.

V. Why the Contact-Surface Motion Matters
Studies 1 and 2 show that the contact-surface motion
can be kept. We now ask why it should be kept. That
a redesign disturbs a measurement made with reused

commands is expected. The question is which change
predicts the disturbance: the skeleton motion, which a
designer would naturally hold, or the contact-surface
motion.
Setup. We simulate the CoDe hand model’s three fingers
in Isaac Lab 3 with Newton and MuJoCo-Warp, using
rigid contact. The fingers use CFL designs with the MCP
held. Flexion is commanded through each hand’s transmission table at one flexion stiffness, K0 = 5 N m/rad.
A cylinder of radius 15 or 20 mm moves freely in the
sagittal plane. It is placed where the reference O (3 mm
pads) touches it with both pads at q0 . The PIP closes
to q0 + 5◦ , the cylinder is released, and a load rises in
one of four directions until the grasp gives way. The
interface change is the pads’ friction, µ from 1.3 to
2.0; on O it raises the limit load by 1.2–2.7 N. For a
hand H, ∆(H) is the change of the limit load, and the
error is E(H) = |∆(H) − ∆(O)|/|∆(O)|, the median over
placements and directions. All gates were fixed before the
runs. The hands are redesigns for 3 → 5, 7 and 9 mm, 18 in
all. Each thickness has R, which keeps the contact-surface
motion, U , which is redesigned for coverage alone, and
redesigns chosen on design metrics so that ∆contact , ∆skel
and C̄pad vary apart. The unchanged finger with thicker
pads collides at 47◦ and cannot be tested.
Reused flexion command. Of the 18 redesigns, 15 hold
the cylinder. Over them E follows ∆contact (Spearman
ρ = 0.83, p = 2 × 10−4 ; Fig. 1d). It does so at each
thickness, and the partial correlation given ∆skel is 0.88.
∆skel does not predict E (ρ = −0.40, p = 0.14), nor does
it given ∆contact (partial −0.18 on a symmetric log error).
Thickness itself adds little: its standardized coeﬀicient is
+0.08. R errs least at each thickness: 3, 21 and 41% at
5, 7 and 9 mm, against 52–124% for U . A redesign can
change its skeleton substantially and still measure like the
original: R moved its skeleton by 7.7 mm and errs 21%,
while a 7 mm hand that moved it by 3.8 mm errs 92%.
The error enters through the contact. At the same
command, U presses 3.3× harder than O (median). The
three redesigns that cannot hold the cylinder are those
whose distal face retreats 1.5–2.6 mm from it. Ranking
them worst keeps ρ = 0.81. The face’s offset at the grasp,
a local measure that ∆contact bounds, predicts E as well
(ρ = 0.92).
Other protocols. Reusing O’s flexion torque instead,
every hand presses within 0.89–1.26× O’s force. E falls
to 3–40% and remains ordered by ∆contact (ρ = 0.75;
Fig. 5a). Matching the distal force with the simulator’s
contact force, an upper bound on compensation, leaves
3–7%. Across a flexion impedance (Fig. 5b), R stays at
14–26%. U rises from 44% to 131% up to K0 and then
levels off, where the contact’s compliance takes up the
interference. The contact-surface motion therefore matters
most when position or impedance commands are reused,
and contact-force feedback removes most of the need.
Robustness. R errs less than U pair by pair under a

tenfold change of contact stiffness, the solver’s impedance
ratio scaled by 0.3 and 3, and doubled substeps (Wilcoxon
p ≤ 3 × 10−4 ). U ’s error moves between 127 and 184%.
A second interface property, pad shape (flat to 2 mm
crowned), repeats the order: over six redesigns its error
follows ∆contact (ρ = 0.94, exact one-sided p = 0.008). Few
placements hold under the crowned pad, and in this set
∆skel falls as ∆contact rises, so this test repeats the order
but does not by itself separate the two.
VI. Limitations and Next Stage
We proposed that a finger redesigned for a new contact
interface keep how its contact surfaces move, not its
skeleton. A fixed axis cannot do this, a crossed fourbar can, and in simulation the change in contact-surface
motion, not in skeleton motion, predicts the error in a
measured interface effect.
The evidence has limits. The analysis covers one planar
finger with equal-thickness pads, cylinder coverage and
no occlusion. Study 3 is in simulation with rigid contact,
one mechanism, one task and one interface property. Pads
of 3–9 mm stop short of camera-based tactile modules.
The MCP column of the contact Jacobian is not kept: a
revolute MCP is a fixed axis, so C1 applies there. Keeping
the contact-surface motion reduces confounding; it does
not remove it.
The next stage is the physical hand (C5). Two to
three interfaces, such as compliant pads [17] and a tactile
module [20], will be exchanged on one CoDe hand without disassembling the articulation, with the tracked face
motion checked against the model.
