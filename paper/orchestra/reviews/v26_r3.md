ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Perspective: mechanism kinematics. I re-derived every result in Sec. III and cross-checked the numbers against the studies.

Central claims and how well they are supported:

C1 (a remote-actuated crossed four-bar PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint PIP at every pad thickness and flexion). The theory is correct, and Study 1 supports the claim well.
- Lemma 1 is correct. (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) R_{pi/2}) follows from det(I - R_q) = 2(1 - cos q).
- Prop. 1 is correct under S1 and S2. With the pole as the centre, the rotated distal pad is the reflection of the proximal pad in the line with normal (cos q/2, -sin q/2). A convex set meets its mirror image iff it crosses the mirror line, since any crossing point is fixed by the reflection. The sign convention gives the per-side gap (t - z) tan(q/2).
- Prop. 2 is correct. It implicitly sets G = L_vp ("joint-side ends meet the phalanx ends"). The phalanx-end separation G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2) is twice the distance of the end point from the mirror line. That is right.
- The pole-lowering argument is right. Lowering the pole by delta adds 2 delta tan(q_req/2) to both the numerator and the denominator at q_req. I also checked that at the other evaluation poses (q < q_req) the denominator grows less, by sin((q - q_req)/2)/cos(q_req/2) < 0. So the monotonicity holds at all of Q_eval, which is slightly stronger than what the paper states.
- Fig. 2 is internally consistent: 5 tan 60° = 8.7 mm, 9 tan 60° = 15.6 mm, 4 tan 60° = 6.9 mm, and the pad lengths 32/25/34 mm follow. The "nearly four times faster" claim checks out: tan 75°/tan 45° = 3.73.
- Study 1 is a clean test of H1, with a falsifiable criterion, an ablation (2R-remote) that separates remote actuation from polycentricity, and seed robustness (worst CFL run beats best 2R-DD in 9 of 10 conditions).
- The main caveat is that mechanical advantage is left free. The CFL's room is partly bought with MA 0.41–0.58. The authors say so, but a coverage-vs-MA front would make C1 more convincing than the argument that "a floor would pick the winner".

C2 (keep the contact-surface motion c(q), not the skeleton). The kinematic part is correct and elegant.
- Prop. 3 and Eq. (6) are correct. Δc = (I - R_q)Δp and |(I - R_q)v| = 2 sin(q/2)|v|.
- Eq. (7) is correct. The pole coordinates in the face frame and in the link-line frame differ by the proximal face depth, so the two pole shifts differ by Δt, and the triangle inequality gives the bound.
- Corollary 1 is correct. For a fixed axis Δp is constant, so the max over q ≤ q_req is attained at q_req. Equality therefore holds when the axis does not slide along the face.
- The RCJ claim is correct. I derived the finite pole of two equal rollers: it sits at x = r on the link line, offset by r tan(q/4). So a fixed r fixes the pole path relative to the link line, and the same bound applies.
- The reported numbers obey Eq. (7). At 120° with Δt = 4 mm the bound is 6.93 mm. R gives 0.9 + 7.7 = 8.6 mm, M gives 2.3 + 4.6 = 6.9 mm (essentially at the bound) and U gives 3.9 + 3.4 = 7.3 mm. This consistency raises my confidence that the simulation and the theory agree.

Does Study 2 test what H2 states? Yes.
- The comparison is conservative: it takes the coverage end of each front, against the c0-kept bound and against a numerically found c0-free floor (0.59–0.79 of the bound). The CFL is below both in every cell.
- The c0-free floor is only numerical. A closed-form version, or at least a statement of what is optimized, would strengthen the "a fixed axis cannot keep it" claim.
- The plotted bound uses Δh with Δd = 0. This is justified by "mounted at the shallowest depth clear of joint hardware", but it makes the fixed-axis disadvantage depend on that packaging rule. A reader may ask whether a smaller pulley or a recessed pad seat would reach Δd > 0 cheaply.

Does Study 3 test what H3 states? Mostly yes.
- The design is better than a plain correlation. The authors construct redesigns at a set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton, bootstrap within thickness, condition on Δskel and on setpoint force, pre-register the main tests and label the post-hoc ones.
- Under a reused position/impedance setpoint, Δcontact (0.80–0.86) is the only candidate whose interval excludes zero, and Δskel = 0 fails badly (95–228%). That result is the paper's most useful practical message.
- My concerns are the following.
  (a) The result is close to mechanistically expected. Under position control, the squeeze is set by how far the faces over-close at q0 + 5°, and that is exactly what c(q) near the grasp pose measures. The partial correlation given setpoint force (0.64) helps, but the strongest single predictor is the face's normal offset at the grasped pose (0.69). That variable is a local, pose-specific version of the same quantity.
  (b) Δcontact is a C0 (path) distance, while the title and C2 speak of keeping the contact Jacobian. J's column is R_{pi/2}(c - p̂(q)), which depends on the instantaneous centre p̂. A small path distance does not bound the column difference: Δc' = R_{pi/2}(Δc - Δp̂). Exactly keeping c(q) keeps J, as stated. But the quantitative claims ("keeps the change within 10–51%", "below about 1 mm") concern path distance, not the Jacobian. A C1 metric (max_q |Δc'(q)|) would match the name.
  (c) Only the PIP column in F_p is considered, and the MCP column is explicitly not kept. With the MCP held in all studies this is consistent, but "the contact Jacobian" in the title overstates the scope.
  (d) Under the other protocols the conclusion changes. Under the touch protocol, Δskel predicts better (0.88 vs 0.44). The paper says this, and the abstract scopes C2 to reused impedance setpoints, but the scope should stay explicit wherever "keeping the skeleton does not help" appears.
  (e) The "true" interface effect is defined on O with only the stiffness changed. This treats the geometric consequence of a thicker pad as belonging to the redesign rather than to the interface. That is a defensible choice, but it should be stated as a choice.
  (f) One contact model is used (point contact, linear normal stiffness). The authors do check robustness across stiffness ×10, friction impedance ×3 and doubled substeps.

Stage-1 fit:
- Problem, hypotheses with failure criteria, and methodology are fully laid out.
- The Stage-2 hardware protocol is concrete: R, M and U at 7 mm span Δcontact 0.9–3.9 mm, with a Page trend test and predicted values.
- A negative result (the reversed share does not rise with Δcontact) is reported honestly.

Presentation: the mathematics is compact and correct, but the prose is compressed to the point of opacity in places. Examples are "Spacers keep Δcontact = 0 ... at 8.6–18.2 points of coverage", the G vs G_sym roles, and S2 when the pole moves in x. Lemma 1 is split around a float, and the Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 labels are small.

Scores rest on three points:
- The design mathematics is correct and checks against the reported numbers.
- The studies test the stated hypotheses with falsifiable criteria.
- C2's "contact Jacobian" framing is stronger than what the C0 metric and the PIP-only column support, and the C2 effect is partly expected under position control.

Overall: a sound, modestly original Stage-1 paper. Accept at 6.
