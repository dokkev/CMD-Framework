CoDe Hand: Same Hand, Different Contact — Keep
How the Contact Surfaces Move, Not the Skeleton,
for Comparable Contact-Interface Studies
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—Contact interfaces such as thick compliant pads
and tactile modules decide how a robot hand touches an
object. To compare two interfaces, the finger must usually
be redesigned, because servos and joints occupy the space an
interface needs. The redesign changes the hand, so a measured
difference cannot be attributed to the interface alone. We
propose that a redesigned finger keep how its contact surfaces
move relative to each other, not its skeleton. In the proximalphalanx frame this motion is one column of the finger’s contact
Jacobian, and a single point sets it: the pole about which the
distal phalanx turns. We prove that a fixed-axis joint cannot
keep it when thicker pads are mounted as close as its joint
hardware allows, and give a closed-form lower bound. A remoteactuated crossed four-bar finger changes it by only 10–51%
of what a fixed axis must, at near-best contact surface. In
simulation, when one flexion command is reused across 15
redesigns for 5, 7 and 9 mm pads, the error in a measured
friction effect follows the change in contact-surface motion
(Spearman ρ = 0.83), and pad stiffness and shape effects
repeat this. The change in skeleton motion does not predict it.
Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand is the next stage
of this work.

I. Introduction
Contact is central to dexterous manipulation, and the
contact interface sets that contact. Compliant fingertips
change how an object is held [17, 12]. Vision-based
tactile sensors turn the contact patch into geometry and
force [20, 11]. Robot hands have become experimental
platforms [15, 16, 6], and most are built around servos
for maintainability. Servo bodies and joints occupy the
space an interface needs, above all around the joint. A
large or thick interface therefore costs range of motion or
contact surface, and carrying it is a redesign, not a module
swap [21, 4].
The redesign changes how the contact surfaces move
relative to each other. For a joint that turns about a
fixed axis this change is unavoidable when the pads sit
at their own depth. Interface studies often reuse the same
commands across hands: scripted grasps, frozen policies,
the same actuator targets. A measured difference then
mixes the interface with the hand’s change. One could
keep the skeleton instead, but the unchanged finger cannot
carry a thicker pad, and the object does not meet the
skeleton. It meets the contact surfaces.
In this work we propose CoDe (contact-centric design),
a servo-driven hand whose redesign target is how its
contact surfaces move relative to each other. Its servos

act remotely through a linkage, which frees the space
under the joints. Its PIP joint is a crossed four-bar linkage
(CFL), whose pole moves with flexion, so a thick pad
can sit under the joint. When a new interface asks for
a redesign, the finger is re-optimized to keep its contactsurface motion, not its skeleton motion (Fig. 1). This paper
analyses one planar finger of that hand. Our contributions
are:
C1 A bound. Finite-pole laws for the clearance, coverage
and contact-surface motion of planar finger joints. A
fixed axis whose pads sit as close as its hardware
allows cannot keep its contact-surface motion when
the pads thicken, with a closed-form lower bound
(Sec. III).
C2 A finger. The CFL keeps more contact surface than a
servo-at-joint finger as pads thicken and the required
flexion deepens (Sec. IV-B).
C3 A redesign that keeps contact. Re-optimized for
thicker pads, the CFL keeps its contact-surface motion well inside the fixed-axis bound at near-best
coverage (Sec. IV-C).
C4 Why it matters. In simulation, with reused flexion
commands, the error in measured friction, stiffness
and shape effects follows the change in contact-surface
motion, not the change in skeleton motion. Softer
actuation or force feedback weakens the dependence
(Sec. V).
Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand (C5) is the
next stage. We do not claim a better hand or a best interface. Keeping the contact-surface motion does not keep
the contact mechanics: those change with the interface,
and they are what an interface study measures.
II. Related Work
Contact interfaces. Vision-based tactile sensors need
an elastomer, a camera and optics behind the contact
surface [20, 11]. Fingers are therefore designed around
them: GelSight Svelte shapes a finger around one camera [21], and a hand has been built to carry large-area
tactile sensing [4]. Thin skins can be replaced without
redesign [3]. Fingertip materials have been compared [17]
and modelled [12]. Thick interfaces come with their own
finger, so comparing them compares fingers.
Finger joints. Open, low-cost hands use fixed-axis servo
joints [16, 6]. Rolling contact joints are compact [9, 18].

Crossed four-bar joints give polycentric finger motion [2,
1], and linkage-driven hands integrate the drive [10].
Prosthetic knees place a four-bar’s instantaneous centre
for stability [14]. None of these is analysed for what it
takes from the pads, or for whether its contact-surface
motion survives an interface change. Co-design methods
let the morphology change freely for a task [19, 8]. We
instead hold the contact-surface motion fixed, so that a
difference can be attributed to the interface.

IV. Keeping the Contact-Surface Motion by Design
A. Architectures and setup
We compare four PIP joints. 2R-DD is a revolute joint
with the actuator at the joint. 2R-remote is the same joint,
belt-driven from an actuator on the proximal phalanx.
RCJ is a rolling contact joint of two equal rollers [9]. CFL
is CoDe’s crossed four-bar [2], driven through its proximal
pivot. All share one goal: Lf = 90 mm, the same actuators,
pad requirements and collision rules. CFL ideal and RCJ
ideal omit the joint actuator’s body and serve as upper
bounds. We report the worst-case mechanical advantage
MAwc and do not constrain it.
Pads are 3, 5, 7 or 9 mm thick, and the required flexion
is qreq ∈ {90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ }. Collisions are checked on a 1◦
grid and re-verified at 0.05◦ . Study 1 maximizes C̄pad over
all design parameters with a genetic algorithm and NSGAII [7], three seeds each, and refines CFL from neighbouring
designs. On 51 designs, coverage computed from pole path
and pads matches the evaluator within 10−13 . On the
CFL designs, Eq. (4) gives the joint gap within 0.05 mm
(median).
B. The CFL keeps more contact surface (Study 1)
Fig. 3 shows the best coverage per condition. CFL
exceeds 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both have
a design, by 2.4 to 14.6 points, and also without the
refinement. The spread grows with thickness and flexion.
From 3 to 9 mm, CFL loses 0.085–0.096 of C̄pad . 2R-remote
loses 0.107 at 90◦ and 0.182 at 120◦ , and at 150◦ its 7
and 9 mm designs cannot hold a cylinder. On the cylinder
measure, 2R-remote leads at 90◦ , the two cross between 5
and 7 mm at 120◦ , and CFL leads at 150◦ . Eq. (5) explains
the split: CFL’s palmar pole lengthens the contour, while
2R-remote’s gap shortens when flexed. Measured as pad
length over the fixed Lf , CFL leads 2R-remote in every
condition beyond 3 mm. RCJ is lowest as built but among
the highest as RCJ ideal, so its loss comes from packaging,
not from the joint.

2R-remote meets the bound exactly at 90◦ and 120◦ .
CFL changes the contact-surface motion least among the
physical joints in every cell: by 0.35–4.31 mm, or 10–51% of
the bound. Its C̄pad stays within 0.2 points of Study 1’s
best. The other joints cannot close the gap by trading
coverage: giving up 2 points along their fronts, 2R-DD
and RCJ stay above the CFL in every cell where they
have a design. CFL absorbs the thicker pad by changing
its skeleton motion and its transmission (MAwc from
−7% to +16%). Control compensates the transmission:
flexion commands through each hand’s servo table hold
flexion within 0.07◦ in simulation. Coverage alone does
not keep the motion. Two independent 7 mm CFL designs
of Study 1 change it by 1.28 and 3.91 mm, against 0.86 mm
for the redesign.
V. Why the Contact-Surface Motion Matters
Studies 1 and 2 show that the contact-surface motion
can be kept. We now ask why it should be kept. Interface
studies compare hands under one protocol. Closing on
touch or on force would use signals that the interface itself
changes, since a softer or sensorized pad detects touch and
force differently. The clean protocol is to reuse one set
of commands, as scripted grasps and joint-space policies
do. A reused command closes each hand past its own
first contact, and where that contact begins along the
command is set by the contact-surface motion. Sec. III
therefore predicts that the error follows ∆contact , and that
the skeleton, which a designer would naturally hold, does
not predict it. We test this prediction.
Setup. We simulate the CoDe hand model’s three fingers
in Isaac Lab 3 with Newton and MuJoCo-Warp. The
fingers use CFL designs (qreq = 120◦ ) with the MCP held.
Flexion is commanded through each hand’s transmission
table at one flexion stiffness, K0 = 5 N m/rad, which
compensates the transmission change. A cylinder of radius
15 or 20 mm rests on each hand’s proximal pad, free in
the sagittal plane, at the place along the face where the
reference O (3 mm pads) touches it with both pads at q0
(q0 = 20◦ to 120◦ in 5◦ steps). The PIP closes to q0 + 5◦ ,
the cylinder is released, and a load rises in one of four
directions until the cylinder slips, loses a pad or collapses
the grasp. Three interface changes are measured: pad
friction (µ from 1.3 to 2.0), pad material stiffness (contact
stiffness from 2.5 to 1.0 kN/m) and pad shape (flat to 2 mm
crowned). For a hand H, ∆(H) is the change of the limit
load, and the error is E(H) = |∆(H) − ∆(O)|/|∆(O)|, the
median over placements and directions. All gates were
fixed before the runs. The hands are 18 redesigns for
3 → 5, 7 and 9 mm. Each thickness has R, which keeps
the contact-surface motion, U , which is redesigned for
coverage alone, and redesigns at set values of ∆contact ,
some keeping the skeleton as closely as possible and some
changing it as much as possible. The unchanged finger
with thicker pads collides at 47◦ and cannot be tested.

redesign that keeps it best (∆skel = 0.4 mm, 5 mm pads)

errs 21%. The other interface changes repeat the result

The mechanism and its protocols. At the same com-

We proposed that a finger redesigned for a new contact
interface keep how its contact surfaces move, not its
skeleton. A fixed axis cannot do this, while a remoteactuated crossed four-bar can, at near-best contact surface. In simulation, the change in contact-surface motion,
not in skeleton motion, predicts how much a reused
command distorts a measured interface effect, for pad
friction, stiffness and shape alike.
Limitations and future work. Our evidence is computational: a planar finger model and a simulated hand whose
pads are represented by a contact model. The physical
stage will measure the contact-surface motion and repeat
the interface test on hardware. The redesign keeps the PIP
column of the contact Jacobian; extending it to the MCP
calls for a polycentric MCP, which we leave to future work.
Camera-based tactile modules are thicker than the pads
studied here and are the next interfaces to bring under
the same analysis.
Stage 2. One CoDe finger keeps its servos, palm and
phalanx bodies; only the pad modules and the CFL links
change, giving O (3 mm), R and U (7 mm). We will record
the parts, time and recalibration each exchange needs

will give the measured ∆contact against the model and

the principle to hardware, and disagreement measures how
much real pads and manufacturing add to the geometry.
