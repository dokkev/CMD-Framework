Same Motion, Different Contact: Design of a
Contact-Centric Robot Hand
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—Many research hands for dexterous manipulation
place a servo at every joint. Such a hand has room for a
contact interface only at the fingertip, and redesigning its
finger for a thicker interface changes how the finger moves,
so the interface’s own effect cannot be measured. We propose
contact-centric design: move the servo off the joint, and when
the interface changes, redesign the finger so that its contact
surfaces keep their motion while the skeleton is free to change.
A finite-displacement pole analysis shows that a fixed axis
above the pad’s mount cannot keep that motion from the same
initial contact point, and that a crossed four-bar keeps most
of it. In design studies, the resulting finger keeps more contact
surface than a servo-at-joint finger wherever both are feasible.
We test this in simulation. When a redesign reuses the original
hand’s impedance setpoint, how far its response to a new pad
departs from the original hand’s grows with how much its
contact-surface motion changes (ρ = 0.94 over 27 redesigns,
pre-registered), not with how much its skeleton changes. This
holds in two simulators and two contact models, and with
the contact-force difference controlled. A redesign for coverage
alone reports that a thicker, more compliant pad raises the
pull-out load, when on the original hand it lowers it.

I. Introduction

O's command

As robots are expected to manipulate everyday objects,
the contact interfaces of their fingers shape both how
forces are transmitted and how contact is sensed: a
compliant pad conforms and spreads the force, and a
tactile skin reports the contact [9, 23, 14, 26]. Servodriven platforms have made learning-based dexterous
manipulation accessible [22, 1, 8], but a new interface on
them needs custom integration for each pairing [27, 4], and
fingertip modules [18, 5] leave the phalanges, where many
grasps rest [6, 11], untouched. Adapting the hand to the
interface instead changes its embodiment. This is more
than an integration cost: it decides whether a measured
benefit of an interface belongs to the interface at all, and
whether a command or policy learned on one hand still
means the same on the next.
The obstacle is the coupling between the interface’s
geometry and the finger’s kinematics. Servos at the joints
take the room an interface needs; a fixed axis trades
interface thickness against coverage and range of motion;
and remote-center-of-motion joints keep a continuous
skin [13, 15] but not the same kinematics across interfaces.
So a basic question stands unanswered: when the interface
changes, which kinematic property must stay unchanged?

Our insight is that a finger should preserve how its
contact surfaces move, its contact Jacobian (how contact
points move with flexion, seen from the proximal phalanx), not how its skeleton moves (Fig. 1(c)). The object
meets the surfaces, not the skeleton: joint commands and

impedance gains [22, 21] press the object through the
surfaces, so if the surfaces move differently, the same
command presses differently. Both the room between the
surfaces and their relative motion are set by where the
joint’s rotation pole lies relative to them, so a joint
that places its pole by design, apart from the joint
hardware, can fit a new interface and keep that motion.
We call this Contact-centric Design (CoDe). The CoDe
Hand (Fig. 1(a)) is a platform for studying interfaces
this way: commercial smart servos moved off the joints
make room, and a crossed four-bar linkage (CFL) at the
proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint places the pole. Our
contributions are:
• What to keep when the interface changes: the contactsurface motion, not the skeleton; no redesign whose
contact surfaces deepen keeps both, a fixed axis
cannot keep the former, and a crossed four-bar keeps
most of it at near-best coverage.
• A modular finger for diverse pads, with more room
for thick interfaces than a servo-at-joint finger.
• Evidence in simulation: how far a reused command’s
response to a new pad departs from the original
hand’s tracks the change in the redesign’s contactsurface motion, not in its skeleton; a policy learned
on the original hand transfers to the redesign that
keeps the motion its task uses.
Throughout, O is the original hand, a crossed four-bar
finger with 3 mm pads; CoDe, Skeleton and Coverage are
its redesigns for thicker pads that keep the contact-surface
motion (∆pd small), the fingertip path (∆skel small), or
neither.
II. Related Work
Tendon-driven hands free the joints of actuators [8],
rolling contact joints [16, 24], crossed four-bar joints [3, 2]
and linkage-driven hands [17] give compact or polycentric
articulation, and remote-center-of-motion fingers carry
thick skin across their joints [13, 15]. None has been
analyzed for whether its contact-surface motion survives
a change of interface. Co-design lets the morphology
change freely for a task [25, 12], whereas contact-centric
design holds the contact-surface motion fixed, so that the
interface remains the variable.

the contact motion.

D. Comparative Design Studies
Four PIP articulations share one finger length and one
actuator (Fig. 3(a)): a revolute joint with the servo at the
joint (at-joint), the same joint belt-driven from a servo on
the proximal phalanx (remote, which separates the benefit
of moving the actuator from that of the articulation),
an RCJ with equal rollers [16], and the CFL [3]. Each
architecture’s coverage-optimal design (Eq. (8), Fig. 3(a))
is its own reference; for the CFL it is also the Coverage
hand of Sec. IV, and its contact-preserving redesign
(Eq. (9)) is the CoDe hand. Pads of 3, 5, 7 and 9 mm and
qreq ∈ {90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ } are considered. Both problems are
solved by evolutionary search [10].
1) Room: The CFL covers more than the at-joint finger
in all 10 conditions where both are feasible, by 2.4–14.6
percentage points (Fig. 3(a)). Remote actuation alone does
as well at 90◦ and up to 5 mm at 120◦ , but at 150◦ its
7 and 9 mm designs cannot hold a cylinder between both
pads.
2) Preserved motion: The CFL preserves its contactsurface motion best of the four joints in all nine conditions
(Fig. 3(c)); at 120◦ , 3 → 7 mm, its whole front lies at
smaller ∆pd and higher coverage than every other front.

B. Results
1) The error follows the contact surfaces: The error
grows with ∆pd . In Isaac Lab this holds in all four tests
(friction, stiffness, a reused torque, and the grid; 14–45
redesigns each; permutation p < 0.001, Holm-corrected),
also with ∆skel held fixed (partial ρ 0.86–0.91). ∆skel ,
coverage and thickness do not. CoDe’s E is 4, 10 and 38%
at 5, 7 and 9 mm, Coverage’s 124–173%. The objective, not
the freedom, does this: at 7 mm, freeing the pivots without
∆1d leaves E at 17% (21% with them fixed); adding ∆1d
brings it to 10%. The four Drake tasks keep this ordering
in both contact models (Fig. 4, Table II); Skeleton errs
as much as Coverage or more (E 222% vs. 124%, Isaac
Lab, 7 mm). Across simulators, contact models and pad
changes (Table I, 7 mm pull-out), CoDe’s median error is
closer to zero than Coverage’s in eleven of twelve settings,
and its typical error E is smaller in all twelve. Swapping
in the thicker, compliant pad itself (Isaac Lab, 7 mm), the
median pull-out load falls by 41% on O and 39% on CoDe
but rises by 48% on Coverage (Fig. 1(d)); over 27 grid
redesigns this error follows ∆pd (ρ = 0.94, pre-registered).
Nor is it only preload: under an equal reused torque the
contact forces stay close to O’s, and ∆pd still predicts the
error with the force deviation held fixed (partial ρ = 0.80;
0.79 over the 27). Re-referencing the setpoint so that the
fingertip contact point sits where O’s does cuts the median
error of 18 Drake redesigns from 42 to 14%, but what

Limitations. The evidence is simulated, and no pad

carry over to Drake even on O.
