ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Hypotheses as I read them:
- H0 (design claim, Sec. III): the finite-displacement pole of the PIP sets both the room available for thick interfaces (Eq. 1) and the relative motion of the contact surfaces (Eq. 2). A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion when the interface deepens (Eq. 4), and a crossed four-bar (CFL) can.
- H1: when a redesigned finger reuses the original hand's impedance setpoint, the error it adds to a measured interface effect grows with the change in contact-surface motion (Δ^prox_distal) and not with the change in skeleton (Δ_skel).
- H2: this is more than a preload change. Δ^prox_distal still predicts the error once contact force is matched.
- H3: a policy trained on O keeps its performance on a redesign that keeps the motion its task uses.

Evidence for each:
- H0 rests on clean, simple kinematics. Eq. 1 checks out (2·tan 60° = 3.46). Eq. 3 is a triangle-inequality bound. The CFL beats the at-joint finger on coverage in 10/10 feasible conditions and has the lowest Δ^prox_distal in 9/9. This part is convincing within its scope: a planar finger, PIP only, and rigid pads.
- H1 has the most evidence. In Isaac Lab, four tests give p<0.001 with Holm correction, partial ρ is up to 0.91 with the skeleton held fixed, and ρ=0.94 over 27 redesigns that the paper calls pre-registered. In Drake, Hold and Acquire have ρ≈0.9, but Dial and Pivot are weaker (0.53, 0.43). The Skeleton-vs-CoDe contrast is well designed: 0.3 mm fingertip deviation vs 6.6 mm pad deviation, and E of 222% vs about 10%. Two concerns limit it.
  - (i) The ground-truth "interface effect" is measured on O, which "carries the new interface's contact stiffness on its own geometry". That is a counterfactual hand that cannot physically carry the thicker interface. The paper defines the interface's effect as excluding its thickness, but thickness is part of what makes an interface different.
  - (ii) Pads are rigid, and compliance appears only as contact stiffness (point or hydroelastic). For compliant pads and skins, deformation and contact migration are first-order, and the paper names them as the main threat.
- H2 rests on a partial ρ of 0.80 with force difference held fixed, plus the re-referencing result (42%→14%, with the residual still following Δ^prox_distal). This is reasonable but thin: one statistic, and the number of points is not given.
- H3 is the weakest and is close to post-hoc. Fig. 5 shows the following.
  - Finger walk: CoDe keeps about 65%, Skeleton about 0%.
  - Relay dial: Skeleton wins.
  - Cube rotation: the paper brings in a third metric, the proximal-pad offset against the palm (ρ=−0.98 over 14 redesigns).
  
  So each task is explained by a different "motion its task uses", picked after the outcome. The Drake Box-pivot task is fingertip contact, yet CoDe wins there (E 7% vs 27%). That conflicts with the claim that "contact at the fingertip's end follows the skeleton". "Carry over" is also generous when CoDe keeps about 65–75% in Isaac Lab. There are two training seeds.

Internal-consistency checks:
- Bound check against Table II: for 3→7 mm, Δt=4 mm gives 2·sin60°·4 ≈ 6.9 mm. Skeleton (6.6+0.3=6.9) and Coverage (3.9+3.4=7.3) sit on or above it, but CoDe (1.1+4.8=5.9) is below it. That is only possible if CoDe's Δt is smaller than the thickness change, for example because F′ frees the pivot heights or the mount changes. The paper does not explain this, and a reader checking Eq. 3 will stop here.
- Table I, Commands columns: under a reused torque, Coverage has 0 error and CoDe +8. Under touch (−17 vs −14) and force (+17 vs −8) the two are comparable. So "the same order appears ... under other ... commands" is not true for torque-type commands. This actually supports a narrower claim: the effect is specific to position or impedance setpoints, which is the right scope, but it should be said.

Basis for scores:
- Value 3: the question is clearly posed and relevant to the tactile, skin and hand-design community and to cross-embodiment policy reuse: what must a redesign preserve so that interface comparisons and reused commands stay valid? The negative result for the "keep the skeleton" baseline is useful. The impact is limited by the planar, PIP-only, servo-hand, rigid-pad scope. The mechanism (same joint setpoint plus different surface motion gives different contact forces) is close to expected impedance physics, so the surprise lies mainly in the magnitude and in Skeleton being as bad as Coverage.
- Feasibility 2: the evidence is simulation only, interfaces never deform, and there is no hardware. The ground-truth definition is counterfactual. H3 lacks an a-priori task-to-metric mapping. Against strong competing submissions with hardware, this sits below the bar for a 3, although the H1 statistics are careful.
- Recommendation: Invite to Rebuttal. The hypothesis and method are fully stated and partly pre-registered, and the Stage 2 plan (hardware CoDe/Skeleton/Coverage fingers, a deforming-pad model) can falsify the central claim.
