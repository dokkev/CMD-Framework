THOUGHT:

The paper asks a narrow, well-posed question. To compare thick contact interfaces, a finger usually has to be redesigned. What should the redesign hold fixed so that a measured difference can be attributed to the interface? The proposed answer is the relative motion of the contact surfaces: the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame, equivalently the finite pole of the distal phalanx relative to the face. The argument has three parts. (i) Elementary planar kinematics: finite-pole clearance (Prop. 1), coverage (Prop. 2), |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| (Prop. 3) and a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1). (ii) Optimization studies over four PIP architectures: coverage in Study 1, Δ_contact in Study 2. (iii) A simulation study (Study 3) showing that the error in a transferred interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) rank-correlates with Δ_contact and not with skeleton change, coverage, thickness or transmission.

My intuitions:

1. The conceptual reframing (keep the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton) is clean and, as far as I know, not stated this way in the hand-design literature. Using the finite pole is elegant, and Fig. 2 explains it well. But the mathematics is textbook planar kinematics. Lemma 1 is Bottema & Roth. Prop. 3 is the identity c − c0 = (I − R_q)(p − c0), and the corollary follows because a fixed axis is its own pole. Calling this "a bound" (C1) oversells it. The novelty is in the framing, not the theory. The paper also does not connect to classical motion-generation synthesis (Burmester theory, precision-point and pole-based four-bar synthesis), which is exactly the tool for "re-synthesize a four-bar so a coupler point follows a given path." The NSGA-II redesign in Study 2 is a numerical solution to a problem with a rich classical literature. Polycentric prosthetic knees and self-aligning exoskeleton joints are also natural related work on matching an instantaneous-centre path.

2. C2 appears to be falsified by the paper's own data, given its stated failure criterion. C2: "The CFL keeps more contact surface than a servo-at-joint finger as pads thicken... It fails if both lose coverage alike." From Fig. 3, 3→9 mm: at q_req = 90°, 2R-DD loses about 5.6 points (74.8→69.2) and CFL about 8.5 (80.2→71.7). At 120°, 2R-DD loses about 8.4 and CFL about 8.5. So against the servo-at-joint finger, the CFL loses as much or more as pads thicken at 90° and 120°. Only at 150° does 2R-DD fail beyond 5 mm. The text then reports the loss against 2R-remote instead (0.182 at 120°). It also concedes that 2R-remote leads on the paper's own objective at 90°, and switches to a different metric (pad length over fixed L_f) to claim a CFL lead "beyond 3 mm." The level difference (2.4–14.6 points) is real. But the hypothesis as written concerns degradation with thickness, and the evidence does not support it at two of three flexion levels. Either C2 must be restated or this must be discussed honestly. In addition, CFL alone gets a neighbour-refinement step, so its optimization budget differs from the baselines.

3. Study 3 is the heart of the paper and the most interesting part. Its design is careful: pre-registered thresholds, designed redesigns that decouple Δ_contact from Δ_skel, a hand-independent placement grid, a torque protocol, and robustness checks. But I have four concerns.
   (a) Under a reused flexion command, a change in where the distal face sits at a given q directly changes penetration, and therefore normal force, in a penalty-based contact model. The correlation between Δ_contact and error is then close to mechanical necessity: Δ_contact is essentially the over-closure. The torque protocol is the right control, and Δ_contact still correlates there (ρ = 0.75, n = 16). But the errors are much smaller (3–40%), and R vs U is 5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%. The practical benefit at equal force is modest.
   (b) The paper's own numbers show that closing from touch or matching contact force leaves 20% or 3–7% median error. That is comparable to or better than R under the reused command (3/21/41%). The dismissal ("both need contact sensing, which a sensorized interface alters") is too quick. Motor-current or joint-torque sensing, an external F/T sensor, or a vision-based touch detector would not depend on the interface under test. If force-matched protocols largely solve the problem, the motivation for constraining the hardware redesign weakens considerably. This deserves a direct argument.
   (c) Study 3 appears to use only CFL redesigns ("the CoDe hand model's three CFL fingers"). Whether Δ_contact predicts error across architectures, for example fixed-axis redesigns that sit at the bound, is not tested. The claim "the contact-surface motion matters," rather than "for CFL redesigns, the contact-surface motion matters," needs cross-architecture hands.
   (d) The samples are small (14–16 hands for most columns, 6 for shape), and five candidates are tested across four settings without multiplicity correction. Δ_skel correlates negatively with error on O's contacts (−0.40, −0.51). That is probably an artifact of the designed sampling and of the Eq. (7) trade-off at fixed mount depth, rather than evidence that a skeleton change "does not help." The partial correlations help but should be the headline. On the grid, Δ_skel is positive (0.46), and the authors explain it away through placements losing a pad. That is plausible but post hoc.

4. Modelling fidelity. Pads are a penalty contact model in MuJoCo-Warp. "Pad stiffness" is the contact stiffness, and thickness does not change compliance. Yet the central physical point of thick interfaces is that their bulk compliance and thickness-dependent deformation change the contact patch. The error metric treats O's measured effect as ground truth. Physically, a 9 mm pad has a genuinely different friction or stiffness effect than a 3 mm pad, so part of the "error" is real physics, not hand confounding. The paper says "keeping the contact-surface motion does not keep the contact mechanics," but then reports any deviation from O as error. The error metric needs a sharper definition of what is being attributed to what.

5. The exclusion of alternatives rests on design requirements stated somewhat by fiat. Spacers are excluded because they "cost 8.6–18.2 points of coverage," and recessed fixed axes are excluded because "lugs beside them would take width from the pad." Both are reasonable engineering arguments. But a practitioner who wants comparable interface studies may happily accept a few points of coverage, or a few mm of pad width, for a trivially exact Δ_contact = 0. The coverage-versus-comparability trade-off should be quantified rather than ruled out. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, and the transmission change is said to be "compensated by control." With position-controlled servos with torque limits, a changed MA changes achievable force, which matters for limit-load measurements on hardware.

6. Writing. The structure is good: contributions stated as falsifiable hypotheses, short sentences, and a clear Fig. 1. But the text is extremely compressed and number-dense. The abstract packs five ranges, and "by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound" reads like a reduction when it means "Δ_contact equals 10–51% of the bound." Several terms are used before or without definition: "transmission table," "degenerate 2R-remote designs (0.21, 0)," "CFL ideal," the Table I column groups ("O's contacts," "grid"), "the solver's impedance ratio." Eq. (4) has an apparent positive-part operator whose meaning is not stated. The title is long and hard to parse. Fig. 4d shows 2R-DD and RCJ starting with Δc(0) ≠ 0, which violates the "keep c0" premise of Cor. 1, so plotting them against the bound mixes two kinds of change. Related work is appropriately short for Stage 1 but misses kinematic synthesis.

7. Stage-1 fit. The hypotheses, methodology and Stage-2 plan (one physical finger with exchangeable pads, O/R/U builds, face tracking, grasp-and-pull) are stated. What insight a negative hardware result would give is not discussed. The guidelines ask for that.

Overall: an original framing with a careful simulation methodology, but the theory is elementary, one of the four stated hypotheses (C2) is contradicted by the paper's own figure, the key Study 3 evidence is confined to one architecture and partly mechanically implied by over-closure, and the practical motivation is undercut by the paper's own force-matching numbers. I lean to reject at this stage, with a clear path to a strong Stage-2 paper.
