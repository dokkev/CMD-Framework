# Review v2 — Reviewer 2 (robot manipulation / tactile sensing, user of interface-comparison hands)

## THOUGHT (note-taking phase)

**What the paper claims.** When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should keep the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal one (c(q), equivalently the PIP column J of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton. The tools are the finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement and three laws built on it: symmetric clearance (Eq. 2), coverage (Eq. 3), and trajectory ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp|| (Eq. 4). Together they give a fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 5). Three planar/simulation studies follow. Study 1: best coverage over 4 architectures × 4 thicknesses × 3 flexion requirements. Study 2: CFL re-optimised to keep c(q). Study 3: Isaac Lab, 18 CFL hands, error in a measured friction effect under reused commands. H4 (hardware exchange) is left to Stage 2.

**My perspective.** I would use such a hand to compare a thin skin, a thick compliant pad and a vision-based tactile module. So I ask three things. (i) Does the confound they identify matter under the control protocols I would actually use? (ii) Does the studied design space reach the interfaces I care about? (iii) Is the Stage-2 protocol specified well enough that I would trust its result?

**Where it is strong.**
- The framing is genuinely useful and, to my knowledge, new. It separates intended interface variation from unintended embodiment variation, and it names a specific invariant (relative contact-face motion) instead of "keep the hand the same". Fig. 1(a–c) makes this concrete.
- The finite-pole analysis is clean and classical. Eq. 4 is simple and gives a closed-form fixed-axis bound (Eq. 5) that the 2R-remote designs meet exactly. That is a good check that the optimiser and the theory agree. The evaluator check (51 designs, 1e-13) and P1 (47/51 optima clearance-active) are good hygiene.
- The hypotheses come with explicit falsification branches ("If false, ..."). Study 1 honestly reports where CFL loses: 2R-remote leads at 90°, and RCJ-ideal closes the gap.
- Study 3 was set up with care: gates were fixed before the runs, the evaluator was never re-tuned, redesigns were selected on design metrics alone, and several protocols were tested, including one (torque reuse) that weakens the headline effect. I appreciate that this is reported.

**Main concerns.**
1. *Practical relevance of the H3 effect under realistic protocols.* The large errors (52–124% for U) occur under reused position commands at a fixed stiffness K0 = 5 N m/rad. Under torque reuse the error falls to 3–40%, and with matched distal force to 3–7%. Interface studies (friction, tactile, compliance) normally grasp to a target normal force. The obvious alternative remedy is therefore "control the contact force" rather than "redesign the kinematics to keep c(q)". The paper does not argue why kinematic preservation is needed when force matching nearly removes the confound. It also does not show which interface measurements (contact location, tactile imprint position, slip onset location, shear direction) still depend on c(q) under force control. For a tactile researcher, that dependence is exactly where c(q) should matter: where on the sensor the object lands. It is not measured.
2. *The studied thickness range does not reach the motivating interfaces.* The introduction motivates the work with vision-based tactile modules (GelSight, DIGIT), but those need roughly 15–30 mm of depth behind the contact face. The studies stop at 9 mm, and at 9 mm the "contact-keeping" CFL already moves c by 2.3–4.3 mm. In Study 3, R at 9 mm still errs by 41%, which is no better than another 9 mm hand (≈40% at D_cL_f ≈ 3.5 mm in Fig. 1d). So preservation is partial exactly where it is most needed, and the trend suggests it fails for real tactile modules. The Stage-2 plan names "a tactile module [21]" without saying how it fits.
3. *The H3 attribution relies on a small, curated design set.* n = 15, pooled over three thicknesses. Pad thickness and D_c are correlated by construction. The redesigns were chosen "so that D_c, D_s and C̄_pad vary apart", and R deliberately has a large skeleton change. That choice may itself produce the negative ρ(E, D_s) = −0.40. "Thickness adds nothing once D_c is known" is asserted without a stated test (partial correlation? regression with thickness as a covariate?). In addition, under position control it is close to built in that a hand whose contact faces sit elsewhere presses differently. The mechanism (force mismatch, 3.9× for U) is the expected one, so the more informative result would be the residual dependence once force is matched. That residual is 3–7% and is not broken down by D_c.
4. *Internal inconsistencies* that a careful reader will catch:
   - Fig. 1 caption says U presses "3.9× harder" (17.4/4.5 N in the figure), but Sec. V-D says "3.3× harder (Fig. 1b)".
   - Sec. V-D says "hands with D_cL_f ≥ 2.9 mm cannot hold the cylinder", yet Fig. 1d and Fig. 5a plot error values for hands at D_cL_f = 3.0–5.1 mm, including U at 3.9 mm.
   - Fig. 1 caption says R "grips like O", but Fig. 1c shows a distal force of 3.0 N against O's 4.5 N, about a third lower.
   - The abstract says 15 redesigns; the contributions say 18; Fig. 5a says n = 15 for position and n = 16 for torque. The exclusions should be explained once.
   - "The CoDe hand model's three fingers" in the Study 3 setup conflicts with the single-finger, two-pad cylinder hold described elsewhere.
   - Sec. IV-B points to Fig. 4 for the four-bar's z(q), P_x(q), but Fig. 4 (pole concept) shows a generic lowered pole, not a four-bar.
5. *H4 / Stage-2 methodology is underspecified for a Stage-1 paper.* The guidelines require the methodology to be fully fleshed out even when results are missing. H4 has one sentence: two or three interfaces, no disassembly, tracked face motion checked against the model. Missing: which interfaces and their dimensions; the measurement (motion capture? tactile imprint location?); the tasks and metrics; the acceptance thresholds for "same actuation and procedure"; and what is learned if exchange fails beyond "the integration cost is the result". I would want an explicit hardware analogue of Study 3: measure a friction or tactile effect across two interfaces on R-type and U-type redesigns.
6. *Scope of the coverage objective.* The objective is a cylinder inscribed in the V, averaged over q ≥ 90° only. Fingertip and pinch contacts at low flexion, which dominate tactile exploration, are not represented. Both pads have equal thickness, whereas many interface studies swap only the fingertip. The model is planar with the MCP held, links and belts on other lateral layers are not collision-checked against pads, and the MCP column is explicitly not kept. That column governs where the finger meets an object relative to the palm or wrist in a real grasp.
7. *Clarity.* The text is very dense and leans on notation; several key sentences are compressed to the point of ambiguity. Examples: "The skeleton (motion) change D_s is the same for the fingertip"; "so with the same pose at extension keeping c is keeping J"; "R stays at 14–26% while U rises ... where the contact's compliance takes the interference". The title is long and repeats the slogan. Figure numbering in the PDF (Study 1 = Fig. 2, pole concept = Fig. 4, placed after Study 2) does not follow the order of first reference.

**Smaller technical points.**
- With φ = q on every design, J reduces to c′. The paper should say that c(q) plus φ fully determines the relative rigid motion of the faces. A one-sentence justification that a single point suffices would help.
- Eqs. 2–3 hold only under S1–S2, which the optima satisfy "approximately". The deviation should be quantified, for example the error of Eq. 3 against the exact evaluator on the 51 designs.
- In Study 2, comparing D_c with Eq. 5 is like-for-like only for CFL and 2R-remote (Δd = 0). 2R-DD and RCJ go below the bound by remounting, so "CFL is 10–51% of the bound" mixes reference points across architectures.
- Study 1 optimisation: CFL runs vary by 1.3–3.4 points and got extra restarts (up to +2.1). The authors acknowledge this favours CFL. The 90° and 120°/5–7 mm crossovers are within that spread, so the H1 "spread grows" conclusion should carry that uncertainty.
- Study 3 depends on rigid contact. The TODO on contact stiffness, solver and timestep is essential, because Fig. 5b already shows the effect saturating at contact compliance. I would also want a compliant-pad contact model, since compliance is the interface property most interface studies vary, and a second interface property besides friction (the promised "shape interface").

**What I need from the TODO.** (a) E vs D_c under at least two contact stiffnesses spanning soft-pad values, two solvers or timesteps, with ρ and the R/U ordering reported. (b) The shape interface run with the same gates. (c) Ideally, a force-matched protocol broken down by D_c, plus a contact-location metric (where the object lands on each pad), which is the quantity a tactile study would see.

**Decision reasoning.** The concept and the analytical core are original and well executed, and the stated hypotheses are falsifiable. But the evidence that keeping c(q) is *what interface experiments need* (H3) is simulation-only, rests on a protocol that force control largely neutralises, and covers thicknesses below the motivating tactile modules. The Stage-2 methodology (H4) is not fleshed out as Stage 1 requires, and there are several number and figure inconsistencies. I lean toward rejection in the current form and could be persuaded if the TODO results land and H4 is specified. Overall 5.
