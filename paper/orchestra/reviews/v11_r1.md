THOUGHT:

What the paper claims. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface (compliant pad, tactile module), the redesign should keep the motion of the contact surfaces relative to each other (the distal-face point path c(q) in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton/fingertip path. The paper (i) derives finite-pole relations for joint clearance, cylinder coverage and contact-surface motion of a planar PIP joint, including a fixed-axis lower bound Delta_contact >= 2 sin(q_req/2)|Delta h - Delta d|; (ii) shows by GA/NSGA-II search that a remote-driven crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) and changes its contact-surface motion least among four joint types (10-51% of the fixed-axis bound); (iii) shows in MuJoCo-Warp simulation that, when a flexion command or joint torque is reused across redesigns, the error in a measured interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) rank-correlates with Delta_contact (rho = 0.60-0.94) and not consistently with skeleton change, coverage, thickness or transmission.

My first impression. The framing is fresh and well posed for a design paper: "what should a redesign hold invariant so that an interface comparison is attributable?" Stating each contribution as a falsifiable hypothesis, fixing thresholds before the runs, reporting bootstrap intervals and running a hand-independent placement grid are all good practice, and above average for a hand-design paper. The pole-based analysis is neat and the numbers check out where I could verify them (e.g., 5 tan 60 = 8.7 mm per pad; the shrink-gap/lengthen-denominator argument after Eq. (5) is algebraically consistent: the denominator grows by 2 delta[tan(q/2)(1 - cos(q/2)) + sin(q/2)]... reduces to 2 delta tan(q/2) as claimed).

Main concerns, in order of weight.

1. Near-tautology of the central empirical claim (C4). In the reused-command protocol the object is placed where O's contact surfaces touch it, and the PIP is closed to q0 + 5 deg. Delta_contact is, by definition, the maximum displacement of the distal face relative to the proximal face from O's. Any hand whose distal face is displaced relative to O's will touch the object earlier or later and over-/under-close; the paper itself reports that U presses 3.3-3.9x harder. So the correlation between Delta_contact and the error in a limit-load difference is largely built in by the protocol. The competing "candidates" are weak: the object never touches the skeleton, so nobody would expect Delta_skel to predict contact forces; pad thickness, coverage and transmission are not plausible invariants either (transmission is undone by construction through the per-hand transmission table). The informative competitor would be something like object-relative contact location, closing aperture, or the contact normal/force at first touch. The torque protocol is the more convincing test, but there the effect is modest (R vs U: 5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%), the sample is 16 hands, and the paper notes that contact-aware protocols (close from own touch, match contact force) reduce the error to 3-20% regardless. That last result arguably undercuts the significance: a practitioner can largely solve the attribution problem with a better protocol rather than a redesigned finger.

2. Correlational evidence over an author-constructed population. The redesign population (R, U, and "redesigns at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible") is chosen by the authors, and Spearman rho over 14-16 hands depends strongly on how that population is sampled. Eq. (7) forces a trade-off between Delta_contact and Delta_skel, which explains the negative rho for skeleton in the main columns; its sign flips to +0.46 on the grid, which the paper attributes to placements lost off the grid. The rho = 0.94 for shape is over six hands and should not appear in the abstract range. Five candidates are tested across four settings without multiplicity control. A factorial design (vary Delta_contact at matched Delta_skel and vice versa, which the "partial 0.88" hints at) and effect-size reporting (error per mm of Delta_contact) would be more convincing than a ranking of correlations. Also, even R errs 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm under the reused command, so "keep the contact-surface motion" does not by itself make interface comparisons trustworthy; the paper should state what residual error is acceptable.

3. Study 1/2 comparisons and hypothesis framing. C2 is formulated against 2R-DD only, which is the weakest architecture by construction (servo body at the joint). Against 2R-remote, CFL loses at 90 deg and at thin pads at 120 deg on the paper's own objective; the text then switches to a different metric ("pad length over the fixed L_f") to claim CFL leads. That metric switching should be avoided or justified up front. In Study 2 the CFL has more design parameters than a revolute or rolling joint, so a lower Delta_contact is expected; the interesting question is whether a fixed-axis finger that is allowed to recess its pad (Delta d > 0) would meet the bound with equality at zero, and the argument against that (pin must sit above the pad mounting surface because lugs would take width) is a single sentence and is not tested in the search. C3 says "well inside the bound" but 51% of the bound is reached in some cell; "well inside" needs a threshold. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, yet the CFL "absorbs the thicker pad by changing its transmission"; for a servo-driven hand this has real torque/speed consequences that are not reported.

4. Theory is correct but elementary. Lemma 1 is classical; Prop. 3 is (I - R_q) Delta p; Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality; the corollary follows directly. Props. 1-2 need restrictive symmetry assumptions (S1, S2, equal pads meeting phalanx ends). This is fine as a supporting analysis but "We prove..." in the abstract oversells it. Calling dc/dq of a single face point "the PIP column of the finger's contact Jacobian" is a rebranding: the contact Jacobian in grasp analysis maps joint velocities to velocities at the (moving) contact point on the object, which is not a fixed point on the distal face. The choice of c (the point under the fingertip) and the frame origin (foot of the MCP axis on the proximal face) are conventions that affect Delta_contact (the start offset of 2R-DD and RCJ in Fig. 4d); the sensitivity of the conclusions to this choice should be discussed.

5. Stage-2 methodology is underspecified. The venue asks that methodology be fully fleshed out, with insights stated even for negative results. The physical stage gets two sentences. Crucially, the error metric E(H) relies on the simulation trick that all pads share one contact model, so that O's effect is the ground truth. Physically, a 7 mm pad and a 3 mm pad are different interfaces, so "what the redesign adds" is not directly observable. How will the physical experiment separate the kinematic error from the true interface difference? Which interfaces, loads and protocols will be used, and what outcome would falsify C4 physically? This is the most important missing piece for a Stage-1 submission.

6. Simulation fidelity. Pads are rigid geometry with a penalty-contact stiffness, so pad "thickness" is purely geometric and compliance-induced contact patch growth (the very reason thick compliant pads exist) is absent. The study setup mentions "the CoDe hand model's three CFL fingers" while the analysis is of a single planar finger with a sagittal cylinder; the role of the other fingers is unclear.

Writing. The prose is terse and disciplined, but too dense for the content: the abstract and Sec. V pack many numbers into each sentence, and several quantities are used before definition or never defined in the text I read (x0, r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, L_vp, S2 "joint-side corners", "impedance ratio"). Table I is hard to parse (column groups "O's contacts / grid / Torque"; bolding meaning; why the shape column is missing). Fig. 1 caption says 15 redesigns while the setup says 18 (the difference is the three that do not hold the cylinder; say so). The title is long and the phrase "Same Hand, Different Contact" contradicts the paper's premise that the hand is redesigned. Figures are clean and informative; Fig. 1 is an effective teaser and Fig. 4d is a nice diagnostic.

Overall. An original and carefully executed framing with honest reporting, but the central empirical claim is close to built-in by the reused-command protocol, the competing invariants are weak, the samples are small, the significance is undercut by the authors' own finding that contact-aware protocols largely remove the error, and the Stage-2 physical validation is not designed. I lean toward rejection at this stage but could be persuaded given the venue's tolerance for preliminary results.
