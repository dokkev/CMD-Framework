ASSESSMENT NOTES:
I read this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methodology reviewer. The paper makes two claims about a planar two-DoF servo finger. C1: a remotely actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage C̄_pad than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. C2: if an impedance setpoint is reused across redesigns, the error in a measured interface effect follows the change in contact-surface motion Δ_contact, which is the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame. It does not follow the skeleton change, so a redesign that keeps Δ_contact lets an interface be studied independently of the finger.

C1 is mostly geometry and is well supported. The pole formulation (Lemma 1, Props. 1-2) explains the trends cleanly: a fixed axis's gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of depth, and the CFL's virtual pole follows the face. Study 1 checks this with GA/NSGA-II over three seeds per architecture. The worst-of-six run still beats 2R-DD in 9 of 10 cells, and the ablation (2R-remote) separates the effect of remote actuation from the effect of the polycentric joint. Fig. 3 and the 0.05 mm median agreement with Eq. (2) are convincing. The main caveat is that mechanical advantage is left unconstrained. The CFL's worst-case 0.41-0.58 is close to a rolling joint and is stated openly, but the abstract and the C1 wording do not carry that qualification.

Prop. 3 and Corollary 1 are correct and simple: |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. They give a useful closed-form floor for fixed-axis and fixed-roller joints, and Study 2 shows that the CFL beats this floor (10-51% of it) at almost no cost in coverage. This part is solid.

C2 is the novel and more interesting claim, and my main methodological concerns are about it.

(1) Contact model. Pads are meshes in point contact with a cylinder, and "pad stiffness" is the normal stiffness of the contact. Under a position setpoint at stiffness K0, the normal force at the setpoint is roughly set by how far the face sits into the object, which is a geometric quantity Δ_contact directly controls. Friction enters through an elliptic cone as a multiplier on normal force. In this model, it is close to expected by construction that the holding-load error follows Δ_contact. The partial correlations help. Holding the setpoint force fixed still leaves ρ = 0.64, and holding the normal offset fixed leaves 0.69, so the effect is not only "nearer face presses harder." The robustness checks also help: contact stiffness ×10, friction impedance ×3, substeps doubled, and a 100-fold range of K0. Still, a real compliant pad has a growing contact patch, distributed pressure, conformity, torsional friction and viscoelastic hysteresis. Thickness and stiffness are physically coupled, and none of this is represented. The Fig. 1d headline ("reports +48% when the truth is −41%") is therefore a statement about this model, not about soft pads.

(2) Ground truth. The "true" interface effect is defined as the soft pad's stiffness on O's own 3 mm geometry. That separates stiffness from thickness, which is only possible in simulation. It is unclear how the Stage 2 physical experiment will get the corresponding ground truth. The stated falsification test (a Page trend of E with Δ_contact over R, M, U) needs E, and E needs Δ(O).

(3) Statistics. Using pre-registration, one-sided permutation tests, a hand-level bootstrap and Holm correction is better practice than usual in this area, and I credit it. But the hands are not independent units. They are redesigns of one O, chosen at set Δ_contact and nested by thickness. With n = 14-15, the bootstrap intervals are optimistic, and "pre-registered" is not externally verifiable. The 45-hand grid is better. Its Δ_skel ρ = 0.22, against 0.86 for Δ_contact, is the cleanest evidence.

(4) Scope. Table I shows the result depends on the protocol. Under an ideal torque, Δ_skel predicts as well (0.71). Under each-finger-closed-from-touch, Δ_skel predicts better (0.88) and Δ_contact is not significant (0.44). The paper says this, but the abstract's "for the interface to be studied on its own" reads more generally than "under reused position setpoints, for one grasp type." There is also one object class (a cylinder of two radii) and one two-phalanx pinch, all planar.

(5) Measurement resolution: one load step is 2.5-5.6% of O's effect, and the design tolerance is E ≈ 20%. This is acceptable, but at the low-Δ_contact end, E may be quantized.

For a Stage 1 extended abstract, the problem, hypotheses (H1-H3), methods and Stage 2 falsification criteria are fully specified, with numeric predictions (21/72/124%). This meets the guideline well. Clarity is the weakest dimension. The text is extremely dense, and symbols and design labels (O, R, M, U, grid, reused setpoint) are introduced quickly. Table I's caption carries much of the method, and Figs. 2 and 4 have very small text.

My scores rest on: a correct, useful geometric analysis (C1, Props.); a novel framing of interface-study confounds through the contact Jacobian; a carefully controlled but model-limited contact simulation; and a well-posed physical plan. I lean accept as a Stage 1 abstract.
