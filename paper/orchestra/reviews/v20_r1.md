THOUGHT:
I read this as someone who has built servo-chain hands (servo-at-joint and belt-remote PIPs) and run pad and tactile comparisons on them. The paper names a problem I know well. On a LEAP/ROBEL-style hand the proximal phalanx is mostly servo body. A thick pad either costs range of motion or forces a redesign. Once you redesign, the pad comparison is mixed up with the new geometry. The authors split this into two claims. C1: a remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more room for pads than a servo-at-joint PIP. C2: a redesign should keep the contact Jacobian (the PIP column, i.e. how the distal face moves relative to the proximal phalanx), not the skeleton. That framing is useful and, as far as I know, new. Everyone in the field has seen a "softer pad" result that was really "the face moved closer under the same position target", but I have not seen it written down as a design invariant.

What convinces me:
- The mechanism in Study 3 is physically right and matches what I have seen on hardware. With a stiffness setpoint reused across hands, a face that advances toward the object over-closes and presses harder, which inflates the measured holding load. Fig. 1b/c and the 41% loss reported as a 48% gain (Fig. 1d) make the point clearly. That sign reversal is the strongest argument in the paper.
- Table I is careful. It has five candidate predictors, a hand-level bootstrap, Holm correction, a grid analysis pre-registered on 27 new redesigns, and partial correlations with Δ_skel held fixed. Pad thickness itself has ρ ≈ 0.1–0.2, so Δ_contact is not just standing in for thickness. The "other protocols" columns are an honest addition: under an ideal torque or touch-at-every-finger protocol Δ_contact no longer dominates, and the paper says so.
- The kinematics are elementary but correct. The finite pole is standard (Bottema–Roth). The 2 tan(q/2) gap growth per mm of face depth for a fixed axis (Eq. 2) is exactly the rule of thumb I would want a student to know. Eq. 7/8 give a clean lower bound that the CFL can beat because its virtual pole can move down with the face.
- For Stage 1, the hypotheses H1–H3 come with stated failure conditions, and the Stage 2 protocol says what would falsify C1 and C2. That is what the guidelines ask for.

Where I push back as a hand builder:
1. Is the problem forced? The obvious engineering fix is to recompute the setpoint per redesign from CAD. The paper reports that this brings the error down to 3–7%, but dismisses it because it needs "the object's pose and a model". Every designer has the CAD model. The object pose is known in a scripted grasp benchmark, which is exactly the setting the paper targets. The paper should state more precisely when setpoint recomputation is unavailable (learned policies transferred zero-shot, teleop data replay). Otherwise C2 reads as solving a problem that a known-geometry correction already handles better.
2. Mechanical advantage. A worst-case MA of 0.41–0.58 at the PIP roughly halves fingertip force from an already torque-limited hobby servo. The paper leaves this to "the payload", but for a hand meant to compare interfaces through holding load this is not neutral. Lower force changes which pad wins, because friction-limited and normal-force-limited failures trade places. I would like at least the achievable pull-out force of O, R and U next to the errors.
3. Pad model realism. The pads are represented by contact friction and a linear contact stiffness (2.5 → 1.0 kN/m) in MuJoCo-Warp. Real thick compliant pads and tactile modules (silicone over a gel, camera-based sensors) conform, enlarge the contact patch, and have depth-dependent stiffness. The over-closure effect is probably larger with real nonlinear pads, but that is unproven. Single-cylinder, single-contact-model evidence is thin for a general claim about "contact interfaces".
4. Scope of Δ_contact. It is the PIP column only, with MCP fixed at 0, a planar 2-DoF finger, and one base design O. The MCP column is explicitly not kept. In power grasps against the palm, the MCP column matters as much. The generality to a three-joint finger with a DIP (or coupled DIP), and to base designs other than O, is asserted but not tested.
5. Independence is partial. The paper sets an ad hoc 20% threshold. R still errs 14–26% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. For a true interface effect of −37%, a 20–40% relative error is still large. "Can be studied on its own" should be softened to "with error that scales with Δ_contact", or the threshold justified against typical interface effect sizes.
6. Coverage proxy. C̄_pad uses an inscribed cylinder at three poses, and the CFL ideal omits actuator bodies. The 2.4–14.6 point gains are believable, but I cannot tell what they mean for grasp success or tactile area.
7. Presentation. The text is very dense. Study 3's setup paragraph needs several readings ("grid", "placement", q_0, K_0, E(H)). Figs. 2–4 have tiny labels. Fig. 1 says R presses 0.7× and U 3.9× as hard; the text says 0.75× and 3.3× (median). Both may be right, as one example versus the median, but readers will trip on it.
8. Related work. Studies that compare fingertip pads or tactile sensors across hands, and their protocols, are only touched through [6]. Since the claim is about interface studies, one or two directly relevant cross-hand pad comparisons would sharpen the gap statement.

Overall: original framing, sound and honest simulation analysis, an executable Stage 2 plan, all simulation-only and with presentation that is hard going. For a Stage 1 extended abstract this clears the bar. I would accept at 6, conditional on the authors addressing the setpoint-recomputation baseline and the MA question in Stage 2.
