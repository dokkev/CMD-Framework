THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a robot finger must be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the invariant to preserve is the relative motion of the contact faces (the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame, equivalently the finite pole of the distal phalanx measured from the proximal face), not the skeleton. It supports this with (i) planar finite-pole results (clearance gap, coverage, |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|, a sum rule Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|, and a fixed-axis lower bound), (ii) GA/NSGA-II design searches comparing four PIP joint types on coverage (Study 1) and on Δcontact after redesign (Study 2), and (iii) an Isaac Lab simulation in which a command or torque is reused across 18 CFL redesigns and the error in measured friction/stiffness/shape effects is correlated with five candidate invariants (Study 3).

My intuitions:

1. The framing is fresh and genuinely useful. "The object meets the contact surfaces, not the skeleton" is a crisp point, and the sum rule (Eq. 7) is a nice, simple statement that a thicker pad forces a choice between keeping the skeleton and keeping the face motion. The math is elementary (rotation about a finite pole, |I − R_q| = 2 sin(q/2)) but correctly applied; I checked Eq. 4 against Fig. 2 (5 tan 60° = 8.7, 4 tan 60° = 6.9), the 3.46 mm/mm slope, and the 10–51% range against Fig. 4 (0.35/3.46 at 120°/5 mm; 4.31/8.49 at 90°/9 mm). Numbers are internally consistent. The cross-check of closed forms against the evaluator (1e-13, 0.05 mm median) is good practice.

2. The theory is not where the risk is. The risk is whether Study 3 shows something non-trivial. Under reused position commands with a stiff joint (K0 = 5 N m/rad), a change in where the distal face goes at a given q is, almost by definition, an interference with an object placed where O touches it, which becomes a grip-force mismatch, which for a friction-limited pull-out load directly scales the measured Δµ effect. So "error follows Δcontact" under the command protocol is close to a restatement of the geometry (Fig. 1: U presses 3.9× harder). The authors seem aware of this and add the reused-torque protocol, where forces are matched within 0.89–1.26× and ρ = 0.75 [0.38, 0.93] remains. That residual (where on the faces the object sits, and hence moment arms) is the genuinely informative result, but it rests on n = 16 CFL hands, one object family, one planar setting, and the interval is wide. The paper should lead with this and be explicit about what is mechanical consequence vs. finding.

3. The practical motivation is undercut by the paper's own baselines. Force matching leaves 3–7% and torque reuse leaves 3–40%, versus R's 3/21/41% under command reuse. Softer actuation shrinks U's error from 119% to 44%. A reader will conclude: use torque/impedance control and the redesign matters much less. The argument that touch- or force-based protocols "need sensing that the interface itself changes" is not convincing for joint-torque (motor current) based protocols, which do not depend on the interface. The claim that "the clean protocol reuses one set of commands" is asserted, not argued from the literature.

4. Scope of the invariant. The invariant is the proximal–distal relative face motion with MCP fixed, tested on a cylinder held between the proximal and distal pads (a hook/power grasp). Many interface studies (tactile fingertips, GelSight-type sensors, pinch with a thumb) care about the fingertip pad's motion in the hand frame, which this redesign explicitly does not keep (the MCP column, whose lever includes pad depth, is not kept; Δskel can grow to 7.7 mm). The paper should say clearly which class of experiments the invariant protects. The limitation paragraph mentions the MCP column but undersells this.

5. Study 3 only contains CFL redesigns. The link "fixed axis cannot keep Δcontact" (theory/Study 2) and "Δcontact predicts error" (Study 3, CFL only) is inferential. No fixed-axis or RCJ hands are in the simulation, so the headline conclusion "a fixed axis cannot do this, while a CFL can" is never tested end-to-end on the measurement error.

6. Overstatements. The Conclusion says "a fixed axis cannot do this"; Corollary 1 says it can if the pad is recessed (Δd = Δh), and the 2R-DD result in Study 2 shows exactly that (servo body holds the face deep, falling below the bound). The real claim is "a fixed axis cannot do this when the mount depth is fixed by joint hardware". Similarly C2 says the CFL keeps more contact surface than a servo-at-joint finger, which is true vs 2R-DD, but on the paper's own stated objective C̄_pad, 2R-remote beats CFL at 90° for all thicknesses and at 120° up to ~5 mm (Fig. 3). Switching to "pad length over fixed L_f" to claim CFL leads beyond 3 mm is metric switching and should be handled more carefully, given "Coverage is the objective of every design search."

7. Fairness of Study 2. The CFL has more free design parameters than 2R-remote (whose only freedom is the axis location, so it meets the bound exactly, as the corollary predicts). That more DoF gives more ability to match a trajectory is expected; the paper should frame Study 2 as confirming that the CFL has the necessary freedom within constraints, not as a competition. Also Fig. 4d shows 2R-DD and RCJ start with Δc(0) ≠ 0 (contact point moved along the face), so their Δcontact includes an offset that Prop. 3's "same c0" assumption excludes; a re-referenced comparison would be fairer. Relatedly, preserving a point path with prescribed rotation (φ = q) is classical rigid-body guidance with timing; Burmester-style finite-position synthesis is the natural baseline/tool and is not mentioned.

8. Statistics. Five candidates × three effects × two protocols with n = 14–16 and non-random, author-constructed redesign sets (including "redesigns at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible"). Because Eq. 7 anti-couples Δcontact and Δskel, the negative skeleton correlation (−0.40, −0.51) is partly forced by how the set was built; this is not evidence against the skeleton per se. The partial correlation (0.88) helps. Transmission is compensated by the transmission table before the test, so its near-zero ρ is by construction and should not be counted as a "candidate that fails". Pad thickness reaching 0.50 under torque reuse deserves comment. The differing hand counts (15/14/16) need explanation. Preregistered gates are a strength.

9. Interface model. Pad stiffness and friction are contact-model parameters; thick pads are not modelled as deformable bodies. For a paper whose subject is thick compliant interfaces, bulk compliance (which changes effective contact location and wrap) is exactly the physics that could break the geometric invariant. This is acknowledged but is a large gap.

10. Error metric. E = |Δ(H) − Δ(O)|/|Δ(O)| defines O's effect as ground truth. But the real question of an interface study (thin vs thick interface) cannot be tested this way, because thickness itself forces redesign; the paper substitutes secondary interface changes (µ, stiffness, crown) as surrogates. This surrogate logic is reasonable but must be stated explicitly; currently the reader has to infer it. Also, the relative error can inflate when |Δ(O)| is small for some placements/directions; median helps but report the distribution.

11. Stage-1 requirements. Methodology for the stated contributions is reasonably fleshed out for C1–C4, but there are no explicit hypotheses, and the Stage-2 plan (C5) is a single paragraph: no face-tracking method, accuracy target relative to the ~0.4–4 mm effects, number of trials, object set, or decision criteria. "Disagreement measures what real pads and manufacturing add" is too thin as the negative-result insight the venue asks for.

12. Writing. The paper is compact and has a clear narrative, but the "short sentences" style frequently becomes cryptic: many symbols/terms are used before or without definition (q_req, L_vp, x0, S, "gates", "transmission table", "impedance ratio", O/R/U appear in Fig. 1 before the text defines them). The abstract sentence "the path of a single point sets it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" is ambiguous. The title is too long and slogan-like. Fig. 1 caption says 3.9× (single placement) while the text says 3.3× (median) without flagging the difference; Fig. 4 caption says "top triangles" while the plotted markers are squares. Section V's flow ("Not only through over-closure" splits across the float) is hard to follow. Related work misses linkage motion-synthesis literature and polycentric/ICR-matching finger design, both directly relevant.

Overall: an original and well-motivated idea with clean, correct elementary theory and a careful simulation, but the central empirical claim is partly built in under the command protocol, generality is narrow (CFL-only, planar, proximal–distal grasp, contact-model pads), the practical case is weakened by torque/force baselines, and the Stage-2 methodology is underspecified. Borderline; I lean reject but could be persuaded given Stage-1 allowances.
