ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and how well each is supported:

1. Theory: when an interface changes, the thing to keep is the contact-surface motion (the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton. The paper derives three results:
   - Eq. (3): ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Δp|| for two planar rotations by the same angle q about different poles from the same c0. This is correct and elegant. It shows that one point's deviation measures the whole distal surface's deviation.
   - Eq. (4): Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|. This follows from the triangle inequality, because F_p translates by Δt relative to F1. Two hidden assumptions:
     - Δskel uses Eq. (3), which needs the same initial fingertip point, and the paper does not state that.
     - Δcontact/Δskel in Eq. (7) are maxima over q, while the bound is taken at q_req. That is fine as a lower bound.
   - Eq. (5), the fixed-axis bound, depends on a design assumption ("pin and lugs must sit above a full-width pad's mount"), not on kinematics alone. So "a fixed axis cannot keep the motion" is a statement about one packaging constraint, and the paper presents it as close to a theorem.

   The analysis is sound but shallow. Its main value is the framing and the clean incompatibility result. It does not supply a synthesis method; the redesign is still found by NSGA-II.

2. Design studies: CoDe gives more coverage than an at-joint finger in all 10 doubly-feasible conditions, by 2.4–14.6 pp. It preserves contact motion best in all 9 conditions. The evidence is reasonable (Fig. 3), but it is optimization-only and CAD-level. Remote actuation alone matches CoDe at 90° and at ≤5 mm/120°, so the articulation's own benefit is narrower than the abstract suggests.

3. Main empirical claim: the error in a measured pad effect follows Δcontact, not Δskel. Support:
   - ρ = 0.94 pre-registered.
   - Partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δskel held fixed.
   - Partial ρ 0.80 with force deviation held fixed.
   - Two simulators and two contact models.
   - The Skeleton control.

   This is the strongest and most careful part. My concerns:

   (a) The reference effect is defined as O's effect with only the contact parameters changed. In reality a thicker pad's effect includes its geometry. Defining the "true" pad effect as the material-only effect on O's geometry largely builds in the conclusion that preserving O's contact kinematics is correct. The Skeleton control answers which departure from O matters. It does not answer whether O-referencing is the right question for interface evaluation.

   (b) No pad deforms. A "thicker, compliant pad" is a softer contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer. The motivating case, thick compliant skins, is exactly where deformation moves the contact point, which the authors acknowledge. So the central claim is untested in the regime it is meant for.

   (c) Sample sizes behind the ρ values are never given:
       - how many redesigns are in the correlation set;
       - how many placements;
       - how many redesigns sit behind ρ = −0.98 in Fig. 5.
       Spearman ρ over a handful of points carries little information. In Fig. 4, two of the four Drake tasks have ρ = 0.53 and 0.43, which is weak support for "the four Drake tasks keep this ordering". The ordering holds, but the correlation is weak.

   (d) Re-referencing the setpoint cuts the Drake error from 42% to 14%. A simple calibration therefore removes most of the problem for command reuse. That weakens the practical case for a dedicated kinematic redesign, though not for policy transfer.

   (e) In Table I, CoDe shows exactly 0% median signed error in several settings. Its E is 10% at 7 mm, so these are medians of spread distributions. The table should show dispersion. Coverage's torque cell is also 0%, so the "eleven of twelve" count should be stated carefully.

   (f) "Pre-registered" is asserted with no verifiable record.

4. Policy transfer (Fig. 5): the pattern is task-dependent and the authors are candid about it.
   - Finger walk follows Δcontact.
   - The relay dial follows the skeleton, and the paper places it outside the claim.
   - The cube follows palm shift.

   With two seeds and three tasks, this is illustrative rather than conclusive. The cube result is driven by Δ_MCP / palm shift, a third quantity, which complicates the clean "contact, not skeleton" message.

5. Systems contribution: the "CoDe Hand" and the "modular finger for diverse pads" appear only as CAD and simulation. There is no hardware, no build, and no demonstration of an exchangeable interface. For RSS, a hand-design paper with no physical prototype and no deformable-pad modeling is a significant gap.

What my scores rest on:
- The conceptual question, which property to keep when the interface changes, is novel and well posed. The pole-based analysis is neat, and the confound control (the Skeleton hand, partial correlations, force-matched torque) is better than typical.
- However, the evidence is simulation-only with non-deforming pads, sample sizes are hidden, two Drake tasks give weak correlations, and the reference definition partly presupposes the answer.
- The writing is very dense. Many terms are defined in passing, and the paper is hard to follow on first read.

I lean to reject, but I could be persuaded.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The prose is extremely compressed. Many key sentences pack two or three claims; see the abstract and Sec. III-A after Eq. (4) ("Depth matters on its own ... keeps where contact happens on the hand"). Splitting these sentences would help first-time readers a lot.
2. "Conventional" in Fig. 1 is the Coverage hand, i.e., the same crossed four-bar. Readers will assume it means a servo-at-joint hand. Use "Coverage" consistently, or say plainly in the panel itself that it is the same mechanism.
3. The intro says Skeleton "keeps the skeleton exactly", but Δskel = 0.3 mm, not zero. Say "closely".
4. Eq. (1) uses complex notation (y ↦ e^{iq}y + b) while Eq. (3) onward uses vectors and R_q. Pick one or state the identification.
5. Eq. (6) writes L_f(M, q), but L_f is defined as a fixed extended length shared by all designs. Remove the arguments or explain the dependence.
6. Δ_MCP is measured in F1 and has no direct relation to the MCP joint's motion. The name is confusing; "palm-relative contact deviation" or similar would be clearer. Fig. 5's "palm shift" seems related but differs; state the relation.
7. Eq. (7) appears in Sec. III-C, but Δ_MCP is first used in Sec. III-A ("defined in Eq. (7)"). Consider defining all three deviations once, before Eq. (4).
8. Sec. III-A's "Room" paragraph: Fig. 2(a) shows that the rolling joint's pole lies at most r tan(q/4) below the contact point, but the main text does not derive or explain this. Add one line.
9. The Fig. 3(c) y-axis "contact motion kept (%)" can be negative and its normalization depends on Eq. (5)'s bound. The caption explains this, but the at-joint points above 0 "from a different initial contact point" are confusing. Consider marking them differently.
10. Fig. 3(a): the per-thickness scatter is small, and the "×: no feasible design" markers at 150° are hard to see. Add numeric labels or enlarge.
11. Fig. 4: the numeric annotations (E point/hydro, Δcontact, Δskel, ρ) under each plot are tiny and colour-coded with no column headers. A small table would be more legible. The sample size behind each ρ should be stated in the caption.
12. Table I: add the number of placements, plus an IQR or CI. Clarify why some CoDe entries are exactly 0%. Make the column grouping ("Other commands" vs. the pad-change columns) clearer, e.g., say which pad change the "Other commands" columns use.
13. Sec. IV-A "Measure" defines "error" and E in-line in a dense paragraph. A displayed equation for the error and for E would help.
14. The difference between "median of loads" (Fig. 1(d)) and "median of per-placement errors" (Table I) is explained only parenthetically. Readers will try to reconcile −39% vs. 0%; state it in the Fig. 1 caption.
15. "Pre-registered" needs a footnote saying where and when the registration was made.
16. Sec. III-D says the "labelled CoDe in the figures" architecture is the crossed four-bar, while "CoDe" also names a specific redesigned hand. The double use of "CoDe" (architecture vs. hand) is confusing in Fig. 3; use e.g. "four-bar" for the architecture.
17. Fig. 2(b) is low-contrast: thin grey lines over grey CAD. The crossed links and pivot labels are hard to read in print.
18. Fig. 5: the "quantity it depends on" row mixes Δcontact, Δskel and palm shift with no header. Label each and give the values' meaning in the caption more explicitly.
19. Contributions bullet 1 ("a crossed four-bar keeps most of it at near-best coverage"): quantify "most" (e.g., Δcontact = 1.1 mm vs. 3.9 mm), as Fig. 1(c) does visually.
20. Fig. 1(b) shows "interfaces it can carry" (tactile, compliant, traction, gel pad), but none of them is evaluated. Either tone the panel down or label it as intended use.
