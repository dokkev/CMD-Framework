ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper asks which kinematic property a robot finger should keep when it is redesigned for a thicker contact interface. Its answer is the motion of the contact surfaces relative to each other (the "contact Jacobian" in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton (joint axes and fingertip path). It makes three groups of claims.

(1) Analytical claims (Sec. III-A). These are clean and, as far as I can check, correct under the stated assumptions:
- Eq. (2): the gap cost of 2 tan(q/2) per mm of depth, 3.46 mm at 120°, is right.
- Eq. (3): Δc = (I−R_q)Δp, so |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. Correct, but only for the same initial contact point c0.
- Eq. (4): the pole shifts seen from F_p and F_1 differ by Δt, which gives Δ_d^p + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|.
- Eq. (5): a fixed axis above the pad mount gives Δ_d^p ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|.

These bounds rest on several assumptions:
- Both designs share the initial contact point and the initial fingertip position.
- Both contact surfaces deepen by the same Δt.
- The pivot of a fixed axis cannot be lowered.

The paper's own Fig. 3(c) shows at-joint designs above the fixed-axis line because they move the initial contact point. So "a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion" is a conditional statement, not an impossibility, and the definitions in Eq. (7) explicitly allow the initial point to move. The mathematics is classical planar finite-displacement kinematics (Bottema & Roth). The novelty is in the framing, not in the tools. The framing is useful, but the contribution bullet presents it more strongly than the analysis supports.

(2) Design claims (Sec. III-D):
- Room: the crossed four-bar covers more than the at-joint finger in all 10 jointly feasible conditions, by 2.4–14.6 pp.
- Preserved motion: it keeps the most motion in all 9 conditions.

The room comparison against the remote (belt-driven revolute) baseline is the right control. It shows that much of the room benefit comes from moving the servo off the joint, not from the four-bar: at 120°/7 mm the scores are 70% for remote vs 73% for CoDe. The four-bar's distinctive advantage is preservation (Fig. 3(c)), and only at 150° in room. All of this is optimization in a planar model with the MCP fixed. No physical finger is built or measured, although contribution 2 is "a modular finger".

(3) Empirical claims (Sec. IV). This is where the paper is strongest and also where my main concern lies.

Strengths of the evidence:
- The pre-registered correlation of error with Δ_d^p (ρ = 0.94 over 27 grid redesigns).
- Partial ρ of 0.86–0.91 controlling for Δ_skel, and 0.80 controlling for force deviation.
- Replication across Isaac Lab/MuJoCo-Warp and Drake (point and hydroelastic contact).
- The Skeleton control, which errs as much as Coverage or more (222% vs 124%).

Together these support the relative claim well: within these simulators, the error in a reused-setpoint measurement follows contact-surface motion, not fingertip-path motion.

Concerns:

(a) Reference and definition. The "true" pad effect is defined as the effect measured on O, with the new pad's contact stiffness but O's own 3 mm geometry. A redesign that reproduces O's contact-surface motion should then reproduce O's measurement almost by construction, because the contact model sees the same contact kinematics. The Skeleton control shows that the alternative hypothesis fails, which is valuable. But the paper never argues why "the effect on O's geometry" is the interface's own effect. A physically thicker pad on a real hand changes contact geometry, and arguably that is part of the interface's effect. The central question ("is the pad's effect measured?") therefore depends on a choice of reference that should be defended explicitly.

(b) No deformation. The motivation is thick compliant pads and skins. Yet neither contact model deforms a pad: "thicker, compliant" is just a softer contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer. In most rows the changed pad property is friction (1.3 → 2.0). The Limitations note that a deforming pad moves the contact point. That is exactly the mechanism that could break the claimed invariance, and it is untested.

(c) Hardware. There is no hardware evidence at all, despite a CAD hand in Fig. 1. For an RSS hand-design paper this is a significant gap.

(d) Weaker results in Fig. 4 and at 9 mm. The ρ values in Fig. 4 for the dial (0.53) and box pivot (0.43) are weak with n = 18 and are given without significance tests. The 9 mm error (38%) shows the approach degrades quickly.

(e) Policy results (Fig. 5). These rest on two seeds with large seed-to-seed variance: cube rotation for CoDe is about 95% vs 55%. The finger-walk task does not transfer to Drake even on O. The claim "transfers to the redesign that keeps the motion its task uses" is supported qualitatively at best. Its strongest element is the palm-shift correlation (ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns).

(f) Practical relevance. Re-referencing the setpoint already cuts the Drake error from 42% to 14%. The case for kinematic preservation over recalibration therefore rests on learned policies, which is the weakest evidence.

(g) Exact 0% medians. Table I shows exactly 0% for CoDe in three MuJoCo columns. This suggests quantized pull-out loads, which may hide variance.

Overall: an original and well-posed framing, a sound small analysis, and careful, pre-registered simulation evidence for a relative claim. The reference definition is under-justified, pad deformation is absent, and there is no hardware. The policy evidence is thin, and the exposition is very dense. I lean toward rejection but could be persuaded.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The abstract and introduction pack many hedged clauses ("from the same initial contact point", "wherever both are feasible", "so it is not only a change of preload"). The core claim is hard to extract on first read. State the reference-hand protocol (same command, pad effect measured against O) plainly before the numbers.
2. The term "contact Jacobian" conflicts with its standard grasping meaning (joint velocities to contact velocities in a world/object frame). The paper also actually uses finite-displacement paths (Eqs. 3, 7), not a Jacobian. Either justify the name or use "relative contact-surface motion".
3. The notation is heavy and inconsistent across sections:
   - Δ_d^p, Δ_d^1 and Δ_skel.
   - Δp and Δp_1.
   - c and c_1.
   - E (median |error|) vs the median error.
   - h, h_i and Δt.
   - F, F′, F_p and F_1.

   Add a short notation box, or reduce the symbols.
4. Eq. (7) appears in the left column of page 3, but its defining text continues in the right column, with a stray "(7)" separated from the equation in the extracted text. Its placement after Eq. (6) and before Eqs. (8)-(9) splits the Design Optimization subsection awkwardly.
5. Table I: the columns "convex", "touch", "force", "setpoint" and the "Other commands" group are never defined in the text. The "compl. pad" columns of MuJoCo-Warp and Drake are not aligned with headers, and the empty cells are unexplained.
6. The 41%/39%/+48% numbers in Fig. 1(d) (medians of loads) differ from the Table I compl. pad values (median of per-placement errors: 0% vs +125%). The text explains this in one parenthetical, which invites confusion. Use one statistic, or label both clearly.
7. Fig. 1(c): the panel titles "contact-surface motion (contact Jacobian)" and "skeleton motion (fingertip)" overlap their plot areas. The caption claim "Coverage keeps neither fully" is hard to see, because Coverage's skeleton change is smaller than CoDe's.
8. Fig. 3(c): the y-axis "contact motion kept (%)" normalized by the fixed-axis bound, with values below −100%, is unintuitive. The caption's explanation of why at-joint designs lie above 0 is dense. Consider plotting Δ_d^p in mm with the bound drawn as a line.
9. Fig. 3(a)/(c) and Fig. 4: the tick labels, legends and the E/Δ/ρ annotations are very small at print size. The four marker colors in Fig. 3 are hard to tell apart in grayscale.
10. Fig. 4 caption: "ranks averaged" over both contact models is unclear. Say whether ρ is computed on pooled data or averaged across models, and give n and p-values.
11. Fig. 2(a): explain the shaded regions (what "CoDe" covers, the grey "contact faces, 3–9 mm pads" band) in the caption. Say why the rolling-joint region ends where it does.
12. Sec. III-D "Both problems are solved by evolutionary search [10]" gives no population size, generation count or convergence evidence. At least state that a fixed budget was used across architectures for fairness.
13. Sec. IV-A "Measure": the paragraph mixes the definition of D, the reference, the non-circularity argument, the noise floor and the pre-registration. Split it into definition, controls and statistics.
14. "Pre-registered" should point to a registration artifact (an anonymized link or supplementary material).
15. The sentence in Sec. III-A "Depth matters on its own: seen from F_1, the contact point deviates by at most Δ_d^p + |Δt| (Δ_d^1, Eq. (7))" is hard to parse. The parenthetical reference to a quantity defined later should be restructured.
16. Related Work is a single paragraph. Expand it with instant-center/polycentric joint design literature and cross-embodiment policy transfer, and position the classical pole analysis relative to them.
17. The Fig. 1(a)/(b) CAD and interface icons suggest a built system. State explicitly in the caption that no hardware results are reported, to avoid misleading readers.
