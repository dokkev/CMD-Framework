THOUGHT:

Intuition. The paper makes a clean conceptual point. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the designer should keep the motion of the contact faces relative to each other, expressed in the proximal-phalanx frame (the PIP column of a point Jacobian), not the skeleton or fingertip path. The supporting machinery is classical planar displacement kinematics: the finite pole (Euler/Chasles), reflection composition, and (I - R_q)Δp. There are three studies. Study 1 is a coverage-optimal design comparison of four PIP joint types. Study 2 redesigns each type from 3 mm to 5/7/9 mm pads while minimizing Δ_contact. Study 3 is a simulated interface-effect experiment showing that Δ_contact, among five candidates, ranks the error in measured interface effects. For a Stage-1 RSS abstract the hypotheses (C1-C4), their failure conditions, and the Stage-2 plan are stated explicitly, which I appreciate.

My background is mechanisms and kinematics, so I re-derived the formal results.

Lemma 1. (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}) is correct, and b = r_C(q) - R_q r_C(0) is correct for a body-fixed point. This is a textbook fact, correctly cited. Two wording issues. "The finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is loose: the two coincide in the limit q -> 0 for any mechanism. The abstract's "the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" conflates the finite (displacement) pole with the instantaneous centre. The distal phalanx of a four-bar does not physically turn about P(q).

Proposition 1 (clearance). I checked it with the half-plane test x cos(q/2) + (y - z) sin(q/2) < 0. It is correct: the binding point is the joint-side corner at face depth t, giving G/2 >= [t - z]_+ tan(q/2). The figure numbers check (5 tan 60° = 8.7, 9 tan 60° = 15.6, 4 tan 60° = 6.9; pad lengths 32/25/34 are consistent). The mirror argument also tolerates unequal pad lengths, because the joint-side corner region is shared. However, S2 is stronger than it reads. Pads are fixed in their links, so "the joint-side corners stay equidistant from the pole in x" must hold for every q. That means the x-coordinate of P(q) must be constant, at the midpoint of the gap. A general crossed four-bar's finite-pole locus moves in both x and z, so S2 is a constraint on the pole locus, not a symmetry convention. The validation reports a 0.05 mm median agreement on CFL designs. It should report the maximum, and how far x_P(q) drifts.

There is a more important framing problem in Prop. 1. "A fixed axis has z ≡ 0" holds only if the axis is placed on the link line. Nothing kinematic stops a fixed axis from sitting at the face (a palmar pin or flexure hinge), which gives zero gap. Fig. 2(c), "pole 5 mm below the link line", could be a fixed axis just as well. What the CFL really offers is a virtual pole with no hardware at it. That is a packaging and embodiment argument, and it should be stated as such. As written, Prop. 1 does not separate fixed axes from four-bars.

Proposition 2 (coverage). I re-derived the denominator. The phalanx ends are mirror images in the bisector line, so their distance is 2(G/2 cos(q/2) + z sin(q/2)). Hence L_f(q) = L_f - G(1 - cos(q/2)) + 2z sin(q/2), which is correct. I also checked the claim "lowering the pole raises C_pad until the pole reaches the face" at a single q with G at its minimum 2(t - z)tan(q/2). dC/dz ∝ (2 sin(q/2)/cos(q/2))(D - N), which is > 0 since C < 1. Once z > t, G = 0 and C falls. So the claim holds, nicely, but only pointwise in q. With the max over q in G and the average over Q_eval it holds approximately. The paper should say so.

Proposition 3 and Eq. (7). Δc = (I - R_q)Δp gives |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. This is correct and independent of c0 given equal c0, so one point suffices, as claimed. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality, since the face-frame and link-frame pole shifts differ by (0, Δt), evaluated at q_req. It is correct under S1 (equal Δt on both faces) and kept start points. I checked it against the Study 3 numbers. For R (7 mm): 0.9 + 7.7 = 8.6 >= 6.93. For U: 3.4 + 3.9 = 7.3 >= 6.93. For the skeleton-keeping 5 mm design, Δskel = 0.4 forces Δcontact >= 3.06, which matches Fig. 5. This is a genuinely useful little result: a thicker pad cannot keep both.

Corollary 1. The rolling-joint claim checks. For equal rollers the finite pole is (r, r tan(q/4)), so the link-frame pole path is fixed by r. The fixed-axis part, however, is close to tautological. "Faces move Δh - Δd away from the axis ... wherever the new axis is placed" amounts to "if the face-to-axis distance changes by δ, then Δcontact >= 2 sin(q_req/2)|δ|". That is Prop. 3 with a constant pole. The physical content is entirely in the premise that the axis cannot follow the face, either by moving palmar or by reducing d, because hardware pins it. The abstract presents this as something "we prove", which oversells it. It also follows that "2R-remote meets the bound exactly" is true by construction, since its mount depth is held, rather than an empirical finding.

Bigger-picture concerns.

(1) The spacer baseline. Design once for the thickest interface and back thinner pads with spacers. This gives Δcontact = Δskel = 0 and literally the same hand, which is the paper's own stated goal (comparability). It is excluded because it costs the thin pads 8.6-18.2 coverage points. But Study 3 shows coverage does not predict the error. For a comparability study, equal coverage across conditions is arguably a feature. The paper needs a stronger argument for excluding it. For example: the spacer changes the interface's backing and compliance stack, real thick interfaces (camera modules) cannot be "thinned", or the over-designed hand is impractical. Otherwise the CFL redesign looks like a solution to a self-imposed constraint ("pad mounted at the shallowest depth").

(2) The simulation model neutralizes the transmission candidate. The simulation uses one joint-space flexion stiffness K0 for all hands and reuses commands through each hand's own transmission table. For a real position-controlled servo, joint stiffness ≈ k_servo (dθ/dq)^2 and torque capacity scales with dθ/dq. The CFL redesign "absorbs the thicker pad by changing ... its transmission" while mechanical advantage is unconstrained. So the near-zero ρ for transmission in Table I is partly an artifact of the model, not a finding. Similarly, the reused-torque protocol reuses joint torque, not servo torque.

(3) The protocol fixes work better than the redesign. Closing from touch gives a median of 20%, and force matching gives 3-7%. R gives 3/21/41%, and reused torque gives 3-40%. Force matching thus beats the proposed design principle. The dismissal ("needs sensing that the interface itself changes") is not convincing for servo hands, where joint position and current are available independent of the pad. This weakens the significance argument and should be confronted head-on.

(4) The design population in Study 3 was built by the authors. Redesigns were made "at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". Through Eq. (7) this deliberately anticorrelates Δcontact and Δskel within each thickness, so the negative ρ for Δskel is partly structural. The partial correlation (0.88) helps. Still, 15-16 hands, one architecture (CFL only), one object family, a joint stiffness that strongly modulates the effect (119% -> 44% at K0/10), and an unspecified interval method make the "only predictor" claim fragile. The mechanism is also fairly mundane: a distal face that sits closer at the same command squeezes more. The authors themselves note that the face offset at the grasp predicts the error equally well (ρ = 0.92). The normal component of Δc to the distal face is the relevant quantity, not the full norm.

(5) A missing mechanism-design connection. Keeping c(q), i.e. the displacement poles of the distal phalanx relative to the face, is a rigid-body guidance (motion generation) problem: Burmester theory and finite-position synthesis (Sandor & Erdman; McCarthy & Soh). That literature gives (a) the fact that a four-bar matches a prescribed motion exactly at no more than five positions, so Δcontact > 0 is generic, and (b) a principled synthesis route that could replace or seed the GA/NSGA-II. Polycentric and self-aligning joint design (prosthetic knees, exoskeleton centrode matching) is also directly related and uncited.

(6) Fairness of Study 1. C2 is stated against the servo-at-joint 2R-DD, but the kinematically fair comparison (fixed axis vs. polycentric, same remote actuation) is 2R-remote. There the result is mixed: 2R-remote leads at 90° and at 3 mm, and crosses at 120°. CFL also gets an extra refinement step, and only three seeds are used per condition.

Presentation. The paper is dense but disciplined. The many symbols (x0, G, Lvp, T_i, S1/S2, Q_eval), the long title, and the hand counts that change between 15, 14, 16 and 18 without explanation make it harder to read than necessary. The figures are good. Fig. 2 and Fig. 4(d) in particular are very instructive.

Decision reasoning. For a Stage-1 RSS extended abstract, the problem, hypotheses and methodology are complete, the math is correct, and the empirical story is coherent and falsifiable. The theory is elementary, some claims are oversold (the "proof" of Corollary 1, "only predictor"), and modeling choices favour the conclusion. I lean towards a weak accept, conditional on the spacer and force-matching arguments being addressed and the Burmester connection being added.
