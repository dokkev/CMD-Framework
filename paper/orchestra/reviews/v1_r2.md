THOUGHT:
The paper argues that experiments comparing contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, vision-based tactile modules) on a robot finger are confounded by the finger redesign the interface forces. It proposes to hold fixed the "contact-centric kinematics" c(q), the path of a distal-face point in the proximal-face frame. It then analyses the joint-pad conflict through the classical finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement. The analysis gives a symmetric clearance law (gap grows as 2 tan(q*/2) per mm of thickness for a fixed axis), a coverage law, a contact-path law (|Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|), and a lower bound on the contact-path change of any fixed-axis redesign at given mount depth. Two planar optimization studies test H1 (architecture bounds coverage, and the spread grows with thickness and flexion) and H2 (only a pole-moving joint can be redesigned for thicker pads while keeping c(q) and coverage). H3 is a Stage-2 hardware protocol.

What I like: For a Stage-1 submission the framing is unusually disciplined. Hypotheses are explicit, each has a stated test and a stated meaning if it is false, and the authors report the cases where their own design loses (2R-remote leads at 90°, RCJ-ideal is among the best, CFL is only partial at 9 mm). The pole-based reading of the joint-pad conflict is elegant and gives a single footing for comparing revolute, rolling and four-bar joints. The fixed-axis bound (Cor. 1) is a clean, checkable reference scale. I checked the numbers that can be verified from the text: 2 tan 45°/60°/75° = 2.00/3.46/7.46. The Table I bounds match 2 sin(q/2)Δh. The Study 1 losses (2R-remote 0.815→0.633 at 120°; CFL 0.781→0.695) match Fig. 3. The analytic checks against the evaluator (1e-13, 1e-9) are a good sign of internal consistency.

My main concerns:
1. The central premise is asserted rather than motivated with evidence. The paper never shows that a contact-path difference of a few mm (or a pole shift) materially changes the quantities an interface study measures: grasp success, contact force distribution, tactile signals. Without that link, Dc is a well-defined geometric quantity of unknown experimental relevance. The authors explicitly decline to set any Dc tolerance, so H2 cannot really be falsified: "10–51% of the bound" is reported as support, but no outcome short of exceeding the fixed-axis bound would count against it. The bound itself is only like-for-like for architectures with Δd = 0. For an extended abstract whose core must "detail the insights under negative results", the absence of even a sketch of how Dc translates into measurement error is the biggest gap. H3 (iv) defers this entirely.
2. Trading one confound for another. The CFL "keeps" c(q) by changing its skeleton motion and its worst-case MA by −7% to +16%. Transmission ratio is exactly the kind of embodiment variable the introduction lists as a confound: it changes the fingertip force for a given actuator torque, so it directly affects grasp outcomes. The paper records it, which is honest. But the claim that CFL redesign reduces confounding then depends on an implicit weighting: a ~1–4 mm contact-path change is treated as worse than a ~10–16% transmission change. That weighting is never argued. 2R and RCJ keep MA exactly.
3. The objective and evidence are fragile relative to the claimed differences. C̄pad is a bespoke cylinder-inscribed, three-pose average whose relation to interface-study quality is not justified. The paper says pad dimensions are requirements, yet pad lengths are optimized. CFL optima are acknowledged not to be converged: runs spread 1.3–3.4 points, and re-seeding raised cells by up to 2.1 points. That spread is the same size as several reported H1 differences: 2R-remote leads at 90° by 1.9–4.1 points, and the 120° crossover is 1.1 points. The fairness of the cross-architecture comparison also leans on modelling choices that favour some architectures: 2R-remote occlusion and belt collisions are not counted, as are other-layer collisions in general, and the fixed actuator pose is chosen by hand. H1 itself ("losses must differ between architectures") is nearly certain to hold for any four distinct mechanisms. The informative part is the direction and mechanism, which Eq. (3) explains well, not the yes/no test.
4. The analytic laws hold only approximately for the actual optima. S1–S2 are violated by up to 3.4 mm for fixed axes and 1.6 mm for CFL. Those residuals are of the same order as the CFL Dc values being reported, so Props. 2–3 are explanatory rather than predictive, as the authors partly concede.
5. Novelty of the theory is modest. The finite pole, the Rodrigues/Chasles-type displacement formula and SAT separation are textbook. The new piece is applying them to pad clearance and contact paths, which is useful but short. Related work omits directly relevant lines: instantaneous-centre / virtual-centre and remote-centre finger joint design (e.g., polycentric prosthetic fingers, compliant cross-axis pivots), and modular or quick-change fingertip and gripper designs in which interfaces are swapped on a fixed finger (e.g., interchangeable tactile fingertips on commercial hands, modular gripper fingers). Those are the most natural baselines for "exchange interfaces without redesign".
6. The Stage-2 protocol is thin for the main payoff. It does not fix which interfaces are used, how contact-face motion is tracked and to what accuracy, how many trials are run, or which grasp protocol is used. The success criterion ("within repeatability of the reference interface") is reasonable but untied to any power analysis. The mount-depth rule (CFL keeps Δd = 0 thanks to a 3 mm pivot housing) is pivotal to the H2 result but is presented as a modelling rule rather than a design decision with its own cost.
7. Presentation is dense. Notation (Lvp, τi, Ti, x0, S1/S2, MAwc, P1–P4, H1–H3) is introduced faster than a 5-page abstract can carry. Fig. 1 shows pad-length percentages while the objective is a different number, which invites confusion. "2R-remote ... does not affect its objective (MA ≡ 1, occlusion not counted)" needs a clearer rationale.

Overall: the paper is a careful, well-structured Stage-1 proposal with honest reporting and a nice geometric lens. But its central methodological claim (that c(q) is the right invariant and that CFL preserves it better at acceptable cost) lacks a link to measurement consequences and quietly accepts a transmission change. Its quantitative comparisons are within search noise in several cells. I lean towards rejection but would not be surprised if co-reviewers value the methodological framing more.
