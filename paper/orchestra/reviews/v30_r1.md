ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: a robot-hand designer who has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them, and who reads this as someone who would have to build and calibrate the CoDe finger.

The paper makes two claims about one planar PIP joint.

(1) Room. A servo on the proximal phalanx driving a crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and flexion. The geometry is clean. Lemma 1 and Props. 1–2 explain why a pole that can drop toward the contact face shrinks the joint gap. Fig. 3a–c backs the claim: CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions, by 2.4–14.6 points. I accept the claim as stated. As a designer I note three limits on how much it means:
- The comparison that matters in practice is 2R-remote, and it matches or beats the CFL at 90° and 120° for 3–5 mm pads (Fig. 3a,b). The CFL's advantage is really at deep flexion and thick pads.
- Coverage is a geometric proxy (a cylinder inscribed in the V). It is never linked to a grasp outcome.
- The CFL optima carry a worst-case mechanical advantage of 0.41–0.58. On a torque-limited smart servo that is roughly half the PIP torque a direct-drive joint gives. "Left free and reported" understates a real cost.

(2) Keep the contact-surface motion, not the skeleton. The kinematic part is correct and neat:
- Eq. (7) and the bound in Eq. (8) show that a thicker pad cannot keep both motions.
- Corollary 1 gives a fixed-axis floor of 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|.
- The CFL reaches 10–51% of that floor at near-best coverage (Fig. 3d–g).

The empirical part is a simulation study under one protocol: an impedance setpoint reused across redesigns. Within that protocol the evidence is careful and broad:
- Spearman ρ of 0.80–0.86 for the change in contact-surface motion (Δcontact), against negative or weak values for the skeleton change (Δskel).
- Holm-corrected p-values.
- A second simulator, including hydroelastic pads.
- Two more open-loop tasks.
- Pre-registration.

The mechanism the authors give, though, is simple. Under a fixed position target, a face that moves changes the preload, and E ≈ |N_H/N_O − 1|. Three things weaken the headline as advice for hand designers:
- The protocols practitioners actually use for pad comparisons follow the skeleton, not Δcontact (Table I). These are re-zeroing at touch with a current or contact threshold (6–61% error) and force matching (3–7%, needing force sensing).
- The direct lead of Δcontact over Δskel is not significant with 14–16 hands.
- The pads in simulation are a point spring, or a hydroelastic layer that is deliberately 3 mm and 0.1 MPa on every hand. Pad thickness therefore enters only as geometry.

The "reversed conclusion" example (+48% reported vs −41% true) is striking. It is also exactly the failure that a touch-calibrated protocol avoids.

No hardware exists yet. The Stage-2 plan is concrete and falsifiable: a Page trend test with predicted values of 21, 72 and 124%. My worry is that the 0.9 mm Δcontact of design R is of the same order as servo backlash plus four-bar pin play at the fingertip. The plan does say it will measure this.

Presentation: the narrative and the theory are tight, and the figures are good. Section IV is very dense:
- The hand counts (18 redesigns; 15, 14, 45, 27 and 18 hands in Table I) are hard to reconcile.
- The row "Δcontact, Δskel held" is not explained.
- "partial 0.79", "given the soft pad's stiffness" and the redesign M in Stage 2 are not defined.

Scores: the theory is sound and the room result is solid. The second contribution is valid, but its practical scope is narrower than the framing suggests. It is a borderline accept for a Stage-1 extended abstract.
