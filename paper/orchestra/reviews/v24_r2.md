ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and evidence (read from the perspective of contact simulation and experimental methodology):

C1 / H1 (room for thick pads). A servo-driven finger with remote actuation and a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more of a cylinder-inscribed coverage measure than a servo-at-joint 2R finger at every pad thickness (3-9 mm) and required flexion (90/120/150 deg). The evidence is a GA/NSGA-II design search, three seeds per architecture, plus closed-form clearance (Eq. 2) and coverage (Eq. 3) laws. The result is clean: 10/10 conditions, a 2.4-14.6 point margin, the CFL's worst of six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9/10, and the 2R-remote ablation separates remote actuation from the polycentric joint. The closed-form gap law matches the optimized CFL gap to 0.05 mm median. This claim is well supported within its geometric model. Caveats: coverage is a purely geometric proxy (a cylinder touching both faces equidistantly from the V apex), mechanical advantage is left free (CFL 0.41-0.58 vs 1 for 2R-DD), and the 2R-DD baseline's mount depth is set by the authors' servo-body and collision rules. All three are stated.

C2 / H2 (keeping the contact Jacobian costs little). Eq. (6), |dc| = 2 sin(q/2)|dp|, and the fixed-axis bound (Eq. 8) are elementary planar kinematics, but they are correct and they frame the design question well. NSGA-II redesigns put the CFL at 10-51% of the bound, while a fixed axis free to slide its contact point stays at 0.59-0.79, at almost no coverage cost. This is convincing as a kinematic result. The comparison with 2R-DD/RCJ needs the c0-offset caveat given in the Fig. 4 caption ("CFL least in 7 of 9 cells" without the offset), which is honest but weakens "least in every cell" slightly.

C2 / H3 (reusing the impedance setpoint, the error in a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact). This is the claim the paper's significance rests on, and it is where my methodological concerns concentrate.

1. What the contact model can express. Pads are meshes in point contact with the cylinder, and "pad stiffness" is the contact's normal stiffness (MuJoCo-Warp soft contact). A pad interface therefore reduces to (a) a geometric face offset, (b) a scalar normal spring and (c) a Coulomb friction coefficient. Under a position target at stiffness K0, the stiffness effect is a series-spring effect on the normal force produced by over-closure, and over-closure is set by where the face sits relative to the object at the target flexion. That over-closure is close to what Delta_contact measures. So H3 is partly built into the mechanism the simulator can represent. The partial correlations help: setpoint force held fixed 0.64, face normal offset held fixed 0.69, and little added given Delta_contact. They show the full path c(q) matters beyond a single offset, but they do not remove the concern that the result is a property of point-contact penalty mechanics. Real thick compliant pads add contact area growth, wrapping, torsional friction, load-dependent friction and contact-point migration under compression. Those are precisely the effects interface studies measure, and they could interact with the redesign in ways this model cannot show. The scaling of stiffness as 1/h (2.5 -> 1.07 kN/m for 3 -> 7 mm) is a 1-D through-thickness spring assumption.

2. The size of pad compression against the tolerance. With 1.0-1.07 kN/m pads and grasp forces of a few newtons, compression is of order millimetres. That is comparable to or larger than the ~1 mm Delta_contact tolerance the paper proposes. The rigid-face kinematic Delta_contact may therefore not be the operative quantity on hardware, where the deformed contact point moves. The Stage 2 plan measures Delta_contact "with link backlash and pad compression", which is right, but the simulation does not test whether the tolerance survives once compression is included.

3. Statistics on a designed sample. Each setting has only 14-15 hands (45 on the grid), and these are not random draws. They are chosen redesigns (R, U, and designs at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton most), constructed partly to decorrelate Delta_contact from Delta_skel. Spearman rho and the hand bootstrap describe this constructed set, not a population of plausible redesigns. The within-thickness intervals are wide ([0.41, 0.99], [0.35, 0.98]). The pre-registration, Holm correction, one-sided permutation tests, explicit failure criteria and reporting of a failed prediction (the reversed share does not rise with Delta_contact) are commendable and unusual. But "pre-registered" is not verifiable by a reviewer, and several of the most persuasive numbers are post hoc: the signed normal offset rho = -0.99 and the partials 0.25/0.06.

4. Protocol scope. Table I shows that under the other protocols (reused flexion torque, closing each finger from its own touch) the skeleton change predicts the error as well as or better than Delta_contact (0.71 vs 0.69; 0.88 vs 0.44). C2 is therefore specifically a claim about position-target/impedance setpoint reuse. The paper says so ("under the setpoints servo hands run"), and the argument that servo hands rarely have the ideal parts other protocols need is reasonable. Still, the abstract's "keeping the skeleton exactly does not help" should carry that qualifier, because under force-based protocols keeping the skeleton is what matters.

5. The error metric. E = |Delta(H) - Delta(O)| / |Delta(O)| is a median over placements where both hands hold. It defines the reference finger O as ground truth, and the "true" interface effect is a counterfactual (O's geometry with the soft pad's stiffness) that exists only in simulation. For a real thicker pad, stiffness and thickness cannot be separated. Excluding failed holds is checked by a sensitivity analysis (0.81, 0.67), which is good. The load-step resolution (2.5-5.6% of O's effect) is adequate for the 20% tolerance but not for the "R errs 3%" figure. Loading rate and quasi-static conditions are not stated.

6. Robustness. Pairwise R < U under contact stiffness x10, friction impedance x3, doubled substeps and a 100-fold range of setpoint stiffness is a good check of solver-parameter sensitivity. It stays within one simulator and one contact formulation; there is no cross-check against FEM or a second engine.

7. Headline figure. Fig. 1d shows R tracking the true change at 5 and 7 mm but clearly under-reporting the loss at 9 mm (roughly -15% vs -50%, read from the bars). This matches the "independent up to about 7 mm" statement but is not reflected in the abstract. The U reversal (+48% vs -41%) is a striking demonstration. U is a coverage-only redesign, which is a plausible but somewhat favourable foil.

8. Stage 2. The physical protocol is concrete and falsifiable: O plus R, M and U at 7 mm, markers, a planar slide, bare vs textured covers, and a Page trend test. It changes friction only, which is the interface swap that can also be applied to O, so it avoids the counterfactual problem. However, with three redesigns Delta_contact and Delta_skel are perfectly anti-ordered, and any other monotone property across R -> M -> U (face normal offset, transmission, setpoint force) is confounded with Delta_contact. The trend test can refute C2 but cannot attribute a confirmed trend to Delta_contact specifically. "43 of 48 blocks" does not obviously match 5 placements x 4 directions.

Stage 1 compliance: the problem, hypotheses H1-H3 with explicit failure conditions, methods, insights under negative results (a failed prediction is reported) and the Stage 2 protocol are all fleshed out. The related work is short and focused. Presentation is the main weakness after the contact-model scope. The text is extremely dense: captions of Table I and Fig. 5 carry several results each, many defined symbols are packed into few lines, and readers must work hard to tell which numbers are pre-registered tests, which are post hoc, and which come from which protocol.

Scores rest on: solid, well-ablated kinematic contributions (C1, H2), a methodologically careful but model-bound H3 whose mechanism is partly built into the point-contact/position-target formulation, and a clear Stage 2 plan. For a Stage 1 extended abstract this clears the bar, with the contact-model scope as the main reservation.
