ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper claims that when a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the property to keep is the motion of the contact surfaces (the contact Jacobian in the proximal-pad frame), not the skeleton (joint axes and fingertip path). It makes three claims.

(i) Analytic (Eqs. 1–5). Finite-displacement pole geometry gives two bounds. First, Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|, so no redesign keeps both motions. Second, a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion from the same initial contact point.
- The derivations are short and, as far as I can check, correct. Eq. 3 is (I−R_q)(p−p′); Eq. 4 is a triangle inequality with a pure translation between F_p and F_1.
- But they rest on conditions that are stated once and then dropped: the same initial contact point c0, and a pure translation of F_p by Δt. Fig. 3(c) itself admits that at-joint designs violate the first.
- The bounds are elementary. The value is in the framing, not the mathematics.

(ii) Design. A crossed four-bar PIP with an off-joint servo makes more room than a servo-at-joint finger. CoDe beats at-joint in all 10 jointly feasible conditions, by 2.4–14.6 pp, and has the best contact-motion preservation in 9/9 conditions.
- This is well supported within the optimization study.
- However, "remote" (a belt-driven revolute joint) matches CoDe in much of the design space. This weakens the claim that the four-bar is what matters for room. The four-bar's main advantage is in motion preservation.
- The "modular finger" contribution exists only as CAD. Nothing is fabricated or tested, and the conclusion says "Hardware tests come next." For a hand-design paper at RSS this is a significant gap.

(iii) Empirical. When O's impedance setpoint is reused, the error in a measured pad effect tracks Δcontact (ρ = 0.94 pre-registered; partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δskel held fixed; partial ρ ≈ 0.8 with force held fixed), not Δskel. It holds across MuJoCo-Warp and Drake and across point and hydroelastic contact. Policies transfer according to the quantity their task touches.

Points the scores rest on:
1. **The ground truth is defined in a way that partly decides the result.**
   - The "true" pad effect is O's response when only contact parameters change (friction, stiffness) on O's own 3 mm geometry. A hand that reproduces O's contact-surface kinematics (Δcontact → 0) reproduces O's response almost by construction, because the simulated pad is just a contact parameter on the same surfaces.
   - The real question a practitioner asks is what a 7 mm compliant pad does to the grasp. Thickness is part of that effect, and the protocol defines it away.
   - The Skeleton control helps (it shows that matching the fingertip path is not enough). Still, the experiment mostly shows that matching contact kinematics reproduces contact outcomes, which is close to tautological in a rigid-body contact simulator.
2. **No pad deforms.** A "thicker, compliant pad" is modeled as a softer point contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer.
   - The headline example (Fig. 1(d)) is that the compliant pad lowers pull-out load by 41% on O. That runs against the usual physical intuition that compliant pads raise grasp robustness through conformation and a larger contact area.
   - So the reference effect that CoDe "correctly" reproduces may itself be a simulator artifact. The headline "reversal" on Coverage would then be a disagreement between two simulated artifacts.
   - The authors acknowledge non-deformation, but they do not discuss how it affects the sign of the reference effect.
3. **Missing baseline: re-mapping the command.**
   - Δcontact compares designs at equal joint angle q. A redesign whose contact path is a re-parametrization of O's would score badly even though a joint-space map q′(q) fixes it.
   - The re-referencing experiment (42% → 14%) shows that much of the error is removable by adjusting the command. That is exactly the cheap alternative a reader will ask about.
   - "A learned policy has no setpoint to re-reference" is not convincing. A fixed joint-space remap can wrap a policy's action output.
4. **The statistics are thinly reported.**
   - The number of redesigns behind each ρ is never given, and neither are the number of placements or a confidence interval.
   - "Pre-registered" has no registry or date and seems to be internal.
   - In Fig. 4, ρ is 0.53 and 0.43 for two of the four Drake tasks. That is weak, yet it is summarized as "keep this ordering."
   - The policy results use two seeds with large seed-to-seed spread (cube rotation: CoDe ≈ 95% vs 55%; Coverage ≈ 70% vs 20%).
5. **Some claims are overstated or not matched in Table I.**
   - The conclusion says "typical error near 10% up to 7 mm," but Table I shows +18%, −18%, +16% and −14% entries, and E reaches 18% in Fig. 4.
   - The torque column favors Coverage (0% vs +8%), yet CoDe is bolded throughout.
   - The Introduction says a redesign that keeps the skeleton exactly "misreports … as badly as one that ignores kinematics altogether." This partly follows from Eq. 4: Skeleton's Δcontact (6.6 mm) is larger than Coverage's (3.9 mm). It is the trade-off, not an independent finding.
6. **Scope is narrow.** The analysis is planar, only the PIP is redesigned, the MCP is fixed, and q_req is 120° for all experiments. Fingertip-end contact is excluded by the authors' own result.

Strengths:
- The question is clear and previously unstated: what must be invariant when the interface changes?
- The analytic lower bounds are clean.
- The design study is systematic.
- The control design is thoughtful: a Skeleton control, partial correlations against Δskel and force, two simulators and two contact models, a correlation set built to decouple Δcontact from Δskel, and a pre-specified analysis.
- Fig. 1 and Fig. 4 communicate the main idea well.

Overall, this is an interesting and original perspective with careful internal controls. The evidence is entirely simulated, with non-deforming pads, a ground truth that is partly circular, no hardware, and thin statistical reporting. I lean toward rejection.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The abstract uses jargon that only makes sense after Sec. III ("a fixed axis above the pad's mount", "same initial contact point"). Say it plainly: "a revolute joint whose pin must sit above the pad."
2. Naming is inconsistent. Fig. 1 says "Conventional" while the text says "Coverage"; "Coverage" is both a hand and a metric (C̄pad); "CoDe" is both the architecture label in Fig. 3 and one specific redesign. Pick distinct names.
3. Section III-D says the coverage-optimal four-bar "is also the Coverage hand of Sec. IV." But Sec. IV-A says Coverage carries the thicker pads and O carries 3 mm pads. Clarify whether O is the 3 mm coverage optimum and Coverage the 7 mm one.
4. E (the median |error|) is defined in passing inside the "Measure" paragraph. Define D, the error, the median and E in a displayed equation.
5. Table I is not self-contained. "convex", "compl. pad", "touch", "force" and "setpoint" are undefined, and the "Other commands" columns do not say which simulator they use. The bolding convention is unclear and looks inconsistent (torque column).
6. "The grid" (a grid of placements, or of redesigns?) and "the correlation set" are introduced tersely. State the redesign counts explicitly.
7. Fig. 4's bottom annotations (E point/hydro, Δ values, ρ) are very small and hard to read in print. The caption should say how many redesigns each ρ is computed over.
8. In Fig. 3(c), the y-axis "contact motion kept (%)" with values below −100 and a bound that does not apply to at-joint designs is confusing. Consider plotting Δcontact directly, with the bound as a reference line.
9. Notation: P(q) in Eq. 1 versus p(q) in Eq. 3. Δt is "how far the contact surfaces deepen" but is easily confused with pad thickness h. ΔMCP measures the position of the proximal surface, not the MCP itself, so the name is misleading.
10. Eq. 2's geometry ("pole at depth z(q) midway between their joint-side corners") needs a small inset figure. The "3.46 mm of gap per mm" number should show its derivation (2 tan 60°).
11. Fig. 2(a): the four-bar region is cut off at the top and the axis labels are cramped. The caption's "r tan(q/4)" uses r without defining it.
12. Isaac Lab and MuJoCo-Warp are used interchangeably (Sec. IV text vs Table I header). Say once that Isaac Lab runs on a MuJoCo-Warp backend.
13. The Introduction's contribution bullets are long and dense. "contact-surface motion" without hyphenation consistency, and "contactsurface" appears in the text extraction (a hyphenation artifact at line breaks).
14. Sec. IV-B1 packs too many numbers into one paragraph (Isaac Lab tests, the ablation, Drake, Table I, Fig. 1(d)). Split it into sub-points or a small table.
15. Fig. 5: the per-seed bars lack error indication. The quantity row mixes Δcontact, Δskel and "palm shift" in a way that needs a clearer header.
16. The Conclusion's "holding the typical error near 10% up to 7 mm" should match the reported range (4–18%) so the summary is not optimistic.
17. Reference [12] is an ICLR 2026 paper; check that the venue and year are final. Several references give "et al." for the first author only, with inconsistent page information.
