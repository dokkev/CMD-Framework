THOUGHT:

I read this as a mechanism and kinematics reviewer, so I checked every analytical statement before judging the studies.

What the paper claims. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should keep the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal contact face (c(q) in the proximal-phalanx frame F_p). It should not try to keep the skeleton motion. The paper argues that a fixed-axis joint cannot do this when pads are mounted at their own depth, that a crossed four-bar (CFL) can come close, and that in simulation the change in contact-surface motion, not in skeleton motion, predicts how much a reused-command measurement of a friction effect is corrupted.

Checking the analysis:

- Lemma 1. (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) J) with J = R_{pi/2} is correct: I - R_q has determinant 2(1 - cos q), and sin q / (1 - cos q) = cot(q/2). The fixed point of the displacement is the finite pole. This is textbook material (Bottema and Roth), and the paper cites it as such. The remark "the finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is also correct. Differentiating P = R P + b gives P' = R'P + R P' + b'. The velocity of the body point at P is R'P + b' = (I - R)P', which vanishes for q != 0 iff P' = 0, so the two coincide on an interval iff P is constant. The authors could add this one line. Notation clash: J denotes both R_{pi/2} (Lemma 1) and the Jacobian column (Eq. 2).

- Proposition 1. I re-derived it. Take the mirror line through P(q) with normal (cos q/2, -sin q/2). Take the palmar joint-side corner at (-G/2, -(t - z)) relative to the pole, with t = d + h. The corner stays on its side iff G/2 >= (t - z) tan(q/2). This gives Eq. (4), and the numbers in Fig. 2 check (5 tan 60 = 8.66, 9 tan 60 = 15.6, 4 tan 60 = 6.93). The convex-set and mirror-image argument is right: if K crosses the line, K meets the line, and those points are fixed by the reflection. The proof needs the positive part [.]^+ when the pole lies below the face. The extracted text shows a bracket that I assume is that. S2 has a strong consequence the text does not spell out: the pole's x-coordinate must stay at the gap midpoint for every q. A four-bar's finite pole moves in x as well as z, so Eqs. (4)-(5) are estimates for exactly the mechanism the paper promotes. The authors do acknowledge that S1 and S2 hold only approximately.

- Proposition 2. The denominator follows from the distance between an end point (-G/2, z) and its mirror image, G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). This implies that L_f(q) is measured along the link line, not along the face. The paper should say so, because "contour length" is never defined. I checked the claim "lowering the pole raises C_pad until the pole reaches the face" by substituting G = 2(t - z) tan(q/2). Then dN/dz = 2T and dD/dz = 2T(1 - c) + 2s = 2T, using the identity T(1 - c) + s = s/c = T. The sign of d(N/D)/dz is the sign of D - N, which is positive whenever C_pad < 1. The claim is correct, and the identity makes it a neat result that deserves one line of proof. One concern about the metric itself: normalizing by L_f(q) rewards designs whose contour shortens when flexed. The paper's own explanation of the Study 1 split ("2R-remote's gap shortens when flexed") is partly a denominator effect, not more usable pad. This should be discussed.

- Proposition 3. Delta c = (I - R_q) Delta p, and ||(I - R_q) v|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||v||. Correct. Note that p(q) is a pole path, not "a single point" as the abstract says.

- Corollary 1. This is where my main reservations lie.
  (i) The impossibility comes from the modelling rule, not from kinematics. The link line is defined through the joint axes, so a fixed axis has z = 0 by definition, and the face sits at depth d + h with d fixed by the "shallowest clear mount" rule. The pole-to-face distance therefore changes by exactly Delta h - Delta d. The "proof" restates this rule through Eq. (6). 2R-DD and RCJ fall below the dotted bound in Fig. 4 precisely by changing d. So "a fixed-axis joint cannot keep it" really means "a fixed-axis joint cannot keep it without reducing its hardware depth or adding a spacer". The abstract's "at their own depth" hides this.
  (ii) The corollary assumes c0 is kept, but the Study 2 optimizer evidently does not enforce this. Fig. 4d shows 2R-DD and RCJ starting apart at q = 0. Without that hypothesis the correct bound is weaker. With Delta c(q) = (I - R)Delta p + R Delta c0, the minimum over Delta c0 of max(|Delta c0|, |Delta c(q_req)|) is only bounded below by half of 2 sin(q_req/2)|Delta h - Delta d|, by the triangle inequality. Fig. 4 should either plot the bound that applies or state c0 as a constraint.
  (iii) "CFL keeps it within 10-51% of that bound" is a misleading phrase. The fixed-axis bound is not a bound on the CFL. The intended meaning is "the CFL's Delta_contact is 10-51% of what any fixed axis must incur at Delta d = 0".
  (iv) The rolling-joint extension holds. I verified that two equal rollers have their finite pole at (r, -r tan(q/4)) relative to the proximal roller centre, so z is fixed by r.

- A stronger result that the paper half-states. "The two pole shifts differ by the face's depth change, so a thicker pad cannot keep both." This implies a sum rule valid for any architecture: Delta_contact + Delta_skel >= 2 sin(q_req/2) |Delta t|, given that c0 and r_C(0) are kept. The paper's own numbers satisfy it. For R (3 -> 7 mm, q_req = 120 deg): 0.9 + 7.7 >= 6.93. For U: 3.9 + 3.4 = 7.3 >= 6.93. The rule is more fundamental and more general than Corollary 1, and it should be stated as the main analytical result.

- The sum rule has a direct cost for Study 3's interpretation. Within a thickness, Delta_skel and Delta_contact are kinematically forced to trade off. The marginal rho(Delta_skel, E) = -0.40 is then largely a consequence of that coupling plus the design selection ("chosen so that ... vary apart"). It is not evidence that skeleton change is irrelevant. The partial correlation given Delta_contact (-0.18) is the right test, and the authors report it, but n = 15 hand-picked designs is small. The headline "the change in skeleton motion does not predict it" overstates what the data can separate. The authors concede this for the pad-shape test but not for the main test.

Studies:
- Study 1. The setup is careful: idealized bounds, 0.05 deg re-verification, 1e-13 consistency check, honest disclosure that extra CFL search favours CFL by up to 2.1 points. The seed spread (1.3-3.4 points) is the same size as several reported differences (2R-remote leads by 1.9-4.1 at 90 deg), so the per-condition ranking is not statistically robust with three seeds. C2 is stated against 2R-DD only, and against 2R-DD it holds in all conditions.
- Study 2. Reporting the coverage end of each front is conservative for the CFL. For the other architectures, though, the rest of the front (Delta_contact versus coverage cost) is the informative object, especially given the spacer argument. Show full fronts, or at least the Delta_contact achievable at a 1-2 point coverage cost.
- Study 3. The mechanism is plausible but close to self-evident. Under reused position commands at stiffness K0, geometric interference at the grasp sets the normal force, and the friction limit load scales with it. The local face offset at the grasp predicts E better (rho = 0.92) than Delta_contact, which is a max over [0, q_req]. This suggests a grasp-local measure is the real variable and Delta_contact is a design-time proxy for it. That is fine, but it should be framed that way. Rigid contact makes pad thickness purely geometric, which isolates the kinematic confound but drops the compliance effects that make thick pads interesting. Force feedback removes most of the error (3-7%), and the authors fairly report this. It limits significance to position- or impedance-reused protocols, which are common in learned-policy evaluation. Small inconsistencies: p = 2e-4 in the text versus 0.0001 in Fig. 1d; n = 15 versus 16 between the two protocols (needs explaining); "3.9x" in the Fig. 1 caption versus a 3.3x median in the text (example versus median, but confusing next to "contact change 3.9 mm").

Venue fit (RSS Stage 1). The problem and methodology are well developed and some results are already in. The Stage-2 plan is a single short paragraph. It lacks the protocol, metrics, the number of interfaces and placements, how face motion will be tracked, and, as the guidelines ask, what is learned if the physical hand does not reproduce the simulation ordering. The hypotheses are implicit in C1-C5 rather than stated as testable hypotheses. Camera-based tactile modules, the motivating case, are thicker than 9 mm, so the paper should say whether the CFL is expected to keep Delta_contact in that regime.

Originality. Placing a polycentric joint's pole for clearance is known from prosthetic knees and cross four-bar fingers. The new part is the framing: an invariant (pole relative to the contact face) chosen for interface comparability, plus the skeleton-versus-contact distinction. The theory is classical kinematics applied cleanly, not new kinematics.

Overall. The analysis is correct as far as I can verify, and well-motivated. Corollary 1 is near-tautological given the mount rule, and it is misapplied when c0 is not kept. The sum rule should replace it as the headline. Study 3 supports the claim, with an over-reading of the Delta_skel result. Borderline, leaning accept for a Stage-1 extended abstract.
