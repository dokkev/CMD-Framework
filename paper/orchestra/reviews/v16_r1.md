THOUGHT:

I read this from the side of someone who builds servo-driven research hands and puts tactile and compliant pads on them. The two problems the paper names are real. On LEAP- or ROBEL-style servo chains a proximal pad has almost no room near the PIP. Comparing interfaces across hands that had to be redesigned to carry them is confounded. I have seen both problems in practice, and I like that the paper treats the second one as a measurement-validity problem rather than a mechanism-design curiosity.

What the paper offers:
(i) A compact planar-kinematics treatment built on the finite pole: a clearance condition (Prop. 1, Eq. 2), a coverage expression (Prop. 2, Eq. 3), the chord relation |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| (Eq. 6), the trade-off Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt| (Eq. 7) and a closed-form fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 8).
(ii) An optimization study (Study 1) that compares four PIP architectures over pad thickness and required flexion, with a 2R-remote ablation that separates "move the servo off the joint" from "make the joint polycentric".
(iii) An optimization study (Study 2) showing that the crossed four-bar can be redesigned for thicker pads while changing Δcontact by only 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound.
(iv) A simulation study (Study 3) in which the error in a measured interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) tracks Δcontact better than skeleton change, coverage, thickness or transmission. Thresholds are fixed in advance, tests are permutation tests with Holm correction, and there is a hand-independent grid and a soft-pad counterfactual.

Its strengths: the hypotheses are falsifiable and written with failure conditions, which suits a Stage-1 RSS abstract. The 2R-remote ablation is the right control. The Stage-2 hardware protocol is concrete: swap pad modules and CFL links on one finger, track markers on the faces, run a paired Wilcoxon with a stated prediction of 21% vs 124%. The statistics are more careful than usual for hand-design papers.

My main reservations, in order of weight:

1. C2's practical force depends heavily on the reused position-command protocol. Under a reused flexion command, Δcontact sets where the faces meet the cylinder, and so the squeeze force (distal force ratio ρ = 0.62; deviation from O's force vs error ρ = 0.94). That is close to a mechanical tautology: if the faces sit elsewhere at the same joint angle, a position-controlled stiff joint over-closes by a different amount. With O's torque reused, the error drops to 3–40% and R vs U narrows to 5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%. Matching contact force leaves 3–7%, near the load-step resolution. So most of the effect is an artifact of position-controlled grasp protocols. An experimentalist with force control or force sensing at both pads would remove most of it. The paper is honest about this, but the abstract and conclusion ("a redesign should keep its contact Jacobian, not its skeleton") read as a general design principle. It should be scoped explicitly to protocols that reuse joint commands or torques. Many policy-transfer settings do that, but tactile-sensor characterization usually does not.

2. No hardware, and the hand-design claims in C1 ignore the things that decide whether a servo hand works. Fig. 1a is CAD only. Mechanical advantage is not constrained in Study 1. The CFL optima have a worst-case MA of 0.41–0.58, roughly halving the PIP torque available from the same small servo compared with 2R-DD. For grasp-strength-limited hands, this is a real cost, and it is not part of the coverage comparison. Backlash and compliance of a servo-plus-crossed-four-bar chain, link-pin loads near the transmission-angle limits (30°/150°), and assembly tolerance of the virtual pole are not discussed. Pole placement sensitivity to manufacturing error matters for C2: the Stage-2 marker tolerance would tell us, but no estimate is given now. In H1, "CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both have a design" is a fair optimization comparison. Still, 2R-DD is a weak baseline for room at the PIP almost by construction (its servo body sits there). The informative comparison is 2R-remote, which already makes room at 90°.

3. The theory is elementary planar kinematics framed under a heavier name. "Contact Jacobian" here is the PIP column of a planar point's velocity, and Δcontact is a max displacement of a single distal-face point c(q) below the fingertip. Since φ = q on all designs, keeping J reduces to keeping the pole path relative to the face. That is classical centrode/finite-pole kinematics (Bottema–Roth), and Eq. 6 is the standard chord formula. The bound in Eq. 8 is useful and the framing is a nice packaging, but the theoretical novelty is modest. The title promises "keep the contact Jacobian", but the MCP column is explicitly not kept and only one point's path is matched. A multi-contact or proximal-face measure, or a check that a single point suffices for the cylinder grasp, would strengthen the claim.

4. Coverage is a proxy. C̄_pad is a geometric overlap of the two pads at the tangency points of an inscribed cylinder, averaged at three poses. It is the objective of every design search, but no grasp-level quantity (grasp stability, enveloping capacity, tactile area used in practice) links coverage to manipulation outcomes. Spacers are excluded "by assumption" although they give Δcontact = 0 at 8.6–18.2 points of coverage. For a lab that knows its interfaces in advance, that may be the preferred engineering answer, and this should be acknowledged more clearly.

5. The predictor comparison in Study 3 is partly shaped by how the redesigns were sampled. Redesigns are chosen "at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". That design decorrelates Δcontact and Δskel, which helps, but it also determines the correlations reported. Some candidates are weak by construction: transmission is undone by the per-hand transmission table, and thickness is coarse with only 3 levels. The 45-hand grid at 7 mm is the stronger evidence and should be foregrounded. n = 14–16 hands per setting with Spearman ρ and wide bootstrap intervals (e.g., [0.33, 0.99] for soft pads) is thin for "ranks first in every setting". The fixed-axis contrast family (95–228% error) is constructed so that Δcontact = √3 Δh is large. It shows the mechanism, but it is not a surprising result.

6. Related work misses close counterexamples to "interfaces gather at the fingertip": whole-hand tactile skins on servo/motor hands (e.g., uSkin/Xela on Allegro, iCub skin), and parallel/adaptive grippers with proximal pads. Remote-centre and virtual-pivot finger mechanisms are also relevant to the "pole on the contact face" argument. Under the Stage-1 rule a short list is fine, but the specific motivation claim deserves these.

7. Clarity. The prose is dense and telegraphic, with many named objects introduced quickly (O, R, U, M0, S1/S2, "contrast family", "O's contacts", "grid", "soft"). Table I's column headers need the text to decode. I had to read Sec. V-B twice to work out which protocol each number belongs to. Fig. 1(d) and Fig. 5 are informative. Fig. 3 legend text is small.

Stage-1 assessment: the problem, hypotheses and methods are complete, and preliminary evidence exists for every hypothesis, which exceeds the Stage-1 bar. The Stage-2 plan is concrete and decisive. My concerns are about scope and practical weight (protocol dependence, MA cost, single-point measure), not correctness. I lean accept, provided the C2 claim is scoped to command/torque-reusing protocols and the MA/strength trade-off is reported alongside coverage.
