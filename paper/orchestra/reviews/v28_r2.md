ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology (does each experiment test what it claims, and are the statistics adequate).

The paper makes three claims. (1) Room: a remote-actuated finger with a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint (2R-DD) finger at every pad thickness and required flexion. (2) Kinematics: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker pad, it should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), i.e. the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, and not the skeleton. A fixed axis has a closed-form lower bound on the change (Cor. 1, Eq. 9), and the CFL reaches 10–51% of that bound at near-best coverage. (3) Consequence: when a servo impedance setpoint is reused across redesigns, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact (Spearman 0.80–0.86 in Isaac Lab, 0.67–0.87 in Drake and other tasks), not Delta_skel. Uncorrected, this error can reverse the sign of a conclusion (U: +48% vs. true −41%). Stage 2 is a hardware test with O, R, M and U and a Page trend test.

Evidence for (1). The coverage metric (inscribed cylinder, three evaluation poses) is clearly defined. The comparison covers 10 conditions with three optimizer seeds, and the margins (2.4–14.6 points) are large relative to plausible optimizer noise. Convergence across seeds is not shown, and the "every thickness and flexion" wording rests on a GA/NSGA-II optimum. Still, the closed-form clearance law (Prop. 1, 2 tan(q/2) per mm for a fixed axis) gives a mechanistic reason to expect the result. I find this claim adequately supported for Stage 1.

Evidence for (2). Lemma 1 and Prop. 3 are textbook planar kinematics applied cleanly, and Cor. 1 is a useful, falsifiable bound. Some caveats:
- Delta_contact is computed at a single distal-face point c below the fingertip, as a max over q ≤ q_req. Prop. 3 says the whole column is kept only if the pole keeps its place relative to the face, but the optimization constrains only one point.
- The text admits that S1–S2 hold only "nearly" for the CFL.
- The phrase "0.59–0.80 of the bound that a fixed axis free to slide its contact point cannot beat" is hard to parse. I could not reconstruct where that floor comes from.

Evidence for (3) is the core of the paper and where my main concerns lie.

(a) Partly a mechanism restatement. The authors say the error "enters through the preload". Under point contact with Coulomb friction, the hold limit scales roughly as mu times normal force. A reused position target at stiffness K0 then gives a normal force set by how far the face sits from where O's face sat. So E, a normalized difference of friction effects, is to first order a normal-force ratio, and Delta_contact is a geometric proxy for that force error. The correlation is therefore expected; the informative tests are the partial correlations with force deviation held fixed (0.79 under torque, pre-registered; 0.64 at setpoint, post hoc).
- These partials deserve to be the headline, with the first-order analytic prediction E ≈ f(K0, Delta_contact, preload) plotted against the simulated E.
- Using the max over q of Delta_contact, when the grasp happens at a specific q0, is a noisier proxy than the local displacement at q0. Why the max was chosen should be explained.

(b) Contact modelling. The two pad interventions are a friction change (1.3 → 2.0) and a stiffness change (2.5 → 1.0 kN/m).
- Under point contact, the "stiffness" is a contact-solver parameter, not a pad constitutive law. The meaning of the stiffness change in MuJoCo-Warp/Newton soft constraints (solref/solimp, cone type) should be stated.
- In the hydroelastic runs every hand carries a 3 mm, 0.1 MPa compliant layer, so the 7–9 mm "thick pad" is only a geometric offset. The thick compliant pad that motivates the paper is never actually simulated as thick: no large deformation, no patch growth with thickness, no viscoelasticity. This is a defensible way to isolate kinematics, but it means the experiments test the setpoint-geometry pathway only. They do not test whether that pathway still dominates once real thick-pad mechanics are present, and the abstract's "also with compliant pads" wording overstates this.
- The "softer 7 mm pad" swap is "given the soft pad's stiffness", so it is also a parameter change rather than a modelled thick pad.

(c) Cross-simulator agreement is weak. Drake point contact gives rho = 0.67 (16 hands), and the hands rank only loosely alike across the two models (0.39). If per-hand errors are not reproducible across simulators, the per-hand E values carry substantial model error. The robust finding is the trend, not the individual values. This matters for Stage 2, which predicts point values (21, 72, 124%).

(d) Statistics.
- n = 14–16 hands per setting. The hands are not a random sample: they are constructed redesigns at set Delta_contact that "keep or change the skeleton most". The design of the sample therefore shapes the correlation and the collinearity among candidates.
- The authors honestly report that Delta_contact's lead over Delta_skel is not significant at 14–16 hands, and is significant only on the 45-hand grid. The 45-hand grid, in turn, mixes 18 redesigns with 27 more 7 mm designs.
- E is the median over placements and directions "where both hands hold". This conditions on survival, and how many cells are dropped per hand is not reported. If high-Delta_contact hands lose more cells, the median is biased.
- E normalizes by |Delta(O)|, so it is unstable when O's effect is small. Reporting Delta(O) and its spread would help.
- "Pre-registered" appears several times without saying where or what was registered.
- The Holm family is not defined.
- Bootstrap intervals are mentioned but not given numerically.

(e) Selected example. The headline "+48% vs. −41%" sign reversal is one hand (U). The paper should say how many of the 27 grid redesigns reverse the sign.

(f) Stage 2 plan. The plan is concrete and falsifiable, which is good for Stage 1. Weaknesses:
- Three ordered groups (R, M, U) give a Page test little power when slip-load repeatability on hardware is often 10–20%. E is a ratio of differences of differences, so its noise is amplified. No power analysis is given.
- Delta_skel falls while Delta_contact rises, which separates those two candidates. However, transmission, mechanical advantage, servo compliance and backlash are not controlled across R, M and U, and any of them could produce a monotone trend.
- Marker-based measurement of Delta_contact needs a stated accuracy relative to the 0.9 mm step.
- Servo stiffness at the "reused setpoint" depends on the firmware's P-gain and load, and the plan should verify K0 on hardware.
- The "bare and textured covers" intervention changes friction. A stiffness or thickness intervention closer to the paper's motivation is absent.

Presentation is very compressed. Several key sentences (the 0.59–0.80 floor, the "7 of 9 without ... start offset" clause, the setup paragraph defining the hand set) need multiple readings, and Table I mixes settings with different n. The figures are informative, although Fig. 4c's task sketches are small.

Overall, the problem is real and under-studied, and the analysis (pole, clearance, fixed-axis bound) is a crisp, transferable idea. The simulation programme is unusually broad for a Stage 1 abstract: two simulators, two contact models, three tasks and alternative protocols. My score rests on four points:
- the main correlational result is close to what the preload mechanism predicts, and the partial-correlation evidence beyond it is modest;
- the thick compliant pad itself is never simulated as such;
- per-hand cross-simulator reproducibility is low;
- the Stage 2 test is likely underpowered as specified.
These are fixable and appropriate for a two-stage review. I lean accept.
