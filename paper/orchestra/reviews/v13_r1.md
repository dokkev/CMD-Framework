THOUGHT:

Reviewer background: robot hand design and dexterous manipulation (servo-driven research hands, tactile fingertips, grasp experiments). I read all six pages, including Figs. 1-5 and Table I, and checked the equations against the text extraction.

What the paper is. A Stage-1 extended abstract with two contributions about one planar PIP joint (MCP fixed). C1: a remotely actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more room for thick proximal/distal pads than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD). The paper supports this with a pole-based clearance analysis (Prop. 1-2) and with GA/NSGA-II coverage optimisation over 4 thicknesses x 3 required flexions (Study 1). C2: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface, it should keep its "contact Jacobian" (the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal face) rather than its skeleton (the fingertip path). A fixed axis cannot do this unless the pad is recessed (Cor. 1, closed-form bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δh-Δd|). The CFL can come close (Study 2: 10-51% of the bound). In simulation (Study 3, Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp), the error in a measured interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) under reused commands or torques tracks Δ_contact and not Δ_skel, coverage, thickness or transmission. Stage 2 is a single physical finger with swappable pads and CFL links, plus a pre-registered failure criterion (R must err less than U).

My intuitions.

Strengths I value as a hand designer:
- The pole view is elegant and correct. I checked Lemma 1: (I-R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}). Prop. 3 follows from Δc = (I-R_q)Δp. The worked numbers in Fig. 2 check out: 5 tan 60° = 8.66, 4 tan 60° = 6.93, and 3.46 mm/mm at 120°. Linking pole depth to (a) the joint gap pads cannot occupy and (b) the contact-face motion is a neat, reusable design insight. It also explains in one line why a virtual pole, and therefore a crossed four-bar, is wanted under full-width pads.
- The diagnosis is real and under-discussed. Thick pads and tactile modules force a redesign, and the redesign confounds interface comparisons. Many tactile-hand papers do compare "interfaces" that come with different fingers.
- Hypotheses carry explicit failure conditions, thresholds were fixed before the runs, the tests are Holm-corrected permutation tests with hand-bootstrap intervals, and the Stage-2 protocol is concrete, with a hardware failure criterion. For Stage 1 this is above average.
- The authors report results that cut against them: 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90°, spacers achieve Δ_contact = 0 at a coverage cost, and without the start offset the CFL keeps the "least change" in only 7 of 9 cells, with the two exceptions costing coverage. They report these honestly.

Concerns, in order of weight:

1. Study 3 is partly tautological, and the headline number is mostly a protocol artefact. Placements are defined as poses where O touches the cylinder with both pads at q0. Every hand then closes to q0+5° under the same position command (K0 = 5 Nm/rad). A redesign whose faces sit where O's sat reproduces O's contact state almost by construction, and one whose faces are deeper over-squeezes. The paper itself shows this: U presses 3.3x harder, the skeleton-keeping fixed-axis family presses 2.2-4.2x harder, and that over-closure is what produces the abstract's "95-228%" figure. Under torque reuse the errors fall to 3-40%. With a contact-sensing or force-matched protocol, which is what a careful experimentalist would use, they fall to 20% and 3-7% (median), and the paper does not report whether Δ_contact still predicts the residual error there. The genuinely geometric part of the effect is R vs U at equal force: 5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%, three pairs of numbers. That is a real effect, but much smaller than the abstract implies. The abstract foregrounds the 95-228% figure and omits the force-matched result. On real servos, position-controlled stiffness is far higher than 5 Nm/rad, so the over-closure story would be even more protocol-dominated.

2. H1 compares against a weak baseline. The fair comparison isolating the crossed four-bar is CFL vs 2R-remote, both remotely actuated. There the result is mixed: 2R-remote leads at 90°, the two cross at 120°, and the CFL wins only at 150°. Most of C1's advantage over 2R-DD comes from moving the servo off the joint, not from the four-bar. The paper argues that 2R-remote "pays in Δ_contact", which ties C1 to C2. That is fine, but then the C1 claim as stated (vs 2R-DD) understates what is actually shown and overstates the role of the CFL. The H1 falsifier ("fails if 2R-DD matches it in any condition") is also easy to pass, given the space a servo body takes at the joint.

3. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, and that matters for a servo-driven hand. A crossed four-bar driven through its proximal pivot, with a pole pushed palmar under deep pads and a transmission angle allowed down to 30°, can have poor and strongly varying torque transmission near q_req. Coverage-optimal designs might therefore be unusable for power grasps. Study 3 hides this, because the command is reused in q-space through each hand's "transmission table". None of the following is reported for the selected CFL designs: fingertip force capacity, link loads, backlash, or sensitivity to link tolerances. This is the first thing I would check before building it.

4. Statistical power and predictor collinearity. Correlations are computed over 6-16 hands in most settings, and n = 6 for shape. Δ_contact and Δ_skel are coupled by Eq. (7), and the redesign families were built to separate them. Spearman over a designed family therefore partly reflects the designer's sampling. On the hand-independent grid, Δ_skel also has ρ = 0.46 (0.86 when placements are lost). The post-hoc subset gives ρ = 0.48 with CI [0.10, 0.78]. The evidence is consistent but moderate, and the H3 falsifier ("fails if another candidate predicts it in every setting") is asymmetric: it is easy for H3 to survive.

5. Scope: a planar finger with the MCP held, one object class (cylinder), q_req = 120° only in Study 3, pads sharing a single contact model, one flexion stiffness. The paper says the principle concerns multi-phalanx grasps, but that the MCP column is not kept is a significant gap for real power grasps. These limits are acceptable for Stage 1, but they bound the generality of C2.

6. Clarity. The writing is extremely compressed. Terms (S1, S2, x0, G, T_i, L_f(q) vs L_f, "start offset", "degenerate (0.21, 0)") arrive with little support, and several sentences need multiple readings. "Contact Jacobian" is used for the PIP column of J, but Δ_contact is a position-level distance, not a Jacobian distance. Keeping c(q) implies keeping J, but not the converse up to offsets, so the naming should be tightened. Study 3's grasp geometry is unclear: three fingers, a cylinder "resting on each hand's proximal pad", free in the sagittal plane — what opposes the load? The title is awkward.

7. Related work is appropriately narrow for the venue. Still, underactuated linkage-finger design (Birglen/Gosselin/Laliberté), which reasons explicitly about phalanx contact and linkage geometry for multi-phalanx grasps, is the most directly relevant missing line.

Desired outcome. The pole analysis and the explicit hypotheses meet the Stage-1 bar, and the Stage-2 plan is concrete. I lean towards accepting at Stage 1, conditional on reframing the C2 evidence around protocols that control force (reporting whether Δ_contact predicts the residual error under contact-sensing/force-matched closing), adding a CFL vs 2R-remote framing for C1, and adding at least a mechanical-advantage/force-capacity check on the chosen CFL designs. Overall 6, Accept, confidence 4.
