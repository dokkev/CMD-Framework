ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: mechanism kinematics. I checked the lemma, propositions, corollary and bounds by hand, and checked the design optimization for correctness and for whether the claims follow from it.

Central claims. (1) A remote-actuated finger with a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger at every pad thickness and flexion. (2) A redesign for a thicker pad should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), the "PIP column of the contact Jacobian", not the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot do this (closed-form bound, Eq. 9), and the CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound. (3) Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured pad effect follows Delta_contact (Spearman 0.80-0.86). This holds in two simulators and three tasks, and the error can reverse a conclusion.

Mathematics: what I verified.
- Lemma 1. Solving P = e^{iq}P + b gives P = b/(1-e^{iq}) = (b/2)(1 + i cot(q/2)). This is correct.
- Prop. 1. I placed the pole at the origin, the joint-side face corners at (-G/2, -(t-z)) and (G/2, -(t-z)), and the bisector normal at (cos q/2, -sin q/2). The corner stays on its side iff G >= 2(t-z)tan(q/2). The reflection argument is sound. When z > t, the mounting-side corner is never critical. Fixed axis: 2 tan 60 deg = 3.46 mm/mm, which is correct.
- Prop. 2. The phalanx ends are mirror images in the bisector, so their distance is 2|n.e1| = G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). This reproduces Eq. (4). The claim "lower the pole by delta and numerator and denominator grow by the same amount" also checks out at q_req: 2 delta[tan(1 - cos) + sin] = 2 delta tan. It also holds for every q <= q_req, because cos(q/2)tan(q_req/2) >= sin(q/2). The paper states the result only at q_req but could state it for every evaluated pose.
- Rolling joint. For two equal rollers I get the finite pole -i r tan(q/4), which matches the text. The paper is careful to keep the finite-displacement pole p(q) (Props. 1-3) apart from the instantaneous centre p-hat (Eq. 5). I appreciated that.
- Prop. 3 and Eq. (8). Delta c = (I - R_q)Delta p gives 2 sin(q/2)|Delta p|. The two pole coordinates differ by the face-depth offset, so the triangle inequality gives Eq. (8). This is correct, given that both start points are kept.
- Numbers. The bound values match Fig. 3: 8.49 mm at 90 deg and 10.39 mm at 120 deg for 3 to 9 mm. 0.35/3.46 = 10% and 4.31/8.49 = 51%.

Where the claims go beyond the derivations.
(a) The fixed-axis bound is conditional, but the abstract and introduction state it as unconditional ("wherever a fixed axis goes"; "only a joint whose pole moves can"). The proof assumes the axis keeps its depth while the face moves down by Delta_t. Kinematically, a pin lowered by Delta_t together with the face keeps c(q) exactly. What forbids that is a packaging constraint: the pin must sit above the pad's mounting surface. That constraint is mentioned only in III-B3 and is not part of the corollary's hypotheses. The paper's own data show the bound's narrow scope. Of the three non-CFL joints, 2R-DD sits below it (its face is already held deep) and RCJ sits below it (its roller grows). Only 2R-remote meets it. So "10-51% of the bound" normalises against a reference that two of the three baselines beat. The fairer statement is the direct comparison: CFL is least in every cell, and least in 7 of 9 once the start offset is removed.
(b) Delta_contact versus the contact Jacobian. The column [c'; 1] depends only on p-hat(q) in F_p. Delta_contact is the maximum position difference of a chosen material point. It therefore includes a constant start offset at q = 0, which leaves c' unchanged. The paper acknowledges such offsets for 2R-DD and RCJ: 9.3 mm for RCJ in Fig. 3g. The mechanism given in IV-B1 is also a zeroth-order one: where the face sits at the setpoint sets the preload. So the evidence supports "keep the face position along the flexion path", which implies but is not the same as "keep the Jacobian column". Having "Contact Jacobian" in the title and abstract over-reads the result. Eq. (11) also does not constrain c0, while Prop. 3 and Corollary 1 assume c0 is kept.
(c) The theory predicts the coverage optimum turns about a point on the face. The CFL optima keep the pole 3.6-4.6 mm above the face at 120 deg. Which constraint (transmission angle, link interference, servo body) stops the pole is never said. Without that, the reader cannot tell whether Prop. 2's design rule or the optimizer is the limit.
(d) "Lets the pad be studied on its own." R's errors are 3, 21 and 41% at 5, 7 and 9 mm, and at 9 mm no CFL redesign gets below about 2.3 mm. A 20-40% bias in a measured friction or stiffness effect is a reduction, not isolation. The conclusion should be scaled to that.
(e) Smaller points. The "first-order" preload prediction gives 230% against 124% measured for U, which is not first order. The ground truth for the reversed conclusion appears as both -37% and "41% less". The Spearman sets are hands chosen by the authors to decorrelate Delta_contact from Delta_skel, and n is 14-16 per setting. The 45-hand grid is the only test with power against Delta_skel. Prop. 1 states only S1-S2, but the mirror argument also needs pads of equal shape, which Prop. 2 adds as "equal pads".

Optimization. The GA and NSGA-II runs use three seeds, with no population size, generation count, design-space bounds or convergence evidence. The idealized upper bounds help: "CFL ideal" sits close to the CFL, which suggests the search is not far off. The room claim should be read against 2R-DD. 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90 deg and at 120 deg for thin pads (Fig. 3a-b). The CFL's coverage advantage over remote actuation alone appears only at high flexion with thick pads.

Overall. The kinematic core is correct and unusually clean for a hand-design paper. The finite-pole formulation and Eqs. (3), (4), (7) and (8) are nice, reusable tools. The simulation evidence is broad and pre-registered. My main concerns are about scope rather than errors: an unconditional framing of a conditional bound, a position-level effect labelled as a Jacobian invariant, and an isolation claim stronger than the 20-40% residual errors allow. Presentation is compact and mostly precise, but some sentences are too compressed to parse (for example "a fixed axis whose contact point may slide stays above 0.59-0.80 of the bound").
