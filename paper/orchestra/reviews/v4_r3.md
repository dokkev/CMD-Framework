THOUGHT:

Reviewer 3 (mechanism design and kinematics). I re-derived every formal statement in Sec. III before forming an opinion, because the paper's argument rests on them.

**Lemma 1 (finite pole).** Correct. For a planar rotation R_q, (I − R_q)^{-1} = ½[I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}]. The determinant is 2(1 − cos q) and sin q/(1 − cos q) = cot(q/2). So P = ½(b + cot(q/2) R_{π/2} b) with b = r_C(q) − R_q r_C(0). Two remarks:
- This is the classical Burmester/Bottema–Roth finite pole. Calling it a lemma is fine. But the abstract's "we prove" should not suggest that this is new.
- The sentence "The finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is true only up to isolated poses. The accurate statement is that the two coincide for all q iff the pole is stationary. There is also a typo: "where and b".

**Proposition 1 (clearance).** Correct under its hypotheses.
- S1 and S2 make the distal pad at q = 0 the mirror image of the proximal pad across the vertical line through the pole. Rotation by q about the pole composed with that reflection is a reflection across a line through the pole, tilted q/2 from vertical.
- A convex set and its mirror image intersect iff the set meets the line, since points on the line are fixed. The "iff" therefore holds, up to the closed/open boundary case.
- The binding corner is the face-side, joint-side corner. Corners dorsal of the pole are on the safe side. This gives G ≥ 2[t − z]_+ tan(q/2). I assume the bracket in Eq. (4) is the positive part; the extraction is ambiguous.
- Fixed axis: 2 tan 60° = 3.46 mm per mm of thickness is correct. Fig. 2 shows the half-gap t·tan(q/2): 8.66, 15.6 and 4·tan 60° = 6.93. All consistent.

Concern with S2: for a general (non-symmetric) crossed four-bar, the finite pole also moves along x as q changes. S2 requires both joint-side corners to stay equidistant in x from P(q) for every q, which only symmetric mechanisms satisfy: an anti-parallelogram, or equal rollers. The paper reports agreement "within 0.05 mm (median)" on CFL designs. The maximum deviation, and how often S2 actually holds, should be given. Otherwise Eq. (4) is an approximation for the CFL, not a law.

**Proposition 2 (coverage).** Correct.
- I re-derived the distance between the phalanx ends. It equals twice the distance from an end to the mirror line: G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). Hence L_f(q) = L_f − G(1 − cos(q/2)) + 2z sin(q/2).
- The qualitative conclusion holds once Prop. 1 ties G to z: lowering z shrinks G until z reaches t, after which it only lengthens the denominator. So the best pole lies on the face. This is a nice, clean insight.
- It also shows that the C_pad objective rewards a contour that shortens when flexed. That is exactly why 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90° on the paper's own objective, and why the ranking flips under (ℓ_p + ℓ_d)/L_f. The objective's normalization is therefore not neutral between architectures, and the paper should say so more plainly.

**Proposition 3 and Eq. (7).** Correct. c = (I − R)p + R c0, so Δc = (I − R)Δp and ‖(I − R)v‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖v‖. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality: ‖Δc‖ + ‖Δr‖ ≥ ‖(I − R)(Δp_c − Δp_s)‖ = 2 sin(q/2)|Δt|.

But the frame for the skeleton motion is ambiguous. Sec. III-B says r_C is written "in the same frame" F_p, whose origin is on the proximal contact face. Prop. 3 says the pole is "measured from the link line instead".
- If r_C is expressed in F_p, then a thicker pad shifts the fingertip's start point by Δt. "Both start points kept" then cannot hold, and Δ_skel ≥ Δt trivially.
- If r_C is expressed in a link-line frame, the two Δ's are measured in different frames.

This matters for C4, where Δ_skel is the competing predictor. It must be defined unambiguously.

Eq. (7) also has a consequence for the experiment. Among near-efficient redesigns of one thickness, Δ_contact and Δ_skel are anti-correlated by construction. The ρ = −0.40 between error and Δ_skel in Fig. 1d is therefore partly structural, not evidence that the skeleton is irrelevant. The partial correlation (0.88) is the right statistic, but with n = 15 its interval should be reported.

**Corollary 1 (fixed-axis bound).** Correct, but nearly definitional.
- With a fixed axis on the link line and face depth t = d + h, the pole sits t above the face in F_p. So |Δp| ≥ |Δh − Δd|, and the maximum over q ≤ q_req ≤ π of 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| is reached at q_req.
- Rollers: I checked that the finite pole of two equal rollers is at depth r tan(q/4) on the bisector. With complex numbers, P = −r(1 − e^{iq/2})/(1 + e^{iq/2}) = i r tan(q/4). Correct.
- The whole content of the bound, however, is in the mount rule (Δd = 0). Once pads must sit at the hardware-limited depth, "the face moves Δh away from the axis" is an assumption, and the bound is a one-line consequence of Eq. (6).
- The paper's own Fig. 4 shows this. 2R-DD, also a fixed axis, falls below the "fixed-axis bound" in every cell (e.g., 5.2 mm vs 8.5 mm at 90°/9 mm), because it remounts its pads. RCJ does too.
- The abstract and C1 ("a fixed axis cannot keep its contact-surface motion when the pads thicken") therefore overstate. The bound is about hardware-limited mount depth, not about fixed axes.
- The rolling-joint clause ("fixed roller radius") hides the same freedom: changing r moves the pole curve.

Further, Fig. 4d says 2R-DD and RCJ "start apart" (Δc(0) ≠ 0). The c0-kept hypothesis of Prop. 3 and Cor. 1 then fails for them, and Δ_contact mixes a start offset with a pole shift. Was c0 constrained in Study 2? If not, the comparison across architectures is not like-for-like.

**The real mechanism insight.** The insight seems to be that a crossed four-bar's pole is virtual, so it can be placed near or below the face where no physical bearing could sit. The paper never says this explicitly.
- How are the CFL links packaged relative to the pad volume? In Fig. 1 the thin links cross through the pad region. Are the links lateral to the pads, and is the collision check 3-D?
- If lateral placement is allowed for links, why is a lateral journal bearing below the face disallowed for the fixed axis?
- This packaging asymmetry decides C1–C3 and needs to be stated.

**Contact-Jacobian framing.** c(q) is a fixed material point on the distal face, not the moving contact point, and the kinematics are relative (distal face vs proximal face). What is kept is the relative twist, equivalently the fixed and moving centrodes relative to the faces: c′ = R_{π/2}(c − IC(q)). "PIP column of the contact Jacobian" is a stretch of the usual term. The centrode/pole language is more precise and connects to a large classical literature (Burmester theory, centrode-based joint synthesis, polycentric prosthetic knees and orthoses) that is barely cited. The J ↔ c equivalence in Sec. III-B is trivial and could be one sentence.

**Studies 1 and 2.**
- Generally convincing as design-space studies, with good checks: 1e-13 agreement, re-verification at 0.05°.
- C2 is phrased against 2R-DD, but the fairer joint-only comparison is 2R-remote. 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90° on the stated objective, and RCJ ideal matches the CFL. So the coverage advantage is largely actuator packaging, not the joint type. The text partly says this; the claim should be phrased accordingly.
- Study 2: "10–51% of the bound" (0.35 to 4.31 mm). At 90°/9 mm the CFL still changes Δ_contact by 4.3 mm. In Study 3 that magnitude gives errors around 100%.

**Study 3.** Interesting and carefully gated (pre-fixed gates, robustness checks, three interface changes), but it has three problems.

(i) The causal chain is close to tautological. Under a reused stiff position command, grasp force is set by interference past first contact, and the face position at the commanded angle is precisely what Δ_contact measures. The finding is expected; the contribution is quantifying it.

(ii) The paper's own protocol comparisons narrow the significance:
- Matching the contact force leaves 3–7% error.
- Closing from touch leaves a median 20% error, which then follows Δ_skel (ρ = 0.88), not Δ_contact.
- So under protocols a careful experimenter would use, the skeleton motion is the predictor, and the "keep contact, not skeleton" message holds only for reused open-loop joint commands.
- The paper states this ("depends on what it reuses"), but the title, abstract and C4 do not.

(iii) The hand R that keeps the contact-surface motion still errs 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. "Measures like the original" is too strong.

Simulation fidelity issues:
- One joint-level stiffness K0 is used for all hands. A real servo through a transmission whose MA_wc varies −7% to +16% gives joint stiffness varying roughly with MA², about −14% to +35%. The simulation idealizes away exactly the transmission change that the redesign introduces.
- Pads are a contact model whose stiffness does not depend on thickness, so thickness enters only through geometry.
- U is "redesigned for coverage alone". The natural alternative a designer would choose, a redesign that minimizes Δ_skel, should be a named hand with its error reported.
- Inconsistency: Fig. 1 caption says U presses 3.9× harder (17.4/4.5), Sec. V says 3.3×.
- Shape: n = 6.

**Stage-2 plan.** Appropriate in type: markers for measured Δ_contact, grasp-and-pull with a force gauge. But O, R and U alone (n = 3) can test R < U, not a correlation. Adding at least one or two more redesigns would make C4 testable on hardware.

**Overall.** A coherent, original framing with correct (if classical) kinematics, honest reporting, and a reasonable Stage-2 plan. The headline claims overstate what the bound and the simulation show. I lean towards acceptance as a Stage-1 extended abstract, conditional on narrowing the claims and fixing the frame and S2 issues.
