ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and how well each is supported:

1. **Analytic claim (Sec. III-A).** When an interface changes, a finger should keep the motion of its contact surfaces (the contact Jacobian seen from the proximal phalanx), not its skeleton.
   - Eq. (4) says no redesign whose contact surfaces deepen can keep both motions.
   - Eq. (5) says a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion from the same initial contact point.
   - Using the finite-displacement pole for this is tidy, and it is the most original part of the paper. Eq. (3) (one point's deviation equals 2 sin(q/2) times the pole's shift) is a neat reduction.
   - The bounds rest on assumptions the text states only briefly. Eq. (3) needs the same initial contact point c0. Yet Δ_d^p is defined to "include q = 0, so a moved initial contact point counts", which falls outside the assumption Eq. (3) uses. Eq. (4) needs the frames F_p and F_1 to differ by a pure translation Δt. Eq. (5) needs a fixed axis that cannot sit below the pad mount, which is a packaging argument, not a kinematic one.
   - Fig. 3(c) itself shows at-joint designs above the "fixed axis" line, explained as starting from a different contact point. So the bound is narrower than the abstract's phrasing ("a fixed axis ... cannot keep that motion").
   - Support: sound but narrow.

2. **Design-study claim (Sec. III-D).** The crossed four-bar covers more than an at-joint finger in all 10 jointly feasible conditions (by 2.4–14.6 pp), and preserves contact motion best in all nine conditions.
   - Reasonably supported by Fig. 3.
   - Coverage is a bespoke metric: an inscribed cylinder scored at three flexion angles. The comparison depends on the authors' own objective and constraint choices.
   - The selection rule for "CoDe" on the Eq. (9) Pareto front ("keeps both best at near-best coverage") is informal.
   - The "remote" baseline nearly matches CoDe at 90° and at ≤5 mm, 120°. So the room benefit comes largely from moving the servo off the joint, and the four-bar matters mainly at large flexion and thick pads.

3. **Empirical claim (Sec. IV).** When O's impedance setpoint is reused, the error in the measured pad effect tracks Δ_d^p, not Δ_skel.
   - Strengths:
     - permutation tests with Holm correction;
     - partial ρ that controls for Δ_skel (0.86–0.91);
     - two simulators and two contact models;
     - a force-controlled check (partial ρ = 0.80);
     - a Skeleton control that rules out the circular reading "of course the hand designed to match O matches O";
     - a stated pre-registration.
   - The sign reversal in Fig. 1(d) is a compelling headline.
   - Weaknesses:
     - (a) No pad deforms. A "thicker, compliant pad" is a softer contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer. The paper is about contact interfaces, yet interface mechanics (conformation, contact-patch growth, contact-point migration) are absent. The authors admit this in the Limitations, but it bears directly on the main claim.
     - (b) The construct "the pad's true effect = its effect on O" is a definitional choice. A thicker pad on a real hand necessarily changes geometry, so it is debatable whether CoDe "measures the pad's effect correctly" or measures a counterfactual. The framing needs a clearer argument for why O-relative effect is the quantity practitioners want.
     - (c) Re-referencing the setpoint already cuts the error from 42% to 14%. Much of the problem is command calibration. The answer "a learned policy has no setpoint" is fair, but fine-tuning or adaptation is not considered.
     - (d) The correlation sets (27, 18, 14–45 redesigns) are built partly from redesigns "held to a prescribed Δ_d^p". So the spread in Δ_d^p is by construction, and their exact composition is unclear.
     - (e) At 9 mm, CoDe's E is 38%. The method degrades where thick interfaces matter most.

4. **Policy-transfer claim.** Supported qualitatively, with notable caveats:
   - only two seeds;
   - the cube task's seed-2 bars differ markedly from seed 1;
   - pad walking does not transfer to Drake even on O.
   - The finding that which kinematic property matters depends on where the task touches (pads vs fingertip end vs palm) is honest and useful, but it weakens the universality of the headline thesis.

5. **Hardware.** The CoDe Hand exists only as CAD. Contribution 2 (a modular finger for diverse pads) has no physical validation: no build, no pad swaps, no tactile skin integration. Every result is simulation.

Score basis: high originality in the framing and the pole-based analysis, careful statistical design, and a clear message. Against that: entirely simulated evidence without pad deformation, for a paper whose subject is physical contact interfaces; an unbuilt hardware contribution; and a debatable reference construct. For RSS this sits at the borderline, leaning reject. A hardware pad-swap experiment showing even one reversed effect would change my assessment substantially.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The paper is very dense in notation (Δ_d^p, Δ_d^1, Δ_skel, Δt, F_p, F_1, C̄_pad, C̄*_pad, F′, Q_eval, D, E). Δ_d^1 is used in Sec. III-A ("Δ_d^1, Eq. (7)") before Sec. III-C defines it. A short symbol table or an earlier definition would help.
2. The Introduction says a redesign "that keeps the skeleton exactly" misreports the effect, but Skeleton has Δ_skel = 0.3 mm, not 0. Soften this to "nearly exactly" or similar.
3. "E" is introduced as "the median |error|" and is elsewhere called the "typical error". Use one name and give E a symbol definition in an equation or in the Table I caption.
4. Table I has blank-looking gaps between columns (e.g., after "stiffness" in the Coverage row). The columns "convex" and "compl. pad" are not explained. Nor does the table say which simulator the "Other commands" block (torque, touch, force, setpoint) uses.
5. The abstract and Fig. 1(d) report medians of loads (−41%, −39%, +48%), while Table I reports medians of per-placement errors (CoDe 0%, Coverage +125%). The text explains this in a parenthetical, but readers will still stumble. Consider reporting both in one place.
6. The sample sizes behind each correlation vary (27, 18, 14–45, 14) and are hard to track. List in one sentence or table which redesign set each statistic uses.
7. Fig. 3(c)'s "contact motion kept (%)" = 100(1 − Δ_d^p/b) allows large negative values, and its caption needs a long parenthetical to explain the at-joint points above the bound. Consider plotting Δ_d^p/b directly, or explain the metric in the main text.
8. Fig. 4's annotation text (E point / E hydro, Δ values, ρ) is very small and hard to read in print. The "three moments" renders are also too small to show anything.
9. Fig. 2(a)'s shaded regions and lines (CoDe region, revolute line, rolling-contact wedge, contact-face band) lack a legend. The meaning of the light-blue CoDe region's upper boundary is unclear.
10. The criterion that selects CoDe from the Eq. (9) Pareto front should be stated precisely (thresholds or a scalarization) rather than as "keeps both best at near-best coverage".
11. Sec. III-D2 refers to "the CoDe hand" defined via Eq. (9), but Eq. (9) appears in Sec. III-C, and "Sec. III-D2" is cited in Sec. IV-A as where CoDe is defined. Make one place the definition.
12. "Relay dial" (Fig. 5) and "a fingertip end turning a dial" (text) name the same task differently. "Pad-turned dial" (Fig. 4) is a different task, which invites confusion. Use consistent names.
13. "Pre-registered" should say where or how (e.g., an anonymized timestamped document), even if only as a footnote.
14. The labels "Coverage" for a hand, "coverage" for a metric, and "Coverage" in Fig. 3(a) for a study panel collide. Consider renaming the hand (e.g., "Coverage-only").
15. Fig. 1(c) plots "change from O (mm)" without stating which quantities are plotted (Δ_d^p-type path deviation vs fingertip deviation over q). Tie the panel to the Eq. (7) symbols.
16. The definition of the fingertip C, and why the "distal contact point below the fingertip" is the chosen representative point, should appear in the main text, not only in the Fig. 2 caption.
17. Related Work is very short. It should engage more with tactile-skin integration on servo hands and with policy-transfer and cross-embodiment literature beyond [12], since policy reuse is a stated contribution.
