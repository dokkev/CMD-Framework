THOUGHT:

The paper's problem is specific and real. Comparing two contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, camera-based tactile modules) usually means redesigning the finger, which confounds the comparison. The proposed fix is to redesign so that the relative motion of the two contact faces is preserved: the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame, or equivalently the finite pole measured from the face. Keeping the skeleton is not the goal. The argument has three layers:
(i) elementary planar finite-pole kinematics: mirror-line clearance (Prop. 1), coverage (Prop. 2), Δc = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| (Prop. 3), and a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1, Eq. 8);
(ii) design-optimization studies showing that a crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more coverage than a servo-at-joint 2R (Study 1) and stays at 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound at near-best coverage (Study 2);
(iii) a simulation study showing that, when commands or torques are reused, the error in a measured interface effect tracks Δ_contact rather than skeleton change, coverage, thickness or transmission (Study 3).

For a Stage-1 RSS extended abstract this is unusually complete. The hypotheses come with failure conditions, the theory is checked against the evaluator (1e-13, 0.05 mm), and Study 3 already includes a hand-independent grid, a torque protocol and robustness checks. I checked the core math. Prop. 3 follows directly from c = p + R(c0 − p): Δc = (I − R_q)Δp, and ‖I − R_q‖ = 2 sin(q/2). The Fig. 2 numbers match Eq. (4) (5 tan 60° = 8.7, 4 tan 60° = 6.9, and a gap growth of 2 tan 60° = 3.46 mm per mm). The Lemma 1 pole formula is the standard (I − R)^{-1} = ½(I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}). The theory is correct but classical. The novelty is in the framing (which invariant to keep and why) and in the observation that a virtual pole is needed when the pins must stay above the pad's mounting surface. The mathematics itself is not new.

My main concerns:

1. Circularity and construction of the Study 3 evidence. Δ_contact is the deviation of the redesign's face-to-face geometry from O's. In the main protocol the cylinder is placed where O touches it with both pads. A hand that reproduces O's face geometry therefore reproduces O's quasi-static grasp almost by definition, so a positive correlation with Δ_contact is close to expected. The non-trivial claim is that the alternatives do not predict the error. But the design set was built along the Δ_contact axis: R minimises it, U ignores it, and the rest are "redesigns at set Δ_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". Eq. (7) also forces Δ_contact and Δ_skel to trade off at a fixed thickness. The negative ρ for Δ_skel (−0.40, −0.51) is thus partly produced by the design set rather than being an independent finding. The hand-independent grid is the right control, but the signal weakens there: ρ = 0.60 overall, and ρ = 0.48 [0.10, 0.78] over the 26 redesigns that keep 80% of placements. Skeleton change also matters on the grid through placement loss (ρ = 0.86). How the 27 extra redesigns were chosen is not stated.

2. Keeping Δ_contact is not yet enough to make interface studies comparable. Under the reused command, R still errs 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm on the friction effect. Under the torque protocol it errs 12–17%. If the paper's goal is comparable interface studies, these errors are large. The paper gives no threshold on Δ_contact, or on the resulting error, at which a comparison becomes valid. Most of the command-protocol error is over-closure (U presses 3.3–3.9× harder). This suggests the reused position command is itself a fragile protocol. The paper's argument that closing on touch or force is disallowed because sensing changes with the interface is reasonable but debatable: an external joint-torque or motor-current threshold does not depend on the interface.

3. The interface physics is not modelled. Pads are a contact model with set friction and contact stiffness. The "thick compliant pad" that motivates the whole paper is only a geometric offset. Whether the effect of a friction change on a 9 mm compliant pad should equal its effect on a 3 mm pad is itself unclear. The error metric E assumes the 3 mm hand's effect is the ground truth for every thickness, and this needs to be argued. Study 3 also uses only CFL redesigns, at q_req = 120° and one stiffness K0. C4 is therefore tested within one architecture, and no fixed-axis hand gives a contrast at large Δ_contact.

4. The coverage objective has an artifact. C_pad divides by the contour length L_f(q), which shrinks when a large fixed-axis gap closes in flexion (Prop. 2). The paper itself notes that 2R-remote leads on C̄_pad at 90° but the CFL leads on pad length over the fixed L_f. This makes the Study 1 comparison metric-dependent. Moreover, C̄_pad is the objective in every search, including the "coverage end" of the Study 2 fronts. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, and the CFL "absorbs the thicker pad by changing ... its transmission". Force capability may therefore degrade unseen.

5. Smaller technical points.
- Δ_contact uses a max that includes Δc(0). This penalises 2R-DD and RCJ for a contact point that moved along the face (Fig. 4d), but Cor. 1 assumes c0 is kept.
- Prop. 1 and Prop. 2 need S1 (equal face depths) and S2 (symmetry). 2R-DD violates S1 by the paper's own account.
- RCJ "ideal omits the joint actuator's body", so the RCJ is not remotely actuated. Its large deficit is therefore an actuator-placement result, not a joint-type result.
- The statistics use small n (14–16 hands, six for shape) across five candidates and five settings. The selection rule ("the only candidate whose interval excludes zero in every setting") is not corrected for multiple comparisons.
- The shape result (ρ = 0.94, six hands) appears in the abstract range without its n.

Writing: the terse, one-message-per-sentence style is mostly effective, and the C1–C4 hypotheses with failure conditions suit Stage 1 well. Several problems remain.
- The title is long and slogan-like.
- The abstract packs too many numbers, and "changes it least ... by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound" is ambiguous.
- Many symbols are used before they are defined or are under-defined (x0, L_vp, r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, the [·]^+ in Eq. 4, z sign convention).
- C1 is a theorem, not a falsifiable hypothesis. C4's failure condition ("another quantity predicts the error in every setting") is one-sided: it cannot fail if Δ_contact itself is weak.
- Units mix: fractions (0.085–0.096, "(0.21, 0)") and percent points.
- Table I's column headers are hard to parse: which columns are the O's-contacts friction, stiffness, grid and torque settings?
- The title promises a "CoDe Hand", but the paper analyses one planar finger with the MCP fixed.
- Related work omits the most directly related lines: polycentric prosthetic knees and four-bar virtual instantaneous centres, and self-aligning exoskeleton joints. Both address keeping relative segment motion with a virtual pole.

Overall: an original and carefully executed framing with correct, if elementary, theory and a well-planned Stage 2. But the central empirical claim (C4) rests on a design set and placement protocol that favour Δ_contact. The best redesign still leaves 12–41% error. The interface mechanics the paper is about are not simulated. I lean toward rejection at the borderline and could be persuaded by co-reviewers given the Stage-1 standard.
