ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and how well each is supported:

1. Keep the contact-surface motion, not the skeleton (Sec. III-A, Eqs. 3–5). The pole argument is neat and mostly right. Eq. (3) follows directly from the planar finite-displacement pole. Eq. (4) is a triangle-inequality bound, and Eq. (5) is the fixed-axis special case. I see three problems:
   - Eq. (3) needs the same initial contact point c0. Δ_d^p and Δ_skel in Eq. (7) explicitly count a moved initial point, yet Eq. (4) is derived by applying Eq. (3) in both frames. A redesign that shifts c0 or the fingertip at q=0 is not obviously covered by Eq. (4). The paper never says whether the bound still holds then, or whether it is only an assumption.
   - Eq. (4) is evaluated only at q = q_req, while Δ is a maximum over q. That direction of the inequality is fine, but the paper should say so.
   - The bound is tight only when the two pole shifts are collinear. In practice it is a fairly weak statement, about 1.7|Δt| at 120°. "No redesign keeps both" is therefore true but quantitatively mild. At 7 mm (Δt ≈ 4 mm, less if the pivots rise) the bound is about 7 mm in total, which matches Skeleton/CoDe's 6.6 + 0.3 and 1.1 + 4.8. So the trade-off is real and modest in size.
   This analysis is the paper's most original element.

2. A crossed four-bar gives more room than a servo-at-joint finger (Sec. III-D, Fig. 3). Supported in optimization: 10/10 feasible conditions, +2.4 to +14.6 pp. But Fig. 3(a) shows that remote actuation of a plain revolute joint is about as good in many conditions. Most of the "room" benefit therefore comes from moving the servo, not from the articulation. The four-bar's distinct advantage shows up mainly at 150° and in preservation (Fig. 3(c)). The hand itself appears only as CAD. Nothing is built or tested physically, although the title says "Design of a ... Robot Hand".

3. Measurement error follows Δ_d^p, not Δ_skel (Sec. IV-B). This is the main empirical claim, and it is reasonably well supported within the paper's framing:
   - multiple tests, permutation tests with Holm correction;
   - partial correlations controlling for Δ_skel and for contact-force deviation;
   - two simulators and two contact models, a Skeleton control, and a pre-registered ρ = 0.94.
   My concerns:
   - (a) Ground truth is counterfactual. The "true" pad effect is defined as the effect on O fitted with the new pad's contact stiffness but O's geometry. The paper's own premise is that O has no room for the thick pad. The reference therefore exists only in simulation, and it assumes the pad's effect is independent of hand geometry. A reviewer could argue that a different finger legitimately has a different pad effect, so "error" is partly a definition. The claim is really about transfer of a fixed impedance setpoint (command/policy reuse). The paper should frame it that way consistently rather than as "misreporting the pad's effect".
   - (b) Some circularity. CoDe is optimized to match O's contact-point path in F_p, and the tasks are scored by how pad contact responds to a reused joint setpoint. The correlation with Δ_d^p is close to expected by construction. The Skeleton control and the correlation sets (prescribed Δ_d^p, varying Δ_skel) help, but the sets are designer-chosen, and their sampling determines ρ.
   - (c) No pad deforms. A "thicker compliant pad" is modelled as softer point contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer. Deformation moves the contact point, which is exactly the quantity under study, so the core mechanism is untested in its most important regime.
   - (d) Re-referencing the setpoint cuts error from 42% to 14%. This shows that simple command retargeting removes much of the problem for setpoint commands. It weakens the motivation for mechanical preservation, and is only partly answered by "a policy has no setpoint" (actions could be retargeted).
   - (e) The error is 38% at 9 mm, so the method's range is limited.

4. Policies transfer to the redesign that keeps the motion their task uses (Fig. 5). Weak:
   - only two seeds, with large seed variance (cube: Coverage about 70% vs about 20%);
   - finger-walk does not transfer to Drake even on O;
   - the cube result rests on a different quantity (palm shift), and the relay dial favours Skeleton.
   The honest conclusion, "it depends on where the task touches the finger", is interesting but it is not evidence for the CoDe principle as such.

My scores rest on these points. There is an original, clearly argued kinematic insight with a careful simulated validation. Against that: no hardware or real pad, a counterfactual ground truth, partly circular evaluation, a policy study that is thin, and very dense presentation. At a systems/design venue like RSS, the lack of a physical prototype for a paper titled as a hand design is a serious gap. Borderline, leaning reject.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The abstract and introduction are very compressed. Phrases such as "keeps that motion from the same initial contact point" and "a redesign for coverage alone reports..." are hard to parse before the reader knows what O, CoDe, Coverage and Skeleton are. Define the three redesigns and O earlier, ideally in the abstract or the first paragraph of Sec. I.
2. The definitions of O, CoDe, Skeleton and Coverage at the end of Sec. I use Δ_d^p and Δ_skel before they are defined (Sec. III-A/C). Add a one-line plain-language gloss, or move the definition.
3. Notation is heavy. Δ_d^p, Δ_d^1, Δ_skel, Δp, Δp_1, Δc, F_p, F_1 and Eq. (7) are all introduced in about half a column, and the bound in Sec. III-A references Eq. (7) before it appears. A small notation table, or a figure showing F_p, F_1, c, r_C and the pole, would help a lot.
4. Eq. (9) uses F′ (pivots freed) but F′ is introduced only after the equation, in prose. Define it before Eq. (9).
5. The phrase "CoDe is the design on the Pareto front of Eq. (9) that keeps both best at near-best coverage" is not an operational selection rule. State the actual criterion (thresholds or a scalarization).
6. Sec. III-D says the crossed four-bar is "labelled CoDe in the figures", while CoDe also names a specific redesign and the hand. Using one label for an architecture, a design and a platform is confusing. Use "Four-bar" for the architecture in Fig. 3.
7. In Fig. 3(c), the y-axis "contact motion kept (%)", defined as 100(1 − Δ_d^p/b) with b the fixed-axis bound, is non-obvious, and values below −100 appear. Explain in the main text why this normalization is used, and why at-joint designs can lie above 0 (they start from a different contact point).
8. Fig. 4: the per-task E/Δ/ρ text blocks are tiny and hard to read in print. The caption's "18 correlated redesigns (both contact models, ranks averaged)" needs a pointer to how the 18 relate to the 27 grid redesigns and the 14–45 per Isaac Lab test.
9. "E" (median |error|) and the signed median error are both used. Table I gives signed values, while the text discusses E. Clarify which statistic each figure or table shows, and give E in Table I as well.
10. Table I has blank cells (e.g., Drake point compliant pad, MuJoCo convex for Coverage?), and bold is used without explanation. Explain the missing entries and the meaning of bold, and add the number of placements per cell.
11. The pull-out load numbers in Fig. 1(d) and Table I use different aggregations. Note this in the Fig. 1 caption (currently only parenthetical in Sec. IV-B).
12. The "Measure" paragraph packs the reference definition, the non-circularity argument, the error formula, the noise floor ("errs at most 6%") and pre-registration into one paragraph. Split it, and give the error definition as a numbered equation.
13. The "Tasks" paragraph mixes the Isaac Lab, Drake and PPO setups. A small table (task, simulator, contact model, # placements, metric) would be clearer.
14. Fig. 5: "share of O's performance kept" needs the underlying metric for each task (e.g., distance walked, dial angle, cube rotation rate), and the bottom row mixes Δ_d^p, Δ_skel and palm shift without units in the caption header.
15. Fig. 2(a): the caption mentions that the rolling joint's pole lies at most r·tan(q/4) below the contact point, but the main text claims "rollers about as long as a phalanx" without a number. Give the required r for 7 mm pads.
16. The derivation of the 3.46 mm/mm figure (2·tan 60°) should be stated, since it is a key quantitative claim.
17. Related Work (one paragraph) is too short to position the paper. Add cross-embodiment and retargeting work, and tactile/pad benchmarking practice.
18. Grammar and style: several sentences rely on colons and semicolons to carry the argument, e.g., "It sounds simple, and its natural answer, keep the skeleton (...), is wrong". Shorter, declarative sentences would improve readability.
19. References: [12] is listed as ICLR 2026, so check that it is citable. [5] and [8] lack venue formatting consistent with the others ("arXiv preprint" vs. plain arXiv).
20. The Conclusion's "holding the typical error near 10% up to 7 mm" should say which simulator or setting this refers to, since Table I and Fig. 4 show values up to 18% for CoDe.
