THOUGHT:

My first reaction: this is an unusual and clever framing. The paper treats "comparable interface studies" as a confounding problem in hand design and argues that the quantity to hold fixed under a redesign is the relative motion of the two contact faces, not the skeleton. The kinematics is classical (the finite-displacement pole, the chord length 2 sin(q/2)·r), but using it to *bound* what a fixed-axis joint can preserve under a pad-thickness change is a tidy observation. The main empirical result (Spearman 0.83 between Δ_contact and the error in a friction effect over 15 simulated redesigns, with Δ_skel not predictive) is clearly stated and the authors report exclusions, partial correlations and protocol variations openly. Limitations are stated more honestly than usual.

My concerns fall into four groups.

(1) The theory is correct but thin, and its "cannot" depends on an assumption. I checked Prop. 3: c = (I−R_q)p + R_q c0, so Δc = (I−R_q)Δp and |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. That is right. Because both designs rotate by the same q, Δc does not depend on which distal-face point is tracked, which makes Δ_contact better defined than it first looks (worth saying in the paper). Fig. 2's numbers agree with Eq. (4): 5·tan60° = 8.7, 9·tan60° = 15.6, 4·tan60° = 6.9. But Corollary 1 is a one-line consequence of Prop. 3, and the claim that "a fixed-axis joint cannot keep it" rests completely on |Δh − Δd| > 0, that is, on pads being mounted "at their own depth". If the designer can recess the face (Δd = Δh), the bound is zero. The paper rules out spacers (thin pad raised to the deep pad's depth) but not the dual: recessing the thick pad into the phalanx. Sec. IV-C even says 2R-DD and RCJ beat the bound by changing face depth or remounting. So the impossibility is really "under a fixed mount-depth rule". This should be stated as such in the abstract and C1. Also, "finite-pole laws for … any planar finger joint" (C1) overclaims: Props. 1–2 assume S1/S2, which "hold only approximately", so Eqs. (4)–(5) are called estimates.

(2) Study 1 does not cleanly support C2. CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions, but 2R-DD differs from CFL in *both* joint type and actuator placement. The comparison that isolates the joint is 2R-remote, and 2R-remote has *higher* coverage at 90° (by 1.9–4.1 points) and at 120° for 3–5 mm. The CFL also got an extra neighbourhood search that "favours CFL by up to 2.1 points", and its seed spread (1.3–3.4 points) is as large as many of the reported gaps. A fair story: CFL wins at deep flexion and thick pads; at moderate flexion, remote actuation and not the four-bar is what helps. The text partly says this, but C2 as written does not.

(3) Study 3 risks being circular, and it leaves out the property that motivates the paper. The cylinder is placed where O's faces touch it, and the hand closes by a fixed command (q0 + 5°) under stiff position control with rigid contact. The squeeze force is then set by how far the redesigned distal face penetrates or retreats relative to O's placement, and that is exactly what Δ_contact (or better, the local face offset, ρ = 0.92) measures. Δ_skel is not predictive because the object never touches the skeleton. So the result follows almost from construction, and the more useful findings are the dose–response with stiffness (U drops to 44% at 0.1·K0) and that force matching removes the error (3–7%). Those suggest the practical recommendation might simply be "reuse forces, not positions". Also: (a) the interface property varied is friction, while the paper is motivated by thick *compliant* pads and tactile modules, and with rigid contact thickness has no mechanical effect besides geometry; (b) the error compares a third property's effect across hands. That is not the target use case (comparing interface A on hand A against interface B on hand B). It is a reasonable proxy, but the paper should explain why. (c) In the crowned-pad test, Δ_skel and Δ_contact are anti-correlated, and the main set has ρ(Δ_skel, E) = −0.40. That suggests the two are structurally coupled (Prop. 3 says they differ by the depth change), which limits how "apart" the selected redesigns really are. The partial correlation for Δ_skel uses a "symmetric log error" while the others use raw error. That looks like analysis flexibility, so report all with the same transform. The "standardized coefficient +0.08" for thickness comes from an unspecified model.

(4) Stage-1 requirements and presentation. The venue asks for fully fleshed hypotheses and methodology, including what is learned if results are negative. The physical stage (C5) gets three sentences: no metric, no interfaces fixed, no protocol for "tracked face motion", no plan for a negative result. The title is very long and reads like a slogan, and it names a "CoDe Hand" that is never shown. The writing is terse, with many short declarative sentences, which helps the storyline. But it leaves symbols undefined or overloaded: J means both the contact Jacobian and R_{π/2}; L_vp, x0, S, "servo/transmission table" and MA_wc are only sketched; Fig. 3's "(0.21, 0)" is cryptic. Units mix fractions (0.085–0.096) and points. There are small inconsistencies: Fig. 1d says p = 0.0001 while the text says 2×10⁻⁴. Fig. 1d's squares vs. circles are not explained (presumably R/U). Fig. 4a–b's bound line is hidden under the 2R-remote curve. The text says U "rises from 44% to 131% up to K0", but Fig. 5b shows ~118% at K0 and 131% at 10·K0. The robustness paragraph gives U errors of 127–184%, above the 52–124% range in the main result, without saying which hands or placements.

On balance, the idea is novel and potentially useful as a design principle. The math is correct and the reporting is careful. The empirical evidence is close to built in by the setup, it does not involve compliance, and it suggests force-based protocols largely remove the problem. That limits significance. The physical-stage plan is underspecified for a Stage-1 paper. I lean toward rejection but could be persuaded, since the venue explicitly allows preliminary validation.
