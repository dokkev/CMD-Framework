THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a robot finger has to be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should preserve the relative motion of the two contact faces (the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame, set by the finite pole of the distal phalanx). It should not preserve the skeleton (fingertip) path. The paper has three parts: (i) planar finite-pole analysis giving clearance, coverage and contact-surface-motion laws, a sum rule (Eq. 7) and a fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 8); (ii) two optimization studies showing that a remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more pad coverage and stays well under the fixed-axis bound; (iii) a simulation study (Isaac Lab / Newton / MuJoCo-Warp) showing that, under a reused flexion command, the error in a measured interface effect rank-correlates with Delta_contact and not with Delta_skel.

My intuition: the framing is new and useful. "Comparable interface studies need a controlled finger" is a real methodological issue in tactile and compliant-fingertip work. The geometric part is tidy. Lemma 1 is classical, but Props. 1-3 and the sum rule are clean, easy to check, and lead somewhere. Study 1/2 are reasonably careful (re-verification at 0.05 deg, cross-checking Eq. 5 against the evaluator to 1e-13).

My main concerns are with Study 3. I looked at it from a contact-modelling and statistics angle:

1. Much of the main result follows from the protocol. The object is placed "where O touches it with both pads at q0", then the PIP is commanded to q0+5 deg under a stiff joint impedance. A hand whose faces move like O's (small Delta_contact) touches at about q0, so it overcloses by about 5 deg and generates O's normal force. A hand with large Delta_contact touches earlier or later and gets a different preload. With mu going from 1.3 to 2.0, the limit load scales roughly as mu*N, so E(H) is essentially |N_H - N_O|/N_O. The paper's own controls confirm this. Matching contact force removes the error (3-7%). Softer actuation shrinks it. Closing from each finger's own touch makes Delta_skel the predictor (rho = 0.88) and Delta_contact much weaker (rho = 0.44, Fig. 5b). So the experiment mostly measures normal-force mismatch under position control with object placement keyed to the reference contact geometry. That is a valid point, but it is closer to a consistency check of the kinematic definition than an independent empirical finding. A simple analytic model (overclosure angle -> penetration -> N -> limit load) would probably predict E(H) from Delta_contact almost exactly. The paper should state this model and test it, not frame the result as a discovery. The headline "skeleton motion does not predict it" holds only for one protocol. Under touch-referenced closure, a common protocol in tactile studies, the skeleton is what matters. The abstract does not say this.

2. The three interface effects are not independent replications. Friction, compliance (contact stiffness 2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m) and shape (flat -> 2 mm crown) are all measured on the same hands, the same placements and the same reused command. All three plausibly act through the same preload mismatch (a softer contact under fixed overclosure gives less N). "Compliance and shape effects repeat this" should not be read as three lines of evidence. The shape result (n = 6) is very thin.

3. The contact model does not represent what the paper is about. Thick compliant pads matter because of thickness-dependent compliance, patch size, and pressure distribution. Here a pad is a rigid geometric volume, and "compliance" is a solver contact-stiffness parameter that does not depend on thickness. A 9 mm silicone pad and a 3 mm pad with the same contact stiffness is not a physically consistent pair. The same applies to "pad compliance" as an interface change. The simulation is also planar (cylinder in the sagittal plane), so there is no torsional friction, no out-of-plane slip and no patch-level effects. This is acceptable for a preliminary study, but the claims about "measured interface effects" should be limited to this abstraction.

4. Statistics. The unit of analysis is a hand, and there are 15 hands. The hands were hand-picked "on design metrics" (not sampled), and 3 of the 18 were excluded because they could not hold the cylinder. Those exclusions are informative: their distal face retreats 1.5-2.6 mm. Their Delta_contact values should be reported, and the analysis should treat them as failures (e.g. maximal error, rank-based), not drop them. Hands cluster by thickness, and Delta_contact is strongly tied to thickness (the bound grows with Delta_h). The pooled Spearman therefore partly reflects thickness. "It does so at each thickness" with about 5 hands per thickness needs numbers. A stratified or mixed-effects analysis would be more convincing. The 95% interval [0.41, 1.00] is wide, and an upper end of exactly 1.00 suggests a percentile bootstrap at the boundary. Partial correlations with n = 15 are fragile. The Wilcoxon p <= 3e-4 for R vs U "pair by pair" cannot come from 3 R/U pairs (the smallest one-sided exact p with 3 pairs is 0.125). It must treat placements x directions x conditions as samples, which is pseudo-replication relative to a hand-level claim. Many tests are run (3 effects x 3 protocols x partials x robustness) with no multiplicity control. "All gates were fixed before the runs" is a good practice and deserves credit.

5. The sum rule confounds the comparison of predictors. Eq. 7 says Delta_contact + Delta_skel >= 2 sin(q/2)|Delta_t|. At fixed thickness, designs near that bound must trade one for the other, so Delta_skel is anticorrelated with Delta_contact by construction. Fig. 1d reports rho = -0.40 for error vs Delta_skel. This negative sign is mostly a geometric consequence, not evidence that the skeleton "does not matter". The paper partially addresses this with partial correlations, but it should state the confound openly. It should also include an explicit skeleton-kept redesign (minimal Delta_skel), since the introduction offers "keep the skeleton" as the natural alternative. U (coverage only) is a weaker baseline than that.

6. Smaller issues. The setup says "three fingers" but the grasp looks like a single-finger proximal/distal enclosure. It is unclear what the three fingers contribute or whether they are pooled. The number of placements, q0 values and q_req for Study 3 are not given in the text (Fig. 1 says 105 deg). Text says U presses 3.3x harder; the Fig. 1 caption says 3.9x (17.4/4.5 N). These are probably different statistics but should be reconciled. It is unclear whether the object placement is defined relative to the proximal face (Fp) or to the MCP/world. With 4 mm thicker pads this matters a lot. The touch-referenced protocol's 20% median error is not small, and R's error under that protocol is not reported.

On Study 1/2: these are optimization comparisons with 3 GA/NSGA-II seeds plus an extra refinement step for CFL only. The paper says the result holds without refinement, which helps. MA_wc is unconstrained and changes by up to 16%. The paper says control compensates the transmission, which is true kinematically, but force capacity and back-drivability also change, and for a contact study that matters. 2R-remote beats CFL on the coverage objective at 90 deg. The paper is honest about this.

Overall: a good idea and a solid geometric core, but the empirical support for C4 is weaker than presented. It mostly reflects a preload mismatch built in by the protocol, uses a contact model without thickness-dependent pad mechanics, and relies on small, selected, clustered samples with pseudo-replicated tests. For a Stage-1 RSS submission, preliminary experiments are allowed and the Stage-2 plan is concrete and falsifiable, which counts in its favour. Still, the claims in the abstract and C4 need to be scoped to "reused stiff joint-position commands", and the statistical analysis needs tightening. I lean towards rejection but could be persuaded.
