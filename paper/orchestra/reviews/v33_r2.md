ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper makes three claims about a planar two-phalanx finger whose PIP servo sits on the proximal phalanx and drives a crossed four-bar (CFL).

Claim 1, room: remote actuation plus a virtual pole keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger at every pad thickness and required flexion.
- Evidence: Propositions 1-2 (clearance and coverage laws under symmetry assumptions S1-S2) and an optimization study over 4 joints x 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions (Fig. 3a-c).
- I checked the algebra of Eq. (3): lowering the pole by delta does add 2*delta*tan(q/2) to both numerator and denominator, as stated.
- The comparison against 2R-DD holds in all 10 conditions where both have a design (2.4 to 14.6 points).
- Fig. 3a-b, however, shows 2R-remote (same pin joint, belt-driven) at or above the CFL at 90 deg and for most thicknesses at 120 deg. The CFL only separates at 150 deg and at the thickest pads. The room claim is therefore mostly a remote-actuation claim; the four-bar earns its place on coverage only at deep flexion. The text says this in half a sentence, but the contribution bullet and abstract do not.
- The coverage metric is bespoke (cylinder inscribed in the V of the two faces, three poses at 90 deg and above) and is never tied to a grasp outcome. For q_req = 90 deg the three evaluation poses collapse to one.

Claim 2, redesign: a fixed axis cannot preserve contact-surface motion when the pad thickens; the CFL keeps the change to 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound at no coverage cost.
- Eqs. (6)-(8) are correct and elementary (Delta c = (I - R_q) Delta p; triangle inequality). The numbers are consistent: 3 to 9 mm at 120 deg gives a bound of about 10.4 mm, which 2R-remote meets in Fig. 3e.
- The bound is conditional on a mounting rule, not on kinematics alone: "a pin cannot sit below the pad's mount" and "full-width pads leave no room for lugs". A pin with side lugs, a narrower pad, or a spacer under the thin pad would escape it. 2R-DD already sits below the bound for the same reason (its servo holds the face deep). The paper's own relaxation (moving c0) gives 0.59-0.80 of the bound. So "cannot" is too strong; "cannot under these packaging rules" is what is shown.
- Delta_contact is the maximum position difference of one face point, including the offset at q = 0. That is a path difference, not a Jacobian difference, and only for the PIP column. The title and the contribution name ("keep the contact Jacobian") are broader than the quantity optimized.
- Pareto fronts for Eq. (10) are not shown; only the most-coverage point of each front is plotted.

Claim 3, measurement: under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact and not Delta_skel.
- Evidence: Isaac Lab pull-out (15, 14, 45 hands), Drake with point and hydroelastic contact, two more open-loop tasks, Table I with bootstrap intervals, Holm correction, partial correlations. For an extended abstract this is a generous amount of computational evidence, and the falsification language (what would refute each prediction) is welcome.
- The mechanism is close to definitional. If the same joint target is commanded at stiffness K0 and the face has moved by Delta_contact, the preload changes by roughly K0-scaled Delta_contact, and a limit load scales with preload. The correlation confirms the mechanism but is not surprising. The first-order prediction matches for R (25 vs 21%) and poorly for U (230 vs 124%).
- The result is protocol-specific, and the paper's own Table I shows it. With re-zeroing at touch (which the paper says "practice does"), the residual is 6-61% and follows the skeleton (rho 0.88) rather than the contact surfaces (0.44, interval includes zero). With force matching the residual is 3-7% on any design. So the conclusion "preserve the contact surface, not the skeleton" flips with the protocol, and the cheapest remedy is a protocol change, not a redesign. The paper does not report what R and U score under re-zeroing.
- The hands are a constructed population (redesigns chosen to "reach a set Delta_contact while keeping or changing the skeleton most"), and E is a median over placements "where both hands hold", which conditions on survival. Hand counts in Table I (15, 14) differ from the 18 stated, without explanation.
- "Each test was pre-registered" cannot be checked; no registry or supplementary record is given.
- "Near 20%" is the headline for R at 7 mm; R is 41% at 9 mm, and in the sign-reversal example R reports -55% against a true -41%. This is much better than U (+48%), but "small" in the abstract of contributions is generous.
- No hardware. A CAD render and a planned test are given. Acceptable for this format, but the claims about real pads (friction, compliance, texture) rest on point-contact and hydroelastic simulation.

Engineering costs are mentioned only in passing: worst-case mechanical advantage 0.41-0.58 (about half the torque of a direct joint), with nothing on link loads, backlash, lateral stiffness, part count, or the MCP joint.

Presentation: the writing is precise but compressed to the point of being hard to follow on first read. CFL is never expanded. M is both the generic design variable and a named redesign in Sec. V-A. Terms such as "the goal", "cells", "the grid", "O's placements", "front's most-coverage design" carry a lot of weight with one-clause definitions. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4 panels are very small at print size. Related work is 12 lines and omits linkage motion-generation synthesis, which is the classical framing of the redesign problem.

Scores rest on: a clear and, to my knowledge, new framing (hold contact-surface motion fixed so the interface is the variable); correct if simple analysis; broad but simulation-only and protocol-conditional evidence; a room claim that is mostly attributable to remote actuation; and dense presentation. I place it at the accept threshold for an extended abstract.
