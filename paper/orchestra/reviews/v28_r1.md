ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer r1 (robot-hand designer; has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them).

The paper makes two claims about a servo-driven finger whose PIP is a crossed four-bar linkage (CFL) driven by a servo on the proximal phalanx.

(C1) Room. Remote actuation plus a polycentric PIP keeps more pad coverage (C̄_pad, a cylinder-in-the-V proxy) than a servo-at-joint revolute finger (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. Evidence: planar GA/NSGA-II optimization, three seeds, in 10 of 10 comparable cells (+2.4 to +14.6 points, Fig. 3a-c). Clearance and coverage laws (Props. 1-2) explain the result: a pole that can sit on the face shrinks the joint gap. The evidence is internally consistent, and the refutation condition is stated in advance. Three things weaken it as a design claim. (i) Fig. 3a-b shows 2R-remote beating the CFL at 90° and at 120° up to about 5 mm, so much of the "room" comes from moving the servo off the joint, not from the CFL. The paper says so, but the contribution bullet attributes the result to "remote servos and a CFL" together. (ii) Coverage is a planar, sagittal-section metric. It ignores what decides room on a real phalanx: the width taken by four pins and two crossed links beside or above the pads, the servo body now sitting on the proximal phalanx (Fig. 1b), cable routing, and the lug stock the pins need. The text dismisses pins for fixed axes because "full-width pads leave no room for lugs", but the CFL also has four pins, and the paper never says where their lugs go relative to a full-width pad. (iii) The CFL's worst-case mechanical advantage is 0.41-0.58. On a servo hand that roughly halves the available PIP torque. The paper reports this but does not weigh it against coverage.

(C2) Keep the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion c(q) when the face deepens: Corollary 1 gives the lower bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|. The CFL redesign reaches 10-51% of this bound at near-best coverage (Sec. III-E). In simulation, under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured friction or stiffness effect follows Δ_contact: ρ = 0.80-0.86 in Isaac Lab, 0.67-0.86 in Drake with point and hydroelastic contact, and 0.82-0.87 in grasp acquisition and dial turning. The skeleton change does not predict it. The kinematic part is clean and, as far as I can tell, correct: Prop. 3 is the standard pole-displacement identity and Corollary 1 follows directly. The simulation part is carefully hedged: pre-registered tests, Holm correction, hand bootstrap, partial correlations with Δ_skel held, and an honest note that with 14-16 hands the lead over Δ_skel is not significant. The mechanism is plausible and well identified: the error enters through the preload, because a face that moves changes how hard the same setpoint presses (R 0.75×, U 3.3×). The sign-reversal example (+48% vs. -41%) is a vivid and useful result for anyone who swaps pads on a servo hand.

My main reservations about C2 come from bench practice.
(a) The headline sensitivity sits at about 1 mm of Δ_contact. That is the same size as the effects the simulators leave out: compression and shear of 5-9 mm soft pads under the grasp preload, servo gear backlash (a few tenths of a degree at the PIP, so a few tenths of a mm at the face), and compliance and clearance in four extra pin joints. Point contact with a stiffness parameter, and hydroelastic contact with the same 3 mm layer on every hand, do not model a thick pad that rolls and shears. Without that, "keeping Δ_contact below 1 mm keeps the error near 20%" is a simulation-only threshold.
(b) The reused setpoint is the crux. In my experience, groups that swap pads re-zero the grasp: close until current or touch, then add an offset. The paper tests "touch at every finger" (6-61%, follows the skeleton) and an ideal torque (9-53%). Neither is described well enough to tell whether they match common practice. Present-current thresholds on Dynamixel-class servos are the obvious practical alternative, and they are missing. Force matching (3-7%) is dismissed because it "needs force sensing at both pads". That is fair, but it shows the problem has a protocol solution as well as a mechanism solution, and the framing should say so.
(c) E is normalized by |Δ(O)|. When the interface effect on O is small, E inflates. The paper does not report the size of Δ(O) per setting, so the 95-228% figures are hard to interpret physically.
(d) The motivating premise, an interface's "own effect apart from the finger", is conceptually delicate for thickness. A thicker pad legitimately changes the geometry, and the paper's choice of O as the reference is a definition, not a necessity. I accept the definition for friction and stiffness swaps. The abstract, however, also names tactile modules, and nothing in the paper tests one.

Stage 2 plan: swappable pad modules and CFL links on one finger. Hands O, R, M, U have Δ_contact of 0.9, 2.3 and 3.9 mm while Δ_skel falls. The test uses a cylinder on a planar slide, bare vs. textured covers, 5 placements × 4 directions × 5 repeats, and a Page trend test, against predictions of 21, 72 and 124%. This is concrete and falsifiable. The design also dissociates Δ_contact from Δ_skel by construction, which is exactly what is needed. Three gaps remain. Four hands give little power if the trend is not monotone. Marker-based measurement of c(q) on a soft, covered pad to sub-mm accuracy is non-trivial and not specified. A textured cover itself changes thickness and compliance, so the interface swap is not pure friction. I would also want O measured with its own pads swapped to 7 mm (the "−37%" case) as a hardware anchor.

Presentation: the writing is compressed to the point of telegraphic. Many numbers sit in single sentences, symbols (S1, S2, t vs. h, z, x0, G, L_vp) are introduced in passing, and Sec. IV-A packs four protocols into one paragraph. Fig. 2b is hard to read. Fig. 1 has no caption text for panel (a). The figures themselves are information-dense and well matched to the text. The related work is appropriately narrow for an extended abstract but omits hands with remote actuation that already carry phalanx pads or skins.

Scores. For Stage 1 the hypotheses, methods and falsification plan are well laid out, and the kinematic analysis is a genuine, reusable insight for hand designers. The empirical case rests on simulated contact that is weakest exactly where the claimed effect lives (sub-mm face motion of thick soft pads), and the practical comparison against routine recalibration protocols is thin. I lean accept.
