ASSESSMENT NOTES:

What the paper claims. Suppose a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface and the original hand's command (a joint impedance setpoint, or a learned policy) is reused. The paper argues that the kinematic quantity to preserve is the motion of the contact surfaces, measured in the proximal-phalanx frame (Δ^prox_distal), not the skeleton or fingertip path (Δ_skel). A finite-displacement pole argument (Eqs. 2–4) gives a lower bound: a fixed axis cannot preserve contact motion when the surfaces deepen, and no redesign can preserve both contact and skeleton motion (Eq. 3). A crossed four-bar linkage (CFL) at the PIP joint lets the designer place the pole. The authors report three pre-registered hypotheses:
- H1: the effect error grows with Δ^prox_distal, not Δ_skel.
- H2: this is not just a preload/contact-force effect.
- H3: a reused policy keeps the most performance on the redesign that preserves the motion its task uses.

Evidence for each hypothesis.

- **Design and theory.**
  - Eq. 1 checks out: 2·tan 60° = 3.46. Eq. 2 is correct for equal rotation angle and the same c0, since ‖(I−R_q)v‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖v‖. Eq. 3 is a clean, useful impossibility statement.
  - The four-joint comparison (at-joint, remote, RCJ, CFL) is a fair optimized-vs-optimized comparison. Each architecture gets its own optimum, pivot heights are freed for all, and the actuator is shared.
  - The CFL wins coverage in 10/10 feasible conditions by 2.4–14.6 pp and has the lowest Δ^prox in 9/9 conditions.
  - This part is solid but modest in size. At 120° the coverage gap is 73% vs 70% for the "remote" variant.
- **H1.**
  - Isaac Lab pull-out: ρ = 0.94 over 27 grid redesigns; partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δ_skel held; Holm-corrected tests across four perturbations.
  - Drake, four tasks, two contact models: ρ is 0.91–0.93 for hold and acquire but only 0.43–0.53 for dial and pivot.
  - The headline example is strong: the coverage-only redesign flips the sign of a compliant interface's pull-out effect.
  - My main concern is that under a joint-setpoint impedance command, contact force is K·(q_target − q_contact), and q_contact is set by where the surface meets the object. So H1 is close to a mechanical consequence of the control law. The real scientific content is the negative half (skeleton preservation does not help) and the size of the effect.
  - Commands that set torque or force largely remove the effect (Table I: errors fall to the −32…+17% range). This shows the scope is tied to position-type commands, which the paper acknowledges only in passing.
- **H2.**
  - One partial correlation (ρ = 0.80 under an equal reused torque, with force difference held).
  - One re-referencing experiment: Drake error drops from 42% to 14%, and the remainder still follows Δ^prox.
  - This addresses the obvious confound but is thin, and "contact force matched" is not operationalized exactly as in the pre-registered failure criterion.
- **H3.**
  - Three PPO tasks, two training seeds each, 7 mm only.
  - Results are mixed by design. CoDe wins finger-walk and cube rotation; Skeleton wins the fingertip-end dial.
  - The authors reinterpret this as "a policy keeps what its task touches." That is consistent with H3 as worded, but the per-task "motion its task uses" is identified after the fact.
  - The cube-rotation statistic (ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns) uses a new metric, the proximal-pad-vs-palm shift, which is not Δ^prox_distal or Δ^base_distal. This looks post hoc.
  - CoDe keeps only about 55–75% in Isaac Lab, so even the "best" redesign loses a lot.

What the scores rest on.
- **Value (3).** The question "what must be kept when the interface changes" is relevant to tactile and compliant-skin research and to cross-embodiment transfer. The pole framing is elegant and general. It is limited by:
  - a single planar PIP;
  - a fixed MCP;
  - near-tautological behaviour under setpoint control;
  - narrow practical scope, since force or torque commands mostly dissolve the problem.
- **Feasibility (2).** There is a lot of simulation evidence and the stats are careful, but:
  - everything is simulated with rigid, non-deforming pads, even though the motivating interfaces (gel, compliant, tactile) are defined by deformation;
  - H3 rests on two seeds and one thickness;
  - some numbers in the text do not reconcile with the tables (see clarity notes);
  - the hardware plan is credible but unstarted.

  Strong competing submissions will bring hardware. Here the hardware test is exactly what decides whether the ordering survives contact migration in real pads.

I still recommend inviting to rebuttal. The hypothesis is crisp and falsifiable, the charter is executable, and negative hardware results would also be informative.
