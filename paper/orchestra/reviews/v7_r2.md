THOUGHT:

My background is manipulation and contact modelling, so I focus on Study 3 (Sec. V). That is where the paper says the kept quantity matters, and it carries the significance of the whole submission. Secs. III–IV support the paper's own design claims well enough. Sec. V's support is weaker than the confident wording suggests.

What the paper argues. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker interface, the designer should keep the relative motion of the two contact faces (one point plus the face rotation, written in the proximal-phalanx frame) rather than the skeleton motion. A fixed-axis joint cannot do this when pads get thicker, unless the pads are recessed. A crossed four-bar can move its pole and comes much closer. In simulation, reusing one command across redesigns distorts measured interface effects in step with Delta_contact.

Theory (Sec. III). Lemma 1 is the classical finite-displacement pole. Prop. 3 is the identity c - c' = (I - R_q)(p - p'), with |I - R_q| = 2 sin(q/2). It is correct, and the paper uses it cleanly. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality, since the face-referenced and link-referenced poles differ by Delta_t. Corollary 1 also holds: the max over q is reached at q_req because 2 sin(q/2) increases on (0, pi). These results are tidy, but none is deep. The contribution is to frame them as design laws for pad-carrying joints. That framing is new and useful, but "we prove" oversells a few lines of planar kinematics. Props. 1 and 2 rest on strong symmetry assumptions (S1 equal depths, S2 equidistant corners, equal pads). The authors do check Eq. (4) against the evaluator (median 0.05 mm on CFL designs), which helps.

The term "contact Jacobian" is non-standard. In grasping, the contact Jacobian maps joint velocities to velocities at the actual object contact points. Here c is a fixed material point on the distal face. Because the face is rigid, one point plus phi does determine the face's motion, so the substance holds. Still, readers from manipulation will expect the contact point to depend on the object, and the paper should state the difference explicitly.

Design studies (Sec. IV). Study 1 is a reasonable optimization benchmark. Concerns:
(i) CFL alone gets an extra "refinement from neighbouring designs". The authors say the result holds without it, which is good, but they should give the unrefined numbers.
(ii) Three seeds per condition, with no spread reported.
(iii) Honestly, 2R-remote beats the CFL on C̄pad at 90° and at thin pads at 120°. The paper switches to a second measure, pad length over fixed L_f, to claim CFL leads "beyond 3 mm". This is a mild case of moving the metric.
Study 2 is the strongest design result. The CFL changes Delta_contact by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound at near-best coverage, and Fig. 4d is convincing. The control showing that coverage-only designs change Delta_contact by 1.28–3.91 mm is a good touch.

Study 3, the simulation study. This is my main concern. My notes:

1. The candidate set is designed, not sampled, and this shapes the correlations. The 15–18 "hands" are the authors' redesigns: R, U, and redesigns "at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". The redesigns were chosen to spread Delta_contact widely, while transmission is matched by construction and coverage is near-best. Under that range restriction, the other candidates cannot show a correlation of any size. Worse, Eq. (7) forces Delta_contact + Delta_skel >= 2 sin(q_req/2)|Delta_t|. Designs pushed to keep one quantity near its bound change the other. The negative rho for Delta_skel (-0.40, -0.51) is then largely a structural result of how the set was built. It is not independent evidence that the skeleton "does not help". The "five candidate invariants" comparison is therefore not a fair horse race. A fair version would draw redesigns at random, e.g. random feasible points from the Study 2 front or the joint feasible set, and then correlate.

2. The protocol is circular. The cylinder is placed "where the reference O touches it with both pads at q0", and the PIP closes to q0 + 5°. The protocol is thus defined in O's contact-face geometry. Any hand that reproduces O's face motion reproduces the initial contact state almost by definition. Any hand that does not either over-closes (U presses 3.3–3.9× harder) or under-closes. So the error tracks Delta_contact because of the geometry of a stiff position-controlled closure. That is expected, and largely a statement about preload. The reused-torque result (rho = 0.75, error 3–40%) is the more informative test and should lead. Even there, the measured effect is a limit load, which depends strongly on where the contact normals point. Normal direction is again set by face geometry relative to an O-defined placement. I would like a protocol in which the object pose is set by something other than O's faces, e.g. a fixed external pose or the hand's own first touch, to see whether Delta_contact still predicts the error.

3. Small n and the inference behind it. With n = 14–16 hands, Spearman intervals are wide (friction [0.41, 1.00]). Table I bolds an entry when its bootstrap CI excludes zero. That covers 5 candidates × 3 conditions = 15 tests with no multiplicity correction. "Positive at each thickness" rests on roughly 5 hands per thickness, which says little. The partial correlation 0.88 "with Delta_skel fixed" is hard to read given point 1. Placements and directions are collapsed into a per-hand median, and their spread is not shown. Fig. 5 has no error bars. No noise floor is reported. How much does E vary when O is re-run against itself with perturbed placement, solver seed or timestep? Without it, R's 3% at 5 mm cannot be told apart from zero or from numerical noise.

4. Effect sizes undercut the claim. Even the best redesign, R, errs 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. Those are large biases for an interface study, and they arise exactly where thick interfaces matter. Meanwhile, a simple protocol change (O's reused torque, or force matching at 3–7%) removes most of the error without any kinematic redesign. The paper argues that force or touch signals are changed by the interface. But joint torque from servo current is not an interface signal, and reused torque already gives 3–40%. The practical takeaway might simply be "match force, not position". The paper needs to argue more carefully why kinematic invariance is still needed once actuation is force-matched. The "at equal force too" result (rho = 0.75) is the right evidence for this, but it is a single sentence.

5. Contact-model fidelity. The pads are "represented by a contact model" in Newton/MuJoCo-Warp. A thick elastomer pad (5–9 mm) differs from a thin one mainly in bulk compliance, patch growth and tangential compliance. Penalty or soft-constraint contact on a rigid pad geometry does not capture these, and "pad stiffness" is changed as a contact-stiffness parameter (2.5 to 1.0 kN/m). The thickness effects the paper wants to compare across hands are therefore largely absent from the simulation, apart from their geometric consequences. The robustness checks (contact stiffness ×10, impedance ratio ×0.3/×3, doubled substeps) are welcome. But they cover only the paired R-vs-U comparison, not the correlation in Table I. It is also unclear which of Newton and MuJoCo-Warp produced which numbers, and whether the two agree. Friction cone type, slip detection threshold and load ramp rate should be stated, because limit-load measurements in MuJoCo-class solvers are sensitive to all three.

6. Scope. The MCP is held at 0, the problem is planar, and the cylinder is free only in the sagittal plane. Real interface studies involve the MCP, whose column is not kept (the paper says so). The sim uses a single object class with two radii.

7. Failures as data. Three of 18 hands cannot hold the cylinder, and they are dropped from the main correlation. Counting them as worst gives 0.81, which is good to report. Still, the main figure should show them.

Presentation. The writing is compact and mostly precise, but dense. Many coined terms (CFL ideal, 2R-remote, R/U/O, Delta_contact vs Delta_skel, contour length) appear within two pages. Table I is hard to parse; the "Hands" row and the column grouping need clearer layout. Fig. 1d and Fig. 5a overlap. Fig. 1 says 15 redesigns while the text says 18. That is consistent, but readers have to reconcile it. The title is too long. The Stage-2 plan is concrete: one physical finger in which only the pads and CFL links change, plus face tracking. It fits the venue's Stage-1 format well.

Overall. The idea of keeping the contact-surface motion, not the skeleton, is a clean and original framing, and the design-side evidence is decent. The causal claim that this is the property that matters for interface studies rests on a designed candidate set, an O-referenced placement and a contact model that ignores what makes thick pads different. For a Stage-1 abstract, preliminary experiments are acceptable. But the paper presents Sec. V's claims ("Only the contact-surface motion predicts the error") with a confidence the statistics do not support. I lean toward rejection in the current form, and I could be persuaded if the authors soften Sec. V and commit to a random-sample and non-O-referenced protocol.
