# Review v2 — Reviewer 1

## THOUGHT (notes)

### What the paper claims
- Problem: comparing contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, tactile modules) on a robot finger usually requires redesigning the finger, which confounds the interface effect with the embodiment change.
- Proposal: the redesign should keep the *contact-surface motion*, meaning c(q), the distal-face point in the proximal-phalanx frame, or equivalently the PIP column J = [c'; 1] of the contact Jacobian. It should not try to keep the skeleton.
- Tool: the classical finite pole (Bottema & Roth). From it the authors derive a symmetric clearance law (Prop. 1), a coverage law (Prop. 2), a trajectory law ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp|| (Prop. 3), and a fixed-axis bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δh − Δd| (Cor. 1).
- Evidence:
  - Study 1 (H1): best coverage C̄_pad for four PIP architectures (2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, CFL) × 4 pad thicknesses × 3 flexion requirements.
  - Study 2 (H2): CFL redesigned for thicker pads changes c(q) by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound at near-best coverage.
  - Study 3 (H3, simulation): over 15 CFL hands that hold the cylinder, the error in a measured friction effect under reused position commands correlates with D_c (Spearman 0.83) and not with the skeleton change D_s (−0.40). Torque reuse and softer impedance shrink the dependence.
  - H4 (hardware) is Stage 2.

### My intuitions
- The core idea is clean and useful: specify a redesign by what the object "sees", the relative motion of the contact faces, rather than by link geometry. Using the finite pole to reason about pad clearance is elegant. Prop. 3 and Cor. 1 are correct. I checked (I−R_q)Δp: its norm is 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|, and the Lemma 1 inverse ½(I + cot(q/2)J) is right. The math is classical, so the originality lies in the application and in framing "contact-surface motion" as the invariant for interface experiments.
- Study 1 is honest. 2R-remote wins at 90°, the curves cross at 120°, CFL wins at 150°, and RCJ-ideal shows the packaging branch of H1. I like that H1 has a stated falsification branch and that part of it actually shows up.
- Study 2 is honestly labelled "partial". But by the paper's own Study 3 scale, the 9 mm CFL redesigns (D_c L_f 2.3–4.3 mm) sit where the measured error is already about 40% or more. So the "keeping" that CFL achieves beyond 7 mm is not sufficient for the stated purpose. The paper should say this explicitly and tie Study 2 to Study 3. Otherwise H2's "holds partially" reads stronger than it is.
- Study 3 is the most important part, and I see three problems with it:
  1. **Near-tautology under position reuse.** If you command the same flexion on two geometries and the contact faces sit in different places, the interference, and so the normal force, differs. A friction-limited load then scales with that force. That the error tracks D_c is almost guaranteed by construction. The authors show this themselves: "the error enters through the contact", and U presses 3.3–3.9× harder. Under torque reuse the error falls to 3–40%. Under force matching it falls to 3–7%. This suggests that much of the confound goes away with a force- or torque-matched protocol, which any careful interface experimenter would use anyway. The conclusion "an interface experiment must keep how the contact surfaces move, not the skeleton" is stronger than the evidence. A fair reading is "under position or impedance protocols, D_c governs the error. Under torque or force protocols it matters much less but still orders the error."
  2. **Rigid contact for a paper motivated by thick compliant pads.** Thickness enters only geometrically, and compliance differences between 3 mm and 9 mm pads are absent. The paper itself notes that above K0 the contact compliance "takes the interference", so the size of H3 depends directly on the missing contact-stiffness robustness (the TODO). This is the key result I need for Stage 1: E vs D_c at two or three contact stiffnesses spanning soft silicone to rigid, at least for R and U, plus a solver and timestep check.
  3. **Statistics and sample design.** n=15, and the hands were deliberately "chosen so that D_c, D_s and C̄_pad vary apart", so the ρ against D_s depends on how the sample was built. A negative ρ (−0.40) against skeleton change is odd and probably reflects that R was constructed with a large skeleton change. The claim "thickness adds nothing once D_c is known" needs a partial correlation or a small regression with its numbers. The position-reuse set (n=15) and torque-reuse set (n=16) are different subsets. Three hands that fail to hold are excluded, and their D_c should be reported.
- **Internal inconsistencies I noticed:**
  - Fig. 1 caption says U "presses 3.9× harder" (17.4/4.5 N in Fig. 1b–a), but Sec. V-D says 3.3×.
  - Sec. V-D says "hands with D_c L_f ≥ 2.9 mm cannot hold the cylinder", yet Fig. 1d and Fig. 5a plot holding hands at 3.0, 3.1, 3.5, 3.9 and 5.1 mm, including U itself at about 3.9 mm.
  - Fig. 1 says R "grips like O", but R's distal force is 3.0 N against O's 4.5 N (−33%), and R still has a 21% error.
  - ρ is used both for cylinder radius and for Spearman's coefficient in the same paragraph.
  - Fig. 5b: the text says U rises "from 44 to 131% up to K0 and levels off", but 131% is the 10 K0 value. At K0 it is about 119%.
  - In Fig. 1d the circles are drawn open, while in Fig. 5a open markers mean torque reuse.
  - The title says "CoDe Hand", but no hand exists yet. Only a planar finger is analysed.
- "c′, the pole of the relative motion" is imprecise. c′ is the velocity of a face point, which *encodes* the instantaneous pole but is not the pole. The paper otherwise works with the *finite* pole, so the reader has to juggle the instantaneous pole (J), the finite pole (Lemma 1) and D_c (positions). This needs one clean sentence.
- Clarity: the paper is extremely dense. The abstract packs five sets of numbers. Many symbols (M, K, C, A, S, Fp, r1,e, r2,s, x0, G, z, τ_i, S1, S2, P1–P4, Q1–Q3, H1–H4) are introduced with little space. The prose is clipped ("Four reasons: ..."), and several sentences need two reads. Q1–Q3 duplicate H1–H3. The study numbering (Study 1/2/3) and the figure numbering differ, and Fig. 4 (pole concept) is placed after Fig. 3 even though Sec. IV uses it.
- Related work is short (fine for Stage 1), but some directly relevant threads are missing:
  - kinematic synthesis of polycentric or four-bar finger joints, where instantaneous-centre placement is standard;
  - modular or exchangeable fingertip and tactile-module designs (e.g., Digit-style swappable sensors);
  - prior work on confounds or controlled comparisons in tactile and fingertip benchmarking.
- Stage-1 fit: the problem, hypotheses and methodology are fleshed out, and falsification branches are given. That matches the RSS Stage-1 criteria. Two things argue for a weak reject. First, the main practical claim (H3) is shown only in rigid-contact simulation and under the protocol that makes it most likely. Second, the presentation has visible inconsistencies. I could be persuaded upward if the robustness TODO comes back clean and the conclusion is scoped to the protocol.

### What I would need from the TODO
- E vs D_c (and vs D_s) at contact stiffnesses spanning compliant pads, with the ranking of R and U preserved and ρ reported per stiffness.
- Solver and timestep sensitivity on at least R, U and O, showing the effect differences are much larger than numerical variation.
- The shape interface (a second interface property beyond friction): does D_c still order the error?
- Ideally, one experiment where the interface change itself involves compliance or thickness, not only μ.
