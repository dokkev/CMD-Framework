ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper makes two claims about a planar two-DoF servo finger. C1: a remotely actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more room for thick pads than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD). C2: when a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker pad, the interface effect measured under a reused impedance setpoint is preserved if the redesign keeps the contact-surface motion c(q), which is the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal frame. Keeping the skeleton does not do this. The paper also bounds how far a fixed axis must change c(q) (Eq. 8), and the CFL stays at 10–51% of that bound.

Evidence for C1 (Study 1). Coverage is a geometric score: a cylinder inscribed in the V between the faces, averaged over three poses. Clearance is checked exactly. The comparison uses GA plus NSGA-II with three seeds per condition. The worst of six CFL runs beats the best 2R-DD run in 9 of 10 cells, CFL leads without the refinement step, and idealized upper bounds are given. For an optimizer-based architecture comparison this is reasonably done. Two caveats remain: three seeds is few, and CFL has more parameters than 2R-DD. Still, a 2.4–14.6 point margin is probably robust. The claim is geometric, not contact-mechanical, and the paper does not oversell it.

Evidence for C2 (Studies 2–3). Study 2 largely confirms the analysis: Corollary 1 is a lower bound, and the optimized CFL sits below it. Study 3 carries the contact-mechanics claim, and my scores rest on it.

Strengths of Study 3:
- The redesigns are chosen to separate Δ_contact from Δ_skel. Designing the sample this way is better than relying on whatever correlation happens to occur.
- Partial correlations are reported with each competitor held fixed.
- Statistics are bootstrapped over hands, with one-sided permutation tests and Holm correction. Some analyses were committed before their runs, and post-hoc analyses are labelled.
- Hands that fail to hold the cylinder are scored as worst-case errors, and the result survives this.
- Robustness sweeps cover setpoint stiffness (100×), contact stiffness (×10 either way), friction impedance (×3) and substeps (doubled).
- The revolute control with Δ_skel = 0, which errs by 95–228%, convincingly shows that keeping the skeleton does not suffice.
Few design papers report statistics this carefully.

Weaknesses of Study 3, in order of how much they affect my judgement:

1. The contact model abstracts away what makes thick compliant pads interesting. The pads are meshes in point contact with a cylinder, and "pad stiffness" is the contact's normal stiffness. There is no contact patch, no pressure distribution, no torsional or patch friction, no conformation, and no thickness-dependent bulk compliance. The only interface changes tested are a friction coefficient and a normal stiffness. These are exactly the parameters a point-contact penalty model separates most cleanly from geometry. So the result shows that "independence" holds for the interface properties most likely to be independent, in a model where they are independent by construction, apart from where the contact sits. The general wording ("an interface can be studied on its own") goes beyond this. Tactile modules and real compliant pads, whose effect depends on which part of the pad loads and how much area engages, are not covered.

2. The mechanism looks largely kinematic. Under a position setpoint at fixed stiffness, grasp force comes from over-closure: the target angle minus the angle at which the face meets the cylinder. If the face path changes, the over-closure and the contact force change too. The paper says as much ("where the faces sit, not how thick the pad is"). The partial correlation with force deviation (0.79) and with the face's normal offset (0.69) point the same way. If so, much of E could be predicted analytically from c(q), the object pose and K_0, without a dynamics simulator. The paper should either show that analytic prediction or explain what the simulation adds beyond it. The kinematic reading does not weaken the claim, but it does narrow it: the confound is specific to over-closure-driven setpoint control. The "touch" protocol supports this. There Δ_contact loses significance (ρ = 0.44) while Δ_skel-held stays significant, and the torque protocol drops to 0.69. The paper scopes the claim to setpoint servo hands, which is fair, but the abstract headline does not mention this dependence.

3. The definition of the "true" interface effect is a modelling choice that partly decides the result. The true effect is O's change when given the soft pad's stiffness on the 3 mm geometry. Any geometric consequence of the thicker pad is therefore assigned to the redesign. That is consistent with the paper's framing. However, an experimenter who wants to know whether a 7 mm soft pad helps their hand may regard the thickness-induced geometry as part of the interface. This choice should be stated and justified explicitly, because "U reports +48% when it is −41%" depends on it.

4. Statistical inference. The "population" of hands is a designed set (n = 14–45), so bootstrap intervals over hands describe stability within that design, not generalization. Several issues follow:
- The within-thickness intervals are wide ([0.41, 0.99], [0.35, 0.98]), and Δ_contact grows with h.
- The grid column has n = 45, but the text says the pre-registered test ran on 27 new hands. It is unclear which set the reported ρ and p come from.
- Pad thickness gets ρ = 0.11 in the grid column, although the grid redesigns are all 7 mm.
- E is a ratio normalized by |Δ(O)|, so it inflates in directions or placements where O's effect is small. The median over placements and directions helps, but the distribution, or the share of placements with small |Δ(O)|, is not shown.
- The load-step resolution is 2.5–5.6% of O's effect. That is comparable to some of the reported errors (R at 3% for 5 mm).

5. Δ_contact is a maximum over the whole range q ≤ q_req (up to 120°), while the grasps occur at 85–115°. A summary restricted to the grasp poses, or the normal offset there, may explain more. The paper hints at this (the 0.69 partial correlation) but does not compare the two.

6. Stage 2 validation:
- The physical test covers friction only (bare versus textured covers), not stiffness.
- It uses three hands R, M, U, and M is never defined.
- The Page trend test across three hands is confounded by whatever else co-varies across R, M, U, such as Δ_skel or coverage. The authors should report Δ_skel for these three, or choose them to break that correlation, as they did in simulation.
- The planned measurement is a planar pull on a slide. Its link to the simulated limit load in four directions should be explained.
Apart from these points, the protocol states numeric predictions (21/72/124%) and failure conditions, which suits a Stage 1 submission.

Presentation. The paper is very dense. Many terse sentences need several readings, for example "Each alternative removes the over-closure with something such a hand rarely has." The fonts in Figs. 2, 4 and 5 are near the limit of legibility. Numbers across the abstract, Fig. 1 and the text use different bases (41% at O's contacts versus −37% on the grid; 0.7× versus 0.75×) without always saying which. The analysis (Lemma 1, Propositions 1–3, Corollary 1) is compact and, as far as I can check, correct.

Overall. The problem is real and under-studied: interface studies confounded by redesign. The hypotheses are falsifiable, the methodology is well developed for Stage 1, and the statistics are unusually careful. The main reservation is external validity. The contact model cannot represent the pad properties that motivate the work, and the effect is plausibly a kinematic over-closure effect specific to setpoint control. Neither point invalidates the Stage 1 claims as scoped in the body. I lean Accept with score 6.
