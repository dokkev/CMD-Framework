THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a robot finger has to be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should keep the motion of the contact surfaces relative to each other, not the skeleton motion. It formalizes this as c(q), the path of a distal-face point in a frame attached to the proximal contact face. It gives a finite-pole analysis (clearance, coverage and contact-surface motion) and a fixed-axis lower bound on the change in c. It shows in optimization that a remote-driven crossed four-bar (CFL) both keeps coverage and keeps c well inside that bound. In simulation it shows that, with a reused flexion command or reused joint torque, the error in measured interface effects tracks Delta_contact and not the other candidate invariants.

My intuitions while reading:

1. The framing is interesting and new to me. Interface studies do conflate interface and finger, and "what should a redesign preserve?" is a fair question for the RSS audience. Stating each contribution as a hypothesis with a failure condition is good practice and fits a Stage-1 registered-report-style venue.

2. The theory is correct as far as I can check, but it is elementary. Lemma 1 is the classical finite-displacement pole. Prop. 3 is the identity Delta c = (I - R_q) Delta p, with ||I - R_q|| = 2 sin(q/2). Cor. 1 follows in one line. Prop. 1 (mirror argument) and Prop. 2 are neat and practically useful, but they rest on strong symmetry assumptions (S1, S2, equal pads, joint-side ends at phalanx ends). Sec. IV-A checks them only on CFL designs ("within 0.05 mm median"). The sum rule (Eq. 7) is the most interesting statement: a thicker pad cannot keep both c and the skeleton path. The value of the theory is that it organizes the design space, not its mathematical depth. The wording "We prove ... a closed-form lower bound" in the abstract oversells it somewhat.

3. The main concern: Study 3 is close to circular. The cylinder is placed in the proximal-face frame at exactly the spot where O touches it with both pads at q0. The PIP is then commanded to the same flexion (after the transmission table), and the error is measured relative to O. Under these conditions, the distal face's displacement relative to O's at the grasp pose decides how much the cylinder is over- or under-squeezed (with a 5 N m/rad joint stiffness). Delta_contact is the maximum of that displacement over q. So Delta_contact predicting the error is close to built into the protocol. The paper itself reports that "the face's offset at the grasp" predicts it at rho = 0.92, better than Delta_contact. That points to a geometric over-closure effect, not a deeper invariance principle. The reused-torque protocol is the right control and partly answers this (rho = 0.75, CI [0.38, 0.93], 16 hands). But the effect is much weaker there (errors 3-40%), and the open diamonds in Fig. 5b show a modest trend driven by a few points.

4. The other candidate invariants are partly straw men, or are decided by how the sample was built.
   - Transmission is compensated by construction ("each hand's own transmission table"), so rho ≈ 0 is guaranteed.
   - Pad thickness and coverage take only three values, and the redesign set was built to span Delta_contact at each thickness ("redesigns at set Delta_contact").
   - Within a thickness, Eq. (7) forces Delta_skel and Delta_contact to trade off, so the sampling itself produces a negative Delta_skel correlation (-0.40, -0.51). "Skeleton does not predict" really means "skeleton anti-predicts because of the sum rule and how the redesigns were chosen". The partial correlation of 0.88 helps, but the sample of 14-16 hands is designed by the authors, not drawn from a population of plausible redesigns.

5. The motivation is partly undercut by the paper's own results. Reusing O's joint torque already cuts the error from 52-124% to 3-40%, and force matching leaves 3-7%. Joint torque (motor current) does not depend on the interface. So the dismissal "Both need sensing that the interface itself changes" applies to touch and force closure, not to torque reuse. A practitioner could reasonably conclude that the protocol, not the mechanism, is the cheaper fix. The paper should present the redesign principle as complementary to such protocols, and quantify what it adds on top of the best interface-independent protocol. Also, the claim that interface studies "often reuse the same commands across hands" has no citation.

6. Simulation fidelity. The interfaces are pads modelled through a rigid-body contact model, with "material stiffness" as a contact-stiffness parameter. Thick compliant pads and camera-based tactile modules differ mainly in bulk deformation, contact patch and damping. Whether a MuJoCo/Newton soft-contact model captures the interface effects being measured is not argued. The robustness check (contact stiffness x10, impedance ratio, substeps) tests numerical robustness, not physical validity.

7. Frame choice and MCP. The whole analysis lives in the proximal-face frame with the MCP held at 0. With a thicker proximal pad, the proximal face moves relative to the palm and MCP, and the paper admits the MCP column is not kept. Study 3 avoids this by placing the object relative to the proximal face. In a real multi-finger grasp the object's pose is set by the palm and the other fingers, so the frame in which "the same contact" is defined matters. This should at least be discussed.

8. Studies 1-2: the results are mostly consistent with the figures. Hypothesis C2 compares CFL to 2R-DD, but 2R-remote beats CFL at 90° and at 3-5 mm at 120° (Fig. 3). The text switches to "pad length over fixed L_f" to restore CFL's lead. That is legitimate if declared in advance, but it reads as metric-switching. "Near-best contact surface" in the abstract needs a precise referent (CFL's own Study-1 optimum, not the best across architectures). The 2R-DD result in Study 2 shows that a fixed axis with a deep reserve can keep c below the plotted line, so the actual argument is about a coverage-vs-Delta_contact trade-off. The front comparison ("giving up 2 points ... stay above the CFL") is the right evidence. Showing the full fronts would make it more convincing than one number.

9. Writing. The prose is short and declarative, which is good, but the paper is very dense. Many symbols (x0, G, L_vp, S1, S2, T_i, t_i, d_i, Delta d, Q_eval) arrive in a few paragraphs. The term "contact Jacobian" is used unusually: here it is the Jacobian of a body-fixed distal-face point in the proximal-face frame, not the usual contact Jacobian at an object contact. The title is overlong. The abstract sentence "the path of a single point sets it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" is hard to parse. Table I says "Bold" but does not explain the different hand counts (15/14/16). Fig. 1 quotes 3.9x and the text quotes 3.3x (single placement vs. median). That is fine but should be flagged. The figures themselves are clean and informative; Fig. 2 and Fig. 4(d) are particularly effective.

10. Stage-1 fit. The problem, hypotheses and methods are fleshed out, and the Stage-2 plan is concrete (O, R, U on one physical finger, tracking both faces). A negative Stage-2 result would still be informative, as the guidelines ask. What is missing is a stronger plan to break the near-circularity in item 3, for example placing the object in a palm-fixed frame, or using a protocol where Delta_contact is not mechanically tied to closure depth.

Overall, the idea is worth hearing and the paper is careful in many places. But the central empirical claim (C4) is close to tautological under the chosen protocol. The practical motivation is weakened by the torque-reuse result. The theory is elementary. I lean toward rejection but could be persuaded given the Stage-1 nature of the venue.
