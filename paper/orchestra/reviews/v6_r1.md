THOUGHT:

Main idea. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the paper argues that the quantity to keep fixed is the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal one (c(q) in the proximal-phalanx frame, called the PIP column of the "contact Jacobian"), not the skeleton/fingertip path. The paper has three parts. (i) Finite-pole kinematics: a clearance law (Eq. 4), a coverage law (Eq. 5), |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| (Eq. 6), the sum rule Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt| (Eq. 7), and a fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 8). (ii) Design searches (Studies 1–2) showing that a remote-driven crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more pad coverage and changes c(q) less than 2R-DD, 2R-remote and RCJ. (iii) A simulation (Study 3, Isaac Lab/MuJoCo-Warp): with one flexion command reused across 15 CFL redesigns, the error in a measured friction/stiffness/shape effect correlates with Δcontact (ρ = 0.83) but not with Δskel, coverage, thickness or transmission.

My intuitions.
- The framing is fresh and useful. Interface studies on robot hands are confounded by the redesign the interface forces. Asking which kinematic invariant a redesign must keep is a good question for RSS. I have not seen it posed this way.
- The kinematics are correct as far as I can check. Lemma 1 is the textbook finite pole. Eq. 6 follows from Δc = (I − R_q)Δp, whose singular values are both 2 sin(q/2). Eq. 7 is a triangle inequality between the pole offset from the face and the pole offset from the link line. These results are elementary, though. Most of the theoretical weight sits on the modelling assumptions, not on the math.
- Corollary 1 ("a fixed axis cannot") depends on the mount depth d being fixed by hardware. The corollary itself says the bound vanishes when the pad is recessed (Δd = Δh). The abstract states this caveat honestly. The conclusion ("A fixed axis cannot do this") drops it. The paper's own data also cut against the framing. 2R-DD, a fixed axis, falls below the plotted bound because its servo body already holds the face deep. At q_req = 90°, 9 mm, 2R-DD changes c by about 5.2 mm against CFL's 4.3 mm. So "10–51% of what a fixed axis must" compares the CFL with a bound, not with the best fixed-axis design. Against 2R-DD the margin at 90° is small.
- Study 2 has a metric artifact. Fig. 4(d) shows that 2R-DD and RCJ already have ||Δc(0)|| ≈ 5 mm and ≈ 9 mm at q = 0, "because their contact point moved along the face". For RCJ at 120°, 3→9 mm, the reported Δcontact (≈ 9.3 mm) is reached at q = 0. It is a bookkeeping offset of where the tracked point sits on the face, not a change in relative motion. Corollary 1 assumes c0 is kept, yet the comparators do not keep it. The cross-architecture ranking in Study 2 therefore partly reflects how the reference point is defined. A sensible fix is either to constrain c0 for all architectures or to report Δcontact after removing the q = 0 offset (or a sliding-invariant version).
- Study 3 is close to true by construction. The cylinder is placed where O touches both pads at q0. The command closes 5° past q0 through a joint of stiffness K0. Under that protocol, the deviation of c(q) from O's is exactly the extra or missing interference, so the grip force, and with it any limit-load difference, scales with Δcontact. The paper's mechanism paragraph says as much (U presses 3.3× harder). The result is a correct and quantified consequence of position-reuse protocols. It is not strong evidence that Δcontact is the right invariant in general.
- The skeleton baseline is partly a strawman, and Eq. 7 forces the outcome. A redesign that keeps the skeleton while the pad thickens must move the faces toward each other by Δt. Of course it pinches harder. The redesign set was built by the authors ("at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible"), so the negative ρ for Δskel comes from how the sample was drawn. The partial correlation (0.88) helps. Still, nobody would seriously propose keeping the skeleton of a finger whose pads thicken, so the comparison that matters is against protocol-level fixes.
- On that comparison, the protocol fixes do as well or better. Matching contact force leaves 3–7% error and closing from touch leaves 20%. R itself errs 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm. The rebuttal is that these protocols "need sensing that the interface itself changes". That holds for tactile sensing but not for motor-current or joint-torque based force closure, which is independent of the interface. Reused torque (3–40%) beats R at 9 mm. So the practical case for redesigning the kinematics, as opposed to changing the protocol, is not made. The paper should state when a kinematics-preserving redesign is needed: for example, frozen joint-space policies that cannot be retrained, or open-loop scripted benchmarks.
- The simulated "interfaces" are contact parameters (friction, contact stiffness, crowning) on rigid geometry. The motivating interfaces are thick compliant pads and camera-based tactile modules. Pad compliance through thickness is exactly what a 9 mm pad adds, and the sim does not model it. Thickness is geometric only.
- Statistics. n = 15 (14 for stiffness; why?), CIs bootstrapped "over hands", three hands dropped for failing to hold (ρ = 0.81 when counted as worst; reasonable). The n for shape (ρ = 0.94) is not given. Every Study 3 hand is a CFL, so the claim "only Δcontact predicts" is never tested across architectures.
- Other loose ends. MA_wc is said to be "reported" but no number appears anywhere. Transmission changes matter for force capability, and the reused-command protocol relies on a per-hand transmission table. Fig. 1 says R "grips at O's level" with 3.0 N against 4.5 N, a 33% gap. Fig. 1 gives 3.9× and the text 3.3× (one placement against the median). That is explained, but it reads as inconsistent. Study 1 switches to "pad length over fixed Lf" to claim a CFL lead over 2R-remote after losing on the declared objective at 90°. It reads as metric shopping. Why "CFL ideal" omits "the joint actuator's body" is unclear when the CFL is remote-driven.
- Terminology. "Contact Jacobian" normally means the map from joint rates to contact-point velocity (in a world or palm frame) at the actual contact. Here it is the PIP column of the velocity of a body-fixed point on the distal face, expressed in the proximal frame. Calling it the contact Jacobian is a stretch and may confuse robotics readers.
- Writing. Sentences are short, but the information density is very high. Notation arrives fast (M = (K, C, A, S), T_i, S1/S2, Fp, G, x0, z(q)), and several key definitions are packed into one-line clauses. The abstract's "the path of a single point sets it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" is hard to parse. The title is too long and has three clauses. Section III's opening jumps from the finger model to a feasibility definition and coverage with floats interleaved. The figures are clean and informative; Fig. 2 and Fig. 4(d) are particularly good.
- Venue fit (Stage 1). Problem, hypotheses and theory are developed in full. The Stage-2 plan is five lines. It gives no sample sizes, no face-tracking method, no definition of agreement or disagreement, and no pad set. The guidelines ask for the methodology to be fleshed out and for the insights expected from negative results. The paper offers only one sentence on the latter.

Decision. This is a creative, coherent paper with correct elementary theory and nice figures. Two of its main claims are weaker than stated. First, the "fixed axis cannot" claim rests on a mount-depth assumption, and the paper's own 2R-DD data fall below the bound. Second, "only contact-surface motion predicts the error" is close to built into the placement/position-reuse protocol, and the alternatives are baselines that cannot win by design. Study 2 also carries a c0-offset artifact. I lean reject, but this is borderline, and a version that fixes the Δc(0) artifact, positions the work honestly against protocol-level remedies, and expands the Stage-2 plan would be a solid RSS paper.
