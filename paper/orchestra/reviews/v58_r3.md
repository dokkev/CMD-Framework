ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and how well each is supported:

1. Theory claim (Sec. III-A). Contact-surface motion (Δ^p_d) and skeleton motion (Δ_skel) cannot both be kept once the contact surfaces deepen (Eq. 4), and a fixed axis cannot keep contact-surface motion (Eq. 5).
   - Eq. 1 (the fixed point of y ↦ e^{iq}y + b) is correct. So is Eq. 3, since Δc = (I − R_q)Δp gives a factor of 2 sin(q/2), provided both designs share c0. The triangle-inequality step to Eq. 4 is also sound.
   - Eq. 3 needs the same initial contact point, but Eq. 4 is stated without that condition. Yet Δ^p_d is defined to include q = 0 and to "count a moved initial contact point". As written, Eq. 4 is not established for redesigns that move c0.
   - The bound depends on Δt, the depth by which the surfaces actually deepen, not the pad thickness. CoDe frees the pivot heights precisely to make Δt smaller. Using Table II's numbers, CoDe has Δ^p_d + Δ_skel = 1.1 + 4.8 = 5.9 mm, below 2 sin 60° × 4 mm = 6.9 mm. So CoDe must deepen its surfaces by less than about 3.4 mm.
   - That makes the "impossibility" conditional on a quantity the design controls. A design that keeps Δt ≈ 0 (which Δ^1_d pushes toward) would face no bound at all. The paper should say this plainly rather than present Eq. 4 as a hard no-go.
   - Eq. 5 rests on a modelling assumption: the pin and lugs must sit above a full-width pad's mount. Fig. 3(c) also concedes that at-joint designs fall below the "fixed-axis bound" because they start from a different contact point. Since Δ^p_d counts q = 0, those designs really do achieve a smaller Δ^p_d than the bound. The claim "a fixed axis cannot keep that motion" is therefore weaker than stated.
   - Verdict: correct and elegant in its core, but over-claimed in its wording.

2. Design-space claim (Sec. III-D). The CFL covers more than an at-joint finger in all 10 jointly feasible conditions, and preserves contact motion best in all nine conditions.
   - Reasonably supported by an NSGA-II study over four architectures.
   - The comparison is in simulation under a single shared finger length and actuator. Optimizer budgets, seeds and convergence are not reported, so it cannot be judged whether each architecture's front is equally well resolved. The paper claims that the "whole front lies at smaller Δ^p_d", but Fig. 3(c) shows points, not fronts.

3. Empirical claim (Sec. IV). When O's impedance setpoint is reused, the error in a measured pad effect tracks Δ^p_d rather than Δ_skel.
   - This is the strongest part of the paper:
     - ρ = 0.94 over 27 redesigns, with partial ρ controlling for Δ_skel and for force deviation.
     - Holm-corrected permutation tests.
     - Two simulators and two contact models.
     - The vivid sign reversal in Fig. 1(d) (−41% on O, −39% on CoDe, +48% on Coverage).
   - Concern: the scope of the effect. In Table I, under the "other commands" (torque, touch, force, setpoint), Coverage's errors are small: 0%, −17%, −4% and +17%. Under a torque command Coverage is even closer to zero than CoDe (0% vs +8%). The large errors, +115% to +347%, appear only under the reused impedance setpoint.
   - The text says that under an equal reused torque, Δ^p_d still predicts the error (partial ρ = 0.80). If the absolute errors under torque are near zero for all hands, a high correlation over a narrow range carries little practical weight. This needs to be reconciled explicitly. As it stands, the headline phenomenon looks largely specific to position/impedance commands, and the paper should say so up front (the title and abstract read more generally).
   - Re-referencing the setpoint alone cuts Drake errors from 42% to 14%. That is a cheap software baseline which removes most of the problem without any hardware redesign.
   - e_H is normalized by |D_O|, so it is ill-conditioned when D_O is small. This plausibly explains values such as 388% and 254%, and makes E comparisons across tasks fragile.
   - The "ground truth" pad effect is defined on O carrying the new pad's contact stiffness on its own geometry, i.e., a pad that O physically could not carry. This is a defensible convention, but it is a convention, and it should be argued for.
   - "Pre-registered" appears twice, with no registry or timestamp that a reviewer could check.

4. Policy-transfer claim.
   - Weakly supported: two seeds, a single 7 mm redesign per class, and seed-dependent bars (cube rotation, Skeleton and Coverage seed 2).
   - Finger walk does not transfer to Drake even on O.
   - The result that Skeleton wins the fingertip-end dial is honestly reported. It shows the claim is "preserve what the task touches", which is more nuanced than the headline.

5. Hardware claim ("a modular finger for diverse pads").
   - The CoDe Hand appears only as CAD. There is no built prototype, no pad swap, and no physical measurement.
   - No pad deforms in either simulator. A thick compliant pad is modelled as softer point contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer, yet deformation-driven migration of the contact point is exactly what thick pads do.
   - For an RSS systems-and-design paper whose title promises the "design of a robot hand", the absence of any physical evidence is the main reason I lean toward rejection.

Overall: the conceptual question (which kinematic property to preserve when the interface changes) is original and well posed. The finite-displacement pole argument is a nice tool, and the simulated evidence is carefully controlled. However:
- the theory is over-stated relative to its conditions;
- the main effect appears to be scoped to impedance setpoints, with a cheap re-referencing baseline that already recovers much of it;
- pads do not deform;
- there is no hardware.

Clarity is hampered by dense, compressed prose and late-defined notation.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. The prose is extremely compressed. Many sentences chain three or four claims with colons and semicolons (e.g., the first contribution bullet; the paragraph introducing Eq. 9). Splitting them would help readers outside linkage kinematics.
2. Eq. 4 is introduced before Δ^p_d is formally defined (Eq. 7), and Δ^1_d appears only in Sec. III-C. Define all three deviation measures once, before the bounds.
3. State the shared-initial-contact-point condition explicitly in Eq. 4, as is already done for Eq. 5.
4. Sentence 2 of the abstract ("Such a hand has room for a contact interface only at the fingertip") is an over-generalization; LEAP-style hands do carry phalangeal pads. Soften it.
5. "CoDe is the design on the Pareto front of Eq. (9) that keeps both best at near-best coverage" is not an operational selection rule. Give the rule (e.g., a coverage tolerance plus a scalarization).
6. Table I: the column headers "convex", "compl. pad", "touch" and "setpoint" are not explained in the text or caption, and the rule for bold entries is unstated. Add a caption sentence.
7. Table I mixes simulators/contact models with "other commands" under one header row. Separate the command axis visually and say which simulator the "other commands" use.
8. Table II: say whether ρ is Spearman or Pearson and give p-values or CIs. "ρ with Δ^p_d / Δ_skel" is placed as a column group beside E, which is easy to misread.
9. Fig. 3(c) is described as showing "fronts", but it plots single points per architecture and thickness. Either plot the fronts or reword "its whole front lies at smaller Δ^p_d".
10. Fig. 3(c): the explanation of why at-joint points lie below the "fixed-axis bound" is in the caption only. Mention it in the text, since it qualifies the abstract's claim.
11. Fig. 1(c): the y-axis "change from O (mm)" is shared by two different quantities in different frames. Label each panel with its symbol (Δ^p_d(q), Δ_skel(q)).
12. Fig. 1(d) reports medians of loads with no spread. Add quartiles or the number of placements.
13. Fig. 4 plots trajectories minus O's, while the text discusses E. Say how the plotted deviations map to e_H, and why some panels (box pivot) have small absolute deviations yet a nonzero E.
14. Fig. 5: give the number of evaluation episodes, show variability (error bars), and explain "palm shift" in the caption, not only in the text.
15. "Contact Jacobian" is used for a positional path deviation. Either justify the term (equal paths imply equal Jacobians) or use "contact-surface motion" consistently.
16. "Pre-registered" should carry a footnote or reference to the registration.
17. Sec. IV-A "Measure" defines D_H, e_H and E in one dense paragraph. A short worked example (O vs CoDe on the friction change) would make Eq. 10 immediately readable.
18. "O against itself, under a tiny command change, errs at most 6%": specify what the command change is.
19. Sec. IV-B1 reports "14–45 redesigns each" and "27 grid redesigns" and "18 Drake redesigns". A small table or sentence listing each test's set and size would avoid confusion.
20. Fig. 2(a)'s header says "q ∈ [0, 120°]", but Sec. III-D also studies 90° and 150°. Note whether the pole regions change.
21. Minor: "Contact-centric Design" with the underlined acronym letters renders oddly. "Servodriven" and "contactsurface" are hyphenation artifacts in the text extraction; check the line breaks. Reference [12] is listed as ICLR 2026; confirm it is the final venue.
22. Minor: "−2.5" axis ticks and many small fonts in Figs. 3–5 are near the legibility limit at print size.
