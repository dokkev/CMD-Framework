THOUGHT:

My background is mechanism design and planar kinematics, so I checked Sec. III line by line before reading the studies.

Lemma 1. (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 [I + cot(q/2) R_{pi/2}] is correct: det(I - R_q) = 2(1 - cos q), and sin q / (1 - cos q) = cot(q/2). This is the textbook finite (displacement) pole, and the citation to Bottema and Roth is appropriate. "The finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is right.

Proposition 1 (Clearance). I re-derived it. Rotating by -q about P after the q = 0 mirror in x = 0 is a reflection in the line through P with direction (sin q/2, cos q/2), whose normal is (cos q/2, -sin q/2) as stated. A convex set and its mirror image are disjoint iff the set lies strictly on one side of the mirror line, because points of the set on the line are fixed by the reflection. The binding point is the deep joint-side corner (-a, -t). With the pole at depth z this gives a >= (t - z) tan(q/2), so G = 2a matches Eq. (4), including the [.]_+. The check on a rolling joint holds too. For two equal rollers I get the finite pole at (r, r tan(q/4)), so "z = r tan(q/4)" is correct. Using the finite pole here, not the instantaneous centre, is the right choice: the configuration at q is fixed by the finite displacement from q = 0, so the per-q test is exact. One caveat is S2 ("joint-side corners stay equidistant from the pole in x" for all q). A four-bar's finite pole moves in x as well as in z, so S2 cannot hold exactly for the CFL. The paper says Eq. (4) matches the CFL gap to within 0.05 mm (median), which is reassuring, but only a median is reported.

Proposition 2 (Coverage). The numerator follows from mirror symmetry. In the denominator, "the phalanx ends lie G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2) apart" is twice the distance from the proximal end to the mirror line. That holds only if the phalanx ends coincide in x with the pads' joint-side ends, i.e. L_vp = G. This assumption is not stated, and in general L_vp != G. The remark after the proposition ("lowering the pole raises C_pad until the pole reaches the face") is not a consequence of Eq. (5) alone. A larger z enlarges the denominator, which lowers C_pad, and shrinks G through Eq. (4), which raises it. The net sign depends on numbers. I checked it at q_req = 120 deg with plausible values and it is positive, but it is a heuristic, not a law. Eq. (5) also exposes a property of the objective: a palmar pole is penalised through the contour length L_f(q). The authors admit that the Study 1 ranking against 2R-remote flips when coverage is measured over the fixed L_f. So C2 depends on the normalisation chosen.

Proposition 3 (Contact-surface motion). c = (I - R)p + R c0, so with c0 kept, Delta c = (I - R) Delta p and ||Delta c|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Delta p||. Correct. For Eq. (7) I wrote both frames explicitly. F_p is the link frame translated by the proximal face depth, so Delta p_F = Delta P_L + (0, Delta t). The triangle inequality then gives Eq. (7), provided both c0 (in F_p) and r_C(0) (in the link frame) are kept and the proximal and distal faces change depth equally (S1). Correct, but the assumptions should be stated in the proposition and not only in the text. Eq. (7) is essentially a triangle inequality. It is useful mainly because it explains the anti-correlation between Delta_contact and Delta_skel in Study 3 (rho = -0.40 vs skeleton). It also means the "skeleton does not predict the error" finding is partly built in by geometry plus the design selection, because hands were chosen at set Delta_contact, some keeping the skeleton and some maximally changing it.

Corollary 1 (Fixed-axis bound). The argument is right: a fixed axis is its own finite pole, ||Delta p|| >= |Delta h - Delta d|, equality iff no shift along the face, and sin(q/2) is monotone for q_req <= 150 deg. But its content comes from the mount rule. If the axis-to-face distance is d + h with d fixed by the hardware, the face moves by Delta h relative to the pole, and the bound follows. The paper then states "Under our mount rule ... Delta d = 0 for a fixed axis". Its own Study 2 contradicts this. 2R-DD is a fixed axis, yet it "remounts its pads" and lies below the dotted "fixed-axis bound" in Fig. 4a-c (e.g. 90 deg, 9 mm: about 5.2 mm vs 8.49 mm). So the mount depth of a fixed axis is design-dependent when the hardware is placed by the optimiser. The bound then holds only for fixed hardware geometry (2R-remote, where it is met exactly). This matters because the abstract and C1 phrase it as "a fixed-axis joint cannot keep it" and "10-51% of what a fixed axis must". Fig. 4(d) also shows that 2R-DD and RCJ violate the "keep c0" premise (Delta c(0) != 0), so their Delta_contact values are not comparable to the corollary at all.

Terminology. "The pole" is used for two different points. The Jacobian column J(q) (Eq. 2) is set by the instantaneous centre: c' = R_{pi/2}(c - p_IC). Lemma 1, Prop. 1 and Prop. 3 use the finite pole. Both formulations are equivalent for "keep c(q) for all q" given equal c0, but the abstract's "a single point sets it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" mixes them. Calling a relative path c(q) "one column of the contact Jacobian" is also loose: the column is its derivative.

Studies. The Study 1 coverage numbers in the text match Fig. 3. I checked 2.4-14.6 points, the 8.5-9.6 point loss of CFL, and the 10.7 and 18.2 point losses of 2R-remote. "10-51% of the bound" matches Fig. 4 (120 deg/5 mm: 0.35/3.46; 90 deg/9 mm: 4.31/8.49). The CFL is relatively worst at 90 deg, where the fixed-axis bound is smallest. Search fairness is unclear. Architectures have different parameter counts, three seeds each, and only the CFL gets an extra "refinement from neighbouring designs". It is said to beat 2R-DD without refinement, but nothing is said for 2R-remote.

Study 3 is the most persuasive part. Its mechanism is close to tautological, though. Under a stiff position command, grip force is roughly K0 times the overclosure past first contact, and overclosure is a function of the face motion along the face normal near q0. That Delta_contact orders the error is therefore expected. More important for the paper's purpose:
(i) Even R, the best redesign, gives 21% and 41% error at 7 and 9 mm. That falls well short of "comparable interface studies", while force matching gives 3-7%.
(ii) Only CFL hands are simulated. No 2R-remote redesign sitting at the fixed-axis bound is tested, so the link "fixed axis -> must incur error" is never shown in simulation.
(iii) The spacer baseline is dismissed on coverage grounds. For attribution, though, a spacer is the textbook control: Delta_contact = 0, no redesign, and the thin pad's mechanics unchanged. It should be in Study 3, and the paper should say plainly that its contribution is achieving near-zero Delta_contact without paying the spacer's coverage cost.
(iv) Fifteen hands with a bootstrap CI of [0.41, 1.00], with several hands per thickness designed at set Delta_contact, is thin evidence for a Spearman claim. The shape result has n = 6.
(v) Pads are rigid geometry with a contact model, so the deformation of thick compliant pads, the reason such interfaces exist, is not represented.

On venue fit: the Stage-1 rules allow preliminary experiments, and the hypotheses, methodology and Stage-2 plan are laid out, with a sensible "either outcome is informative" paragraph. The theory is mostly correct. The framing (hold relative contact-surface motion fixed to de-confound interface studies) is original. Its significance rests on whether the confound matters in practice compared with simpler controls (spacers, fixed test rigs, command retargeting, force-matched protocols). The paper does not fully settle this. Clarity is hampered by dense notation, assumptions scattered outside the formal statements, and the dual meaning of "pole". I lean slightly toward reject but could be persuaded.
