THOUGHT:

My overall impression is that the paper makes an unusual and potentially useful methodological point. If you want to compare two contact interfaces, such as thin and thick pads, on hands that had to be redesigned to carry them, you should keep the relative motion of the contact faces fixed across the redesigns, not the skeleton. The paper puts this into planar finite-pole kinematics, gives a fixed-axis lower bound, shows that a crossed four-bar (CFL) can keep this motion while still covering nearly as much of the object, and runs a simulation in which the "contact-surface motion change" Δ_contact tracks how far a reused command distorts a measured interface effect. Each contribution is stated as a hypothesis with a way to fail. That suits the RSS Stage-1 format well and is better than most submissions manage. My concerns fall into four groups.

(1) How much of the theory is new. Lemma 1 is the textbook finite pole. Eq. (6) is just ||(I − R_q)Δp|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||. Corollary 1 then follows in one line: a fixed axis is its own pole, so moving the face by Δh moves the pole by Δh in the face frame. Props. 1 and 2 (the mirror-line clearance and coverage) are neat, but they assume symmetric pads (S1, S2) that the optimized designs do not satisfy. The paper also never says which classical body of work it builds on. Prescribing the relative motion of two bodies through a four-bar is Burmester motion generation and centrode synthesis, and polycentric prosthetic knees use exactly the argument that "the pole can be virtual and lie outside the physical pivots". Neither is cited. What is new is the framing, the pad-coverage analysis, and the claim that this quantity is what an interface study should hold fixed. The paper should say so plainly, not present C1 as "a bound".

(2) Circularity in Study 3. Δ_contact is defined in the proximal-face frame. The cylinder is placed where O touches it, relative to the proximal face ("an object resting on a pad can only be placed relative to that pad's face"). The error compares contact on that same face. So the experiment is built in the same frame that defines Δ_contact. Δ_skel is measured from the link line, so a thicker pad shifts it by Δt by construction, and it starts at a disadvantage. The grid check helps, but the text does not say which frame the grid is fixed in. If the grid is also face-relative, it does not remove this concern. I would like to see the skeleton candidate also defined relative to the face, i.e. the fingertip path written in F_p. If the claim is that the joint type matters beyond a simple offset of the reference frame, that comparison is the fair one.

The redesign set has a second problem. Its members were built "at set Δ_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". This makes Δ_skel anti-correlated with Δ_contact (ρ_skel = −0.40 / −0.51 in the main protocol). The negative skeleton correlation is an artefact of the chosen set, not evidence against the skeleton, as the authors partly admit when the grid makes it 0.18.

(3) The statistics behind "only Δ_contact predicts". With n = 14–18 non-independent hands, "the 95% CI of every other candidate contains zero" is a weak test. Under the reused-torque protocol, coverage (−0.52) and thickness (0.50) are not far from Δ_contact (0.75). The paper needs a test of whether the correlations differ, e.g. a paired bootstrap of ρ_contact − ρ_other, or a multivariate or partial analysis over all candidates. A partial correlation given Δ_skel alone is not enough. Thickness takes only three values, so its Spearman ρ is limited by ties. The shape result (ρ = 0.94) rests on six hands. The abstract's "ρ = 0.75–0.94" combines these unequal sample sizes. Hands that cannot hold the cylinder are left out, and they are exactly the redesigns whose distal face retreats, which is a selection effect. The error is a ratio to |Δ(O)|, which may be small for some placements. Medians help, but the authors should report Δ(O).

(4) Is the problem real, and how big is the practical gain? No cited interface study reuses commands across redesigned hands. The motivating scenario is asserted, not documented. The fix the paper argues for gives only a modest gain. R still errs 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm under the reused command. Under the reused torque, R errs about half as much as U, but both are in the 5–28% range. Matching the contact force leaves 3–7% on any hand. The authors dismiss force matching because it "needs sensing that the interface itself changes". But a fingertip F/T sensor, or an external force measurement in a benchmark setup, does not depend on the pad. So the practical case for redesigning the joint over changing the protocol is not established. The sim also models pads as a rigid geometry plus contact stiffness. The thing being redesigned for (thick compliant pads) therefore has no compliance of its own, only geometry. In reality the "true" interface effect on a 9 mm pad would differ from that on a 3 mm pad, so using O as ground truth needs justification.

Other technical points:
- Study 1 does not support C2 as stated. 2R-remote beats CFL at q_req = 90° for every thickness, and at 120° for 3–5 mm. The claim holds only against 2R-DD, plus a switch to a second metric, "pad length over fixed L_f", which reads as picking the metric that wins. CFL also gets an extra refinement step the other designs do not get. The authors say the 2R-DD result holds without it, but the 2R-remote comparison should be checked the same way.
- Fig. 4. The dotted "fixed-axis bound" is not a bound for 2R-DD, which falls below it because Δd ≠ 0, or for RCJ, whose radius changes. So "10–51% of the bound" is measured against a reference line, not a limit the competitors cannot pass. In Fig. 4(d), 2R-DD and RCJ start with Δc(0) ≠ 0 ("their contact point moved along the face"). Δ_contact as a max over q then counts a start-point offset that the redesign optimizer could have removed by placement. Is the comparison fair, given that the corollary assumes c0 is kept?
- The paper says the 51 designs match the evaluator "within 1e-13". Which formula was used? Eq. (5) needs S1 and S2, which general designs break. That check only confirms the code, and the more informative check is Eq. (4) against the real gap (0.05 mm median). How large is the worst case?
- "Coverage is the objective of every design search", but Study 2 is bi-objective. This is minor but inconsistent.
- The claim that a fixed axis cannot keep c because "a physical pin must sit … above the pad's mounting surface" deserves more thought. Pockets in the pad around the pin, a split pad, or a curved-slot or virtual-pivot revolute joint could recess the face locally. Why are these excluded?

On clarity, the writing is terse almost to excess. Many sentences pack one idea densely, and key quantities are introduced in passing: x0, G, S1/S2, r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, M = (K, C, A, S) (barely used again), and "degenerate 2R-remote designs (0.21, 0)". Several points are confusing:
- "the CoDe hand model's three CFL fingers" against 18 redesigns.
- What R and U mean in Study 3, given that R is not simply the Study 2 design.
- The protocol of placing the cylinder at q0 and closing to q0 + 5°.
- What exactly the grid is.

The title is long and three-part. The "contact Jacobian" wording inflates what is a single-DoF relative rigid motion. The claim that keeping J is equivalent to keeping c is trivial and could be one clause. The Stage-2 plan is three lines. It lacks pass/fail criteria for C5, any account of how Δ_contact will be measured on hardware or with what accuracy, and the number of pads, objects and trials. The venue asks for methodology to be fully fleshed out, including insights from negative results, so this section is under-specified. The figures are good. Fig. 1 makes the point quickly, and Fig. 2 makes the clearance law intuitive.

Overall, the idea is interesting and the authors have clearly worked hard to test their invariant against alternatives. But the main empirical claim leans on an experiment framed in the invariant's own frame, a small and purpose-built hand set, and weak comparisons between correlations. The practical motivation is also not documented. I lean toward rejection at the Stage-1 bar, but I could be persuaded if the circularity and the statistical comparison are addressed.
