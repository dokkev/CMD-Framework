THOUGHT:

The paper asks what a redesigned finger should preserve so that two contact interfaces (e.g., 3 mm vs 9 mm pads) can be compared fairly when the thicker interface forces a redesign. Its answer is the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal contact face, c(q), expressed in the proximal-phalanx frame, rather than the skeleton (fingertip) path. Section III supplies the kinematics. The finite pole (Lemma 1) leads to a symmetric clearance law (Prop. 1), a coverage law (Prop. 2) and ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Δp|| (Prop. 3), which gives a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1) and a trade-off between contact and skeleton motion (Eq. 7). Sections IV–V contain three studies: a coverage-optimization comparison of four PIP joint types (Study 1), a redesign that minimizes Δcontact (Study 2), and a simulation of reused commands/torques for three interface changes (Study 3).

My intuitions while reading:

1. The theory is correct and cleanly stated but classical. Prop. 3 is (I − R_q)Δp, and |I − R_q| = 2 sin(q/2). Lemma 1 is textbook finite-displacement kinematics (correctly cited to Bottema & Roth). Cor. 1 follows directly once one accepts the modelling assumption that a physical pin must sit above the pad mount surface and that the mount depth is set by joint hardware. The value is in the framing (pole relative to the contact face, not the link line) and in turning it into a falsifiable design criterion, not in new mathematics. Classical motion generation (Burmester precision-position synthesis) and the polycentric four-bar knee/finger literature, which designs exactly where the instant/finite centre lies, are the obvious precedents. Neither is discussed, and a GA is used where a synthesis formulation might give more insight.

2. Study 1 and 2 are internally consistent with the figures. Fig. 3 shows CFL above 2R-DD everywhere, but 2R-remote above CFL at 90° and at thin pads at 120°. Choosing 2R-DD as the comparator in C2 is defensible (servo-at-joint is the status quo), but the strongest competitor in coverage is 2R-remote. The paper itself notes that the "cylinder" coverage measure normalizes by a design-dependent contour length L_f(q), which penalizes CFL's palmar pole. Because the objective's normalization changes with the design, cross-architecture coverage comparisons partly reflect the metric. The text explains this, but the main claim is still stated on that metric. Optimization effort is asymmetric: CFL gets an extra neighbourhood refinement. The authors say the 2R-DD conclusion holds without it, which is good, and there are three seeds per condition.

3. Fig. 4(d) is revealing. 2R-DD and RCJ "start apart" by 5–9 mm at q = 0 because their contact point moved along the face. So their Δcontact in Fig. 4(a–c) is largely an offset at extension, not a difference in pole motion. That offset could in principle be absorbed by re-placing the object or re-indexing the reference point. Δcontact as defined (max over q of ||c − c0||, no alignment) therefore mixes "where on the face" with "how the faces move". The stated premise of Cor. 1 ("keep c0") is not enforced for those architectures. This weakens the claim that CFL is uniquely good at keeping the motion. A shape-aligned or offset-removed variant of Δcontact should be reported alongside.

4. Study 3 is the crux (C4), and where I have the most concerns.
   (a) Near-circularity in the main setting. Objects are placed where O's contact faces meet the cylinder, and Δcontact measures how far R's faces depart from O's in exactly that frame. By construction, a hand with Δcontact ≈ 0 reproduces O's contact geometry. The hand-independent grid is the right control, and the effect survives there (ρ = 0.60, partial 0.58). But in that setting Δskel also correlates positively (ρ = 0.46), and pad loss drives a ρ = 0.86 relation. The "only consistent predictor" conclusion then rests partly on Δskel changing sign across settings. A negative Δskel–error correlation in the O-placement setting is expected from Eq. (7): the redesigns trade one change against the other, so it is not independent evidence against the skeleton.
   (b) The mechanism is mostly over-closure. With the reused position command, U presses 3.3–3.9x harder than O. With O's torque reused, forces match within 0.89–1.26x, and errors drop to 3–40% with much smaller R-vs-U gaps (5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%). With force matching or closing from touch, the error is 3–7% or 20%. A careful experimenter would not reuse a position command across hands with different pads. So the practical significance of "keep c(q)" depends on a protocol the paper itself shows to be the worst one. The argument that touch/force sensing is unavailable because "a sensorized interface alters it" is not convincing: motor-current or joint-torque sensing is independent of the pad.
   (c) Even the recommended design errs substantially. R errs 21% (7 mm) and 41% (9 mm) under the reused command, with Δcontact ≈ 0.9 mm at 7 mm. The sensitivity is roughly 20–25% error per mm of Δcontact, so only the 5 mm redesign is near "comparable". The paper does not discuss what Δcontact a study needs for a given tolerated error, and that seems to me the actionable output.
   (d) Statistics: 14–16 hands, chosen by design (not sampled), a bootstrap over hands, 5 candidates × 4 settings with no correction for multiple comparisons, and the shape result on six hands only. The pre-registration statement ("thresholds fixed before the runs") is welcome.
   (e) Physical modelling: the thick compliant pads the paper is about appear in simulation only as geometry plus a contact stiffness. Pad deformation, which changes where contact occurs and how load spreads, is exactly what thick pads do. All Study 3 redesigns are CFL, so C4 is tested within one architecture.

5. Scope: planar, PIP only, MCP locked, cylinders, multi-phalanx enveloping grasps. The motivating interfaces (GelSight, DIGIT) are mostly used in fingertip/pinch contact, where the paper admits the principle does not apply. That gap between motivation and scope should be addressed head-on.

6. Writing. The paper is disciplined: hypotheses carry explicit failure conditions, which fits the Stage-1 call well, and the figures are clear (Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 are particularly effective). But the text is extremely compressed and acronym-dense (CFL, RCJ, 2R-DD, 2R-remote, O/R/U, C1–C5, Δcontact/Δskel, F_p, T_i, S1/S2). Several key sentences carry too much at once (e.g., the abstract's "by 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound, at near-best contact surface"; "R errs less than U at 20–24 of the 22–24 paired placements under..."). The title is long and self-contradictory ("Same Hand" for a paper about redesigns). The Prop. 2 discussion ("A palmar pole lengthens the contour ... Lowering the pole raises C_pad") reads as contradictory, because a longer contour in the denominator of Eq. (5) lowers C_pad. The net effect through G needs one more sentence. Calling dc/dq "the PIP column of the contact Jacobian" is non-standard: it is a relative body-frame point Jacobian of one face point. The equivalence "kept J ⇔ kept c" is trivial and takes space. The related work omits polycentric-joint/ICR design (prosthetic knees, anthropomorphic finger four-bars) and classical motion synthesis, both directly tied to the novelty claim.

Desired outcome: a Stage-1 paper with a clean, well-posed idea and honest preliminary evidence, but the central practical claim (C4) is weaker than presented. Three things would move me: (i) an offset-aligned Δcontact; (ii) error-vs-Δcontact under the force-matched protocol, with a "required Δcontact for X% error" statement; (iii) at least a deformable-pad sanity check. I lean slightly toward rejection but could be persuaded given the Stage-1 standard.
