THOUGHT:

Note-taking.

What the paper claims. When a contact interface (thick pad, tactile module) is swapped on a servo-driven finger, the finger has to be redesigned, and the redesign confounds the comparison. The authors argue that the redesign should keep the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal contact face (c(q), the PIP column of a "contact Jacobian" in the proximal-face frame), not the skeleton. Section III gives finite-pole laws: a clearance gap G_sym = max 2[t - z]^+ tan(q/2) (Prop. 1), a closed-form coverage (Prop. 2), |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| (Prop. 3), and a fixed-axis lower bound Δ_contact >= 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δh - Δd| (Cor. 1). Study 1 optimizes coverage for four PIP joint types. Study 2 re-optimizes for thicker pads and shows the crossed four-bar (CFL) stays at 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound. Study 3 (Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp) reuses a flexion command or joint torque across 15-45 redesigns and shows that the error in a measured interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) rank-correlates with Δ_contact (ρ = 0.60-0.94). Skeleton, coverage, thickness and transmission do not correlate consistently. Stage 2 promises a physical O/R/U comparison with a pre-stated failure criterion.

Checks on the math. I verified the key identities:
- (I - R_q)^{-1} = ½(I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}), so Lemma 1 is right (the extracted text is garbled).
- Fig. 2 numbers: 5 tan 60° = 8.66, 9 tan 60° = 15.6, 4 tan 60° = 6.93.
- The gap slope of 2 tan 60° = 3.46 mm/mm.
- The claim that lowering the pole by δ shrinks G by 2δ tan(q/2) and lengthens the denominator "by the same amount": tan(1 - cos) + sin = tan, so it holds. The "best pole is on the face" argument follows, under S1/S2 and only at q_req, while C̄_pad averages over three poses.
- Cor. 1 follows from Prop. 3 because a fixed axis has a constant pole and sin(q/2) is monotone on [0, π].
- Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality once both start points are kept.
- Spot-checking the percentages against Fig. 4: 0.35/3.46 = 10% at 120°, 5 mm; 4.31/8.49 = 51% at 90°, 9 mm.
The analysis is internally consistent and pleasantly compact. The statement "the finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" needs "for all q". The step "and conversely" after the integral inequality is not a converse of the inequality. It is a trivial differentiation.

Where I am skeptical.

(1) Is the Study 3 result close to true by construction? The object sits on the proximal pad with the MCP locked, and φ = q on every design. The command is mapped through each hand's transmission table. So the only thing that can change the grasp geometry at a given q is c(q), i.e. where the distal face is relative to the proximal face. Transmission is removed from the comparison by construction, so its null correlation is uninformative. Pad thickness and coverage are only weakly linked to grasp geometry. The one serious competitor is Δ_skel, and on the hand-independent grid Δ_skel correlates positively (0.46), for a reason the authors attribute to placements lost. Their post-hoc analysis on 26 redesigns rescues it. The interesting finding is the magnitude, not the ranking: a 1-4 mm change in face motion distorts the measured friction effect by 50-150%. That magnitude depends on the protocol (stiff position command at K0 = 5 N m/rad) and on the rigid-ish soft-contact model.

(2) The paper's own data point to a cheaper fix. Under reused torque the error drops to 3-40%. With contact-sensing protocols it drops to 3-7% (force-matched) or 20% (own touch). So a reader may conclude that interface studies should change their protocol rather than their finger kinematics. The paper names these protocols in one sentence and moves on. This is the most important counter-argument to the motivation, and it is not addressed.

(3) Spacers. Building the finger for the thickest pad and spacing thinner pads keeps Δ_contact = 0 exactly. The cost is 8.6-18.2 points of coverage. Study 3 finds that coverage does not predict the error (ρ ≈ -0.2). By the paper's own evidence, then, spacers are the best way to obtain comparable interface studies whenever the interface set is known. The argument "a new interface forces a redesign" is reasonable, but it reduces the scope to sequential, unforeseen interface additions, and that should be said in the motivation.

(4) C2 is framed against 2R-DD only, but 2R-remote beats CFL in coverage at 90° and at 120° below ~6 mm. The headline "the CFL keeps more contact surface" therefore holds only against the servo-at-joint baseline. The paper defends this with "2R-remote pays in C3", which is fair but should be in the claim. The abstract's "less than three other joint types in nearly every condition" and the body's "least among the physical joints in every cell" (with 7 of 9 when the start offset is removed) need to be reconciled.

(5) Statistics. n = 14-16 hands for the main correlations and six for shape. Redesigns are deliberately constructed (set Δ_contact with skeleton kept or changed), so they are not a sample from any natural design distribution. Δ_contact grows with thickness by construction. "It holds at each thickness" rests on about five hands per thickness. Survivorship: the error is computed only over hands that hold the cylinder, and the excluded hands are those whose distal face retreats, i.e. high-Δ_contact ones. In Fig. 1d / 5a the relation is far from a function: a 9 mm redesign at Δ ≈ 3.5 mm errs ~40% while a 5 mm one at Δ ≈ 3.1 errs ~103%. At 9 mm, one non-R redesign appears to err as little as R (~40% vs 41%), which strains "R errs least at each thickness". Holm correction and pre-fixed thresholds are commendable.

(6) The "reused torque" protocol assumes joint-torque control. A real remote-actuated CFL produces servo torque, and its transmission changes with the redesign. So on hardware the torque protocol would re-introduce exactly the transmission change that the command protocol undoes with a table. Mechanical advantage is explicitly unconstrained, so CFL redesigns for thick pads may have poor force transmission.

(7) Simulation realism. All pads share one contact model regardless of thickness (intentional, to isolate geometry, and acknowledged for Stage 2). Limit loads in MuJoCo soft contact are notoriously sensitive to solver parameters. The robustness check (tenfold stiffness, impedance ratios, substeps) is reported only as R-vs-U wins, not as the correlation.

(8) Scope. The analysis covers a planar PIP with the MCP fixed, and multi-phalanx grasps only. The MCP column, whose lever includes the pad depth, is explicitly not kept. In any real grasp with a moving MCP the proximal face's motion relative to the palm changes with thickness too, so the principle covers only part of the hand's contact geometry.

Writing. The prose is terse, often to the point of opacity. A large symbol set is introduced in a few paragraphs: O, R, U, CFL, CFL ideal, 2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, S1, S2, Q_eval, T_i, G, x0, t_i, F_p, Δ_contact, Δ_skel. Fig. 2 appears before the model is defined. There are forward references ("Spacers are excluded (Sec. IV-C)"). The title is long and its "Same Hand, Different Contact" tagline reads opposite to the message (the hand is redesigned; the contact motion is kept). Table I's column structure (Reused command: fric./stiff./grid fric.; Torque: fric.; plus a "Hands" row and an "O's contacts" label) is hard to parse, at least in this text extraction. Stating each contribution as a hypothesis with a failure condition is a good practice that suits the Stage-1 format. The Stage 2 protocol is concrete, with a falsifiable criterion (R vs U on identical pads). Related work is short, as the venue requests, but it misses the closest neighbours:
- linkage-driven / underactuated finger design that analyses phalanx contact forces (e.g. Birglen, Laliberté and Gosselin)
- grasp analysis with contact Jacobians / grasp matrices (e.g. Prattichizzo and Trinkle)
- prior work on comparing tactile sensors on a common platform.

Venue fit. For an RSS Stage-1 extended abstract, the problem, hypotheses and methods are fully stated and the analysis is checkable. The simulation study is more than preliminary, and the Stage-2 plan is explicit and falsifiable. The weaknesses are mostly about significance and framing, not mathematical errors: the protocol alternative, spacers, and the near-tautological candidate set. All are addressable in Stage 2. I lean to a borderline accept.
