ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Central claims. (C1) A finger with remote PIP actuation and a crossed four-bar (CFL) keeps more pad coverage C̄_pad than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. (C2) For an interface effect to be measured independently of the redesign that carries the pad, the redesign should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), the distal-face path in the proximal-face frame (called the PIP column of the contact Jacobian), not the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot keep it at a fixed mount depth (Cor. 1 lower bound), the CFL comes in at 10–51% of that bound, and in simulation the error of a reused impedance setpoint follows Δ_contact (ρ ≈ 0.80–0.86).

I re-derived the mathematics in Sec. III.
- Lemma 1: (I − R_q)^{-1} = ½(I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}) is correct. The RCJ finite pole z = r tan(q/4) is correct for two equal rollers whose contact point lies on the link line.
- Prop. 1: the reflection argument is sound. Rotation about P is the composition of the q=0 mirror and a mirror tilted by q/2. A convex set and its mirror image are disjoint iff the set misses the line, because a point of the set on the line is fixed by the reflection. The numbers in Fig. 2 check out: 5 tan 60° = 8.7, 9 tan 60° = 15.6, 4 tan 60° = 6.9, and 2 tan 60° = 3.46 mm per mm.
- Prop. 2: G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2) is twice the distance from a link-line phalanx end to the mirror line, so Eq. (3) follows once L_vp = G.
- Pole-lowering: at q_req, dD/dz = 2 tan(Q)(1 − cos Q) + 2 sin Q = 2 tan Q with Q = q_req/2, which equals the numerator change, as claimed. At the smaller evaluation poses the denominator grows by less, so C̄_pad rises even faster. Past the face, [·]_+ = 0 and C_pad falls, so the optimum is the pole on the face. The argument is correct.
- Eqs. (6)–(7): Δc = (I − R_q)Δp + R_qΔc_0, and |(I − R_q)v| = 2 sin(q/2)|v|. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality on the pole expressed in the face frame and in the link-line frame. Both are correct.

So the stated results hold under their stated hypotheses. Props 1–2 are exact only under S1/S2 (the pole moves in depth alone). The authors say this and quantify the error (Eq. 2 is within 0.05 mm median, 1.6 mm max), so the propositions explain the trends rather than certify the designs. That is appropriate.

Main technical point: Cor. 1 assumes c_0 is kept, but Δ_contact (Eq. 5) penalises a c_0 shift only through its value at q = 0. The Fig. 4 caption concedes that 2R-DD and RCJ "fall below" the bound because their contact point slid along the face. The relevant comparison class for "a fixed axis cannot keep it; the CFL cuts it to a fraction" is therefore a fixed axis that may also slide c_0 along the face. I minimised max_q |(I − R_q)Δp + R_q(α,0)| over the axis position along the face and the along-face c_0 shift α. The achievable fixed-axis floor is 0.59, 0.67 and 0.79 of Eq. (8) at 90°, 120° and 150°. It is ≥ 0.5 in general, from the max of the q = 0 and q = q_req terms. The CFL's worst cell (51%) still lies below this floor, so H2 survives, but the corollary and the "10–51% of the bound" framing should state the c_0-free bound. Otherwise the reader cannot tell whether the CFL's advantage is a pole effect or a c_0 effect. The caption's "7 of 9 cells without that offset" hints at the latter for some cells.

Second point: naming versus metric. Keeping c(q) exactly keeps the Jacobian column. But Δ_contact is a C^0 path distance, and the mechanism the paper identifies is a zeroth-order position effect: the face's signed normal offset at the grasped pose sets over-closure at a reused setpoint, giving post hoc ρ = −0.99 for the direction. The first-order quantity, the column itself (lever |c − p̂|), is never isolated as a candidate. "Transmission" is the servo-to-flexion map, not the column. The title's "keep the contact Jacobian" is therefore supported only in the limiting case. The quantitative evidence is for "keep the contact-surface path". This is a framing issue, not an error, but it is the paper's headline.

Do the design studies test what the propositions say?
- Study 2 is the direct test of Prop. 3 and Cor. 1. 2R-remote meeting the bound exactly at 90° and 120° is a nice validation of the theory. The 150° cell is degenerate.
- Study 1 tests H1, a comparative optimisation claim that Props 1–2 only motivate. H1's falsifier is clear. The CFL gets an extra neighbourhood refinement, but the authors report that the worst of its six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10 cells, which addresses the budget concern. One trend is not explained by the pole analysis: 2R-remote (z ≡ 0) beats the CFL at thin pads at 90° and 120°. If the optimal pole lies on the face, the CFL's deficit at 3 mm must come from four-bar hardware (x_0, lugs, transmission-angle constraint). The paper should say so, because it is part of the C1 ablation.
- Study 3 (H3) is not a test of the propositions. It is a mechanics test of whether the kinematic quantity predicts measurement error. The design is good: redesigns placed at set Δ_contact that keep or change the skeleton most decorrelate the two quantities, which Eq. (7) otherwise couples. It has pre-registered tests, partial correlations, within-thickness resampling and Holm correction. The scope limits are real, though. There is one contact model (point contact on meshes, with the pad reduced to a normal stiffness), one cylinder task, the MCP held, and n = 14–15 hands in two of the three reused-setpoint settings. Under the touch-closing protocol the skeleton predicts better (0.88 vs 0.44). C2's "keeping the skeleton does not suffice" is therefore protocol-specific, which the paper states but the abstract compresses.

Stage 1 fit: the problem, H1–H3 with falsifiers, and a Stage 2 physical protocol with a pre-stated Page trend test are all present. This exceeds the Stage 1 bar. Presentation is very dense: undefined symbols (r_{1,e}, r_{2,s}, b, x_0), an overloaded Table I caption, and Fig. 2 text unreadable at print size. The scores rest on correct and useful kinematics, a well-designed simulation test of C2, and the bound and naming issues above, which are fixable.
