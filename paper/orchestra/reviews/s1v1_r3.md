ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper as I read it (all 6 pages, figures and tables included): when a servo-driven finger is redesigned for a thicker contact interface, the paper argues the redesign should keep the motion of the contact surfaces (the distal pad's path seen from the proximal pad, Δ_distal^prox), not the skeleton (joint axes and fingertip path, Δ_skel). The argument has three parts:
- A finite-displacement pole analysis (Eqs. 1–4): a pole near the contact faces saves gap, and a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion when the surfaces get deeper.
- A crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP, the "CoDe Hand", which places the pole by design.
- A simulation study. The original hand's impedance setpoint is reused on 5/7/9 mm redesigns, and the "effect error" is how far each redesign's measured interface effect departs from the original hand's.

Hypotheses and evidence:
- H1: effect error grows with Δ_distal^prox, not with Δ_skel.
  - Evidence: Isaac Lab ρ = 0.94 over 27 pre-registered grid redesigns; partial ρ "up to 0.91" with the skeleton held fixed; four Drake tasks under point and hydroelastic contact (Table II: ρ = 0.93/0.91 for hold/acquire, but only 0.53/0.43 for dial/pivot); Table I.
  - This is the best-supported claim.
  - My concern is about value, not feasibility. Under a joint-space impedance setpoint with the object placed relative to the proximal pad (Drake), the contact force depends on the flexion at which the distal pad meets the object, so the result follows almost directly from the geometry. The skeleton never touches the object.
  - "Keep the skeleton" is presented as "the natural answer", but no evidence (citations, practice) shows anyone holds this view. It risks being a straw man.
  - Sec. IV.B.2 concedes that the relevant frame changes with what holds the object (proximal pad vs. palm). That makes the "right" quantity depend on the fixture rather than be a single principle.
- H2: the effect is not just a preload change. Evidence: partial ρ = 0.80 with the force difference held fixed under an equal torque; re-referencing the setpoint cuts the Drake error from 42% to 14%, and the remainder still follows Δ_distal^prox. This is reasonable support, but thin, and it is stated in only a few sentences.
- H3: a policy learned on O keeps its performance on the redesign that keeps the motion its task uses.
  - Evidence: 3 PPO tasks, 3 redesigns, 2 seeds. In Isaac Lab CoDe keeps only about 55–75%, and Skeleton wins on relay dial.
  - The governing "motion its task uses" is identified after the fact:
    - pad-relative motion for finger walk;
    - fingertip path for relay dial ("outside our claim");
    - palm-relative proximal-pad position for cube rotation (ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns).
  - As stated, H3 is close to unfalsifiable.
  - It is also in tension with the Box-pivot Drake task. That task is a fingertip task, yet there CoDe beats Skeleton, which keeps the fingertip within 0.3 mm.
  - The abstract's "policies ... carry over" overstates the result.

Scope confounds:
- No interface deforms. "Compliant" is a contact-stiffness parameter.
- The main interface effect is a friction change (1.3 → 2.0), not the tactile or compliant interfaces that motivate the work.
- The planar finger has the MCP fixed and only the PIP redesigned.
- The "ground truth" O carries the new interface's contact properties on a 3 mm geometry, which is a counterfactual hand.
- The CoDe Hand exists only as CAD, even though the title promises the design of a hand.
- The coverage advantage of the CFL is modest (2.4–14.6 pp), and moving the servo alone (Remote) matches it at 90° and up to 5 mm at 120°.

Scores:
- Value 2: the question matters for cross-embodiment reuse and interface benchmarking, but the core finding is close to a geometric consequence for an impedance setpoint, and the hypotheses are narrowly scoped.
- Feasibility 2: H1 is well supported in simulation, but its transfer to deforming real interfaces, the post-hoc H3, and the fixture dependence all remain open. Against competing submissions that bring hardware and many tasks, the evidence is limited.
