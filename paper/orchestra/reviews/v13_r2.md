THOUGHT:
I read this as someone who runs contact/interface experiments and cares about what a measured "interface effect" actually isolates. The paper makes two claims. C1: a servo-driven finger with a remote PIP actuator and a crossed four-bar (CFL) leaves more room for thick proximal/distal pads than a servo-at-joint finger (2R-DD). C2: when a finger is redesigned for a new pad, the quantity to hold fixed is the contact-surface motion c(q) in the proximal-phalanx frame (the paper calls this "the contact Jacobian"), not the skeleton (fingertip path). A fixed axis cannot hold it (closed-form lower bound, Cor. 1), and in simulation the error in a measured interface effect follows Δcontact rather than Δskel. Hardware is deferred to Stage 2, which the venue allows.

What I like. The problem is real and under-discussed. Interface papers routinely compare pads or skins mounted on different fingers, and "same protocol, different hand" is a confound most authors ignore. The pole-based treatment is clean. Lemma 1 is textbook, but Prop. 1–3 and Cor. 1 give a compact argument for why a fixed axis loses room (gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm) and why it cannot keep c(q) without recessing the face. Eq. (6) is nice: Δc = (I − R_q)Δp does not depend on which distal-face point is chosen, so the "wherever the object touches" claim holds. The hypotheses come with explicit failure conditions. Study 3 reports Holm-corrected one-sided permutation tests, hand-bootstrap intervals, thresholds "fixed before the runs", a hand-independent grid, a post hoc check against placement loss, and solver robustness checks. That is more statistical care than most simulation papers in this area show. The Stage-2 protocol has a falsification criterion and handles one real confound: 3 mm and 7 mm real pads differ in compliance, so the test is R vs U.

Where I am not convinced, ordered by weight.

(1) The Study-3 sample is designed, not sampled, and the design decides the candidate competition. The 18 redesigns are R, U, "redesigns at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible", and a contrast family built with Δskel = 0 and Δcontact = √3Δh. The paper then ranks five candidates by Spearman ρ across these hands. By construction, Δskel is pushed toward zero or negative correlation with Δcontact, which is exactly why Δskel shows ρ = −0.40/−0.51. Thickness, Δcontact and Δskel are also mechanically coupled (Eq. 7 says Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q/2)|Δt|), so the candidates are not independent. The paper never shows the candidates' inter-correlation, VIFs or a joint model. "Δcontact outranks Δskel" is therefore partly a property of how the hands were chosen. The grid study (45 hands, ρ = 0.60, partial 0.58) is the better evidence, but it is also designed, and there Δskel is positively correlated (0.46, 0.86 with placements lost). I would like either a space-filling or random sample of redesigns over the feasible set, or a regression with all candidates entered jointly and their collinearity reported.

(2) The main protocol (reused position command) nearly makes H3 a tautology, and the protocols an experimenter would actually use leave little error. Under a reused flexion command with K0 = 5 Nm/rad, a face that sits deeper closes onto the cylinder earlier and squeezes harder (U 3.3×, fixed-axis 2.2–4.2×). The friction-limited pull-out load then scales with that squeeze. So "error follows Δcontact" largely reads "error follows over-closure", and Δcontact is a geometric proxy for over-closure. The paper partly admits this ("Not only through over-closure"): with reused torque the error falls to 3–40%, and protocols that sense contact or match force leave 20% and 3–7% median. Force-matched closing is standard in careful interface studies, and it leaves 3–7%. That severely limits the practical significance. The paper needs to show (a) that 3–7% is above the simulation noise floor and matters for an interface conclusion, and (b) whether Δcontact still predicts the residual error under force matching, with intervals. Squeeze force, the obvious mediator, should also be one of the candidates; it is the variable a reader would test first.

(3) The simulation contact model removes the confound the paper is about. "Pads of every thickness share one contact model, so E is what the redesign adds." In reality a 7 mm compliant pad differs from a 3 mm pad in effective stiffness, contact area, pressure distribution and, through those, friction behaviour, and these interact with the interface change being measured (friction, stiffness, shape). The paper argues kinematics matters, which is fair. But the size of the kinematic effect relative to the pad-mechanics effect is the question that decides whether C2 matters, and the simulation cannot answer it. MuJoCo-Warp soft constraints with contact stiffness of 1.0–2.5 kN/m and μ = 1.3–2.0 are also not a pad model. Limit-load-at-slip under slow pulling is exactly where soft-constraint creep, cone approximation and impedance parameters bias results. The robustness check (10× stiffness, impedance ratio, substeps) helps, but it reports only the R < U ordering, not the ρ values or the error magnitudes.

(4) No null control or noise floor for E. E(H) = |Δ(H) − Δ(O)|/|Δ(O)| is a relative error with a denominator that can be small (stiffness and shape effects on the limit load). Values up to ~200% in Fig. 5a suggest |Δ(O)| is small for some changes. I could not find E for a hand with Δcontact = 0: O itself under perturbed placements or solver seeds, or the spacer design that keeps Δcontact = 0 by construction. A spacer hand is the natural positive control for H3 and costs one more row. Absolute Δ(O) per interface change and per-hand dispersion over placements/directions should also be reported. E is a median over a hand-specific subset of placements "where both hands hold", so the evaluation set differs per hand. The post hoc 80% analysis is a partial fix only.

(5) Δcontact is a max over q ≤ q_req, but each trial is evaluated at q0 + 5° for q0 ∈ [20°, 120°]. The metric that should predict a trial's error is the contact-point difference at the test configuration, not the worst case over the range. The summary may be right, but the choice is not justified, and it mixes poses the trials never visit.

(6) H1 and C1 are narrower than stated. H1 compares only against 2R-DD, and only "where both have a design". 2R-remote (a fixed axis with remote actuation) beats the CFL at 90° and at thin pads at 120°. So the coverage gain at moderate flexion comes mostly from remote actuation, not the crossed four-bar. The four-bar's coverage advantage is clear only at 150°, plus its C2 advantage. "Mechanical advantage is not constrained" is a real fairness gap for a servo hand: a four-bar optimized for coverage can trade away transmission, and the grip-force consequences matter for the same interface experiments. Three GA seeds with an extra refinement step for CFL only is thin, though the paper says H1 holds without refinement.

(7) Terminology and claims. What is measured and held is the contact-surface path c(q), and Δcontact is a path distance. "Contact Jacobian" (after Montana/MLS) usually means the map from joint velocities to the contact-frame twist, including the normal, and the paper's J = [c′; 1] is only its planar PIP column. Keeping c(q) implies keeping J, but not the reverse, and the paper says "keeps the contact Jacobian" when it means the former. The title and abstract should use the measured quantity. "Distorts the friction effect by 95–228%" comes from a contrast family built to keep its links while thicker pads push its faces deeper. It is a straw man under a reused position command, and its torque-protocol error is not reported.

(8) Stage 2 plan. The Stage-2 plan is concrete but lacks a power or effect-size analysis. With 5 × 4 × 5 trials per hand and cover, can hardware scatter (pad hysteresis, slide friction, cover friction variability) resolve the predicted R vs U difference, which under torque control is ~5–11 points? The plan does not say how slide friction or pull-rate effects are controlled. E is still defined against O, which carries different pads. R vs U cancels that confound only if pad compliance and the friction change combine additively. A brief note on this, and on how "R errs no less than U" will be tested (paired test over placements? CI on the difference?), would make the falsification criterion operational.

Smaller points. Fig. 1 says U presses 3.9× and the text says 3.3×; I assume one is a single placement and the other a median. Coverage is given sometimes as fractions (0.085–0.096) and sometimes as points (2.4–14.6). "Matches the evaluator within 10^-13" is a self-consistency check, not validation. The text is very dense, many symbols are defined in passing, and Sec. III is hard to follow without rereading. Figs. 1b/c and 4d are small. The related work is appropriately focused for Stage 1, but it omits prior work on how fingertip/pad compliance and geometry confound grasp-force and friction measurements, which is the paper's own motivation.

Overall: a thoughtful, original framing with solid geometric analysis and better-than-usual statistical hygiene. But the central empirical claim (H3) rests on a designed hand set, a protocol that makes over-closure the dominant mechanism, and a contact model that abstracts away pad mechanics. Under the realistic, force-matched protocols the effect drops to single digits. For a Stage-1 abstract these are fixable: add a spacer null control, joint-candidate modelling, H3 tested under force matching with squeeze force as a candidate, and a power analysis for Stage 2. As written, I lean slightly toward reject but could be persuaded.
