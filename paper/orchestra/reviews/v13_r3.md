THOUGHT:
I read this as a kinematician. I checked Lemma 1, Propositions 1–3, Corollary 1, the derivation of Eq. (3), the "lower the pole by δ" argument, the numbers in Fig. 2 and the √3Δh contrast family. The paper reduces two design questions to the relative finite pole P(q) of the distal phalanx: how much pad room the PIP joint takes (C1), and whether a redesign keeps the relative motion of the two contact faces (C2). That framing is clean and, as far as I know, new for robot-hand interface studies. Most of the mathematics is right. My concerns are (i) how much of the theory is new beyond textbook pole geometry, (ii) how weak the falsification criteria for H1, H2 and the Stage-2 test are, (iii) the physical mechanism behind the large Study 3 errors, and (iv) a few gaps between the title and definitions and what is actually computed.

Mathematics, item by item:
- Lemma 1. In complex form, 1/(1−e^{iq}) = ½ + (i/2)cot(q/2), so P = ½(b + cot(q/2) R_{π/2} b). Correct. The statement "finite pole = instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis" is also correct as an identity in q. Differentiating c = R_q c0 + (I−R_q)P gives c' = (I−R_q)P' + R_{π/2}(c−P), which equals the IC form iff P' = 0.
- Rolling joint, z = r tan(q/4). I re-derived it from the perpendicular bisector of the distal roller centre's chord: the pole lies at distance r/cos(q/4) along the q/4 ray, so it sits at depth r tan(q/4) below the q=0 contact point. Correct.
- Prop. 1. Rotation by q about P equals the reflection in the q=0 mirror line followed by the reflection in the line at q/2. So under S1 and S2 the distal pad at q is the mirror image of the proximal pad, and the convex-set argument gives the iff. The joint-side face corner at depth t−z gives half-gap (t−z)tan(q/2). The Fig. 2 numbers (8.7 = 5 tan 60°, 15.6, 6.9) and 3.46 mm/mm agree. However, S2 needs P_x(q) to be constant in q. For a four-bar it is not. The 0.05 mm median agreement is the only evidence that S2 holds approximately, and no maximum is given.
- Prop. 2. The mirror argument gives a phalanx-end separation of G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2), so the denominator is correct. The claim that lowering the pole by δ adds the same amount 2δ tan(q_req/2) to numerator and denominator checks exactly: tan − tan·cos + sin = tan. Since C < 1, coverage rises. At q < q_req the denominator grows less, so the conclusion holds over Q_eval as well. One caveat: this treats the maximum in Eq. (2) as attained at q_req, i.e., t−z(q) not shrinking too fast.
- Prop. 3 and Eq. (6). Δc = (I−R_q)Δp, so ‖Δc‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖. Correct. The paper does not state the useful consequence: given the same start configuration, Δc does not depend on which distal-face point is tracked. That is the real justification for tracking a single point c. Eq. (7) is a correct triangle inequality on p and p + Δt·e_y. Corollary 1 is correct for q_req ≤ 180°, with equality when the axis does not slide along the face. The √3Δh = 2 sin 60° Δh value for the contrast family is consistent.
- Pole palmar of every pivot. For a crossed four-bar, the instantaneous centre near q=0 is the crossing point of the two links. That point lies inside the convex hull of the four pivots, so it cannot be palmar of every pivot. Only the finite pole at larger q (the intersection of the two lines through the ground pivots at the crank half-angles) can be. The sentence "a pole on a deep face must be virtual, as a four-bar's is: it can lie palmar of every physical pivot" needs this qualification, and a plot of z(q) for the optimal CFLs would settle it.

Conceptual and empirical issues:
1. "Keep the contact Jacobian" overstates what is shown. Δ_contact is a sup-norm of the integrated position difference of one point. A small Δ_contact does not bound the Jacobian difference (the relation runs the other way: Δ_contact ≤ q_req · max‖ΔJ‖). The CFL also does not keep it: it changes by 0.35–4.31 mm, 10–51% of a fixed-axis bound, and R still errs 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm. The bound itself rests on the "shallowest mount depth, no spacers or recess" rule, which removes the fixed axis's trivial fix of recessing. That rule is reasonable (the pin needs hardware above the mount surface), but it carries the whole of H2 and should be stated as the premise.
2. The H1 baseline is weak. 2R-DD has the servo body at the joint, so it is close to a strawman for pad room. The informative comparison, 2R-remote, is mixed (it wins at 90°, crosses at 120°, loses at 150°). The paper explains this honestly with Eq. (3), but C1 as written avoids the real competitor. The coverage normalisation by L_f(q) also rewards a gap that shortens the contour when flexed, so the cell-wise ranking may depend on that choice.
3. The H2 falsifier ("fails if it reaches the bound") is very weak. The bound applies to fixed axes, and a four-bar with a movable pole and a free optimiser will almost always beat it. A meaningful H2 would state an absolute Δ_contact tolerance tied to the Study 3 error, or a CFL-versus-2R-remote comparison at equal coverage.
4. In Study 3 the error is largely a normal-force effect of position control with a small preload. The grasp closes only 5° past touch with K0 = 5 N·m/rad, about 0.44 N·m. A 1 mm deeper face at a ~45 mm lever adds ~0.11 N·m, a 25% force change. With Coulomb friction, the relative friction-effect error is about |N_H/N_O − 1|, which matches "presses 2.2–4.2× harder" → 95–228%. So the headline magnitudes depend on the protocol (preload and K0). The robustness check reports only the ordering R < U, not magnitudes. The paper's own result that force-matched protocols leave 3–7% error weakens the practical significance: an interface study could simply match contact force. It is still useful that torque reuse leaves R about half of U, and that Δ_contact stays the only significant predictor.
5. The candidate comparison partly reflects the sample. Eq. (7) forces Δ_contact and Δ_skel to trade off at fixed Δh, so the negative ρ for Δ_skel in Fig. 1d is built in. The partial correlations and the grid experiment help, and the grid shows Δ_skel also significant (0.46). The stated H3 falsifier ("fails if another candidate predicts it in every setting") is lax: H3 would survive even if Δ_contact predicted nothing.
6. Δ_contact for 2R-DD and RCJ includes a start offset (c0 moved along the face). Under Eq. (6) that offset is not a change in motion. The paper reports the offset-free comparison (7 of 9), which is good, but the definition should separate the two.
7. Stage 2. C1 on hardware has no hypothesis or threshold ("we record parts, time and recalibration"). The C2 falsifier ("R errs no less than U") has no predicted effect size and no power argument for 5×4×5 trials per hand and cover.
8. Minor inconsistencies. Fig. 1 says U presses 3.9× harder; the text says 3.3×. Coverage is reported both as fractions (0.085, 0.182) and as points. "(0.21, 0)" in Fig. 3 is not explained. Optimisation uses three seeds, with no seed spread against the 2.4-point minimum margin. "Pole-based coverage matches the evaluator within 1e-13" is a self-consistency check, not a validation. The torque protocol says nothing about servo torque limits, although mechanical advantage is unconstrained.
9. Related work. Classical analyses of how linkage-driven and underactuated fingers distribute force over phalanx contacts (Birglen, Laliberté and Gosselin) and grasp/hand-Jacobian treatments (Prattichizzo and Trinkle) are directly relevant to "where the faces meet the object matters at equal force". Centrode-based design of polycentric joints beyond one knee paper would also help place Props. 1–3.

Overall: a well-posed Stage-1 paper with correct, compact kinematics, a clear hypothesis structure and unusually thorough preliminary simulation. The theory is elementary pole geometry put to a new use, the title overstates what is achieved, and the practical significance depends on open-loop protocol reuse. I lean accept for Stage 1 if the falsifiers and the force-mechanism discussion are tightened.
