ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper looks at a real and familiar problem for anyone who has built servo-chain hands. Servo bodies at the joints leave room for a contact interface only at the fingertip, and a finger redesigned to carry a thicker pad no longer moves like the original. A pad or tactile comparison across redesigns therefore mixes the interface effect with the kinematic change. There are two claims.

C1: a PIP joint actuated remotely through a crossed four-bar (CFL) gives more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. The geometric argument is clean and, to my knowledge, new in this framing. It rests on the finite-pole lemma, the clearance bound G_sym (Prop. 1) and the coverage law (Prop. 2): a fixed axis loses 2 tan(q_req/2) of gap per mm of face depth, while a four-bar's virtual pole can follow the face. Study 1 supports the claim well. CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions by 2.4–14.6 points, the worst of six CFL runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10, and the 2R-remote ablation separates remote actuation from the polycentric joint. The ablation is honest, because it shows 2R-remote matching or beating CFL at 90° and at thin pads. The claim is restricted to 2R-DD, and that restriction is correct. The cost is mechanical advantage: CFL has a worst case of 0.41–0.58 against 1 for a servo at the joint. Study 1 leaves this free. For low-cost servo hands that are already short of grasp force, this is not a footnote. It roughly halves fingertip force for the same servo. The trade-off is only named in the conclusion. I would want at least one coverage-vs-MA front.

C2: a redesign that keeps the PIP column of the contact Jacobian (small Δ_contact) lets the interface be studied on its own. The theory is good. Eq. (7) and Corollary 1 show that a fixed axis cannot keep both the contact-surface motion and the skeleton motion. They also show why "keep the skeleton" fails, which is a nice counter-intuitive point, illustrated in Fig. 1b–c. The evidence is all simulation: Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp, point contact, and pad stiffness entered as contact normal stiffness. Within that model, the statistics are careful. They use pre-registered tests, bootstrap and permutation CIs, a Holm correction, partial correlation given setpoint force, and Δ_contact explains more than the other four candidates (ρ 0.80–0.86 vs ≤0.22 for the skeleton when the setpoint is reused). The claim is narrower than the title and abstract suggest, for four reasons.

1. It holds for the reused-impedance-setpoint protocol. Under the "touch" protocol, the skeleton predicts the error better (0.88 vs 0.44), and the paper says so.
2. Even the best redesign R still errs by 21–25% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm, so the interface is not studied "on its own" beyond about 7 mm. The 20% tolerance is chosen post hoc.
3. Only the PIP column is kept. The MCP column, whose lever includes pad depth, changes. So "keep the contact Jacobian" really means "keep one column of it".
4. The scope is multi-phalanx grasps. The fingertip, where most interface studies happen, is explicitly excluded.

From the hardware side, the scale of the proposed tolerance is the most important issue. The tolerance is "Δ_contact below about 1 mm". A 1.0 kN/m pad compresses about 1 mm per newton. Servo backlash of 0.25–0.5° at a 40–50 mm lever, and pin clearance in a printed crossed four-bar, are each a few tenths of a mm. All of these are the same order as the tolerance and will move the effective contact surface under load. The simulation folds compression into contact penetration, but it has no backlash, link compliance or pad conformity (area, torsional friction). The Stage 2 protocol has markers, R/M/U on one finger, a planar slide, 5×4×5 repeats and a one-sided Page trend test, with the failure criterion stated in advance. That is the right test and is pre-registered well. It should also report the measured backlash and pad compression next to Δ_contact.

A definitional point: the "true" interface change in Fig. 1d is the stiffness swap on O at 3 mm. Thickness itself is thus assigned to the redesign, not to the interface. That is defensible, but it should be stated explicitly, because a pad designer would say thickness is part of the pad.

Stage 1 requirements: the problem, hypotheses H1–H3, methods and failure criteria are fully specified, and the insights from a negative Stage 2 result are clear. The related work is compact and relevant. However, prior work on finger kinematics designed around contact location is thin, and so are compliant-pad models beyond a spring.

Presentation is the main weakness. The text is extremely dense, with a large symbol and acronym load (O/R/U/M/M0, CFL, RCJ, 2R-DD/2R-remote, S1/S2, Δ_contact/Δ_skel, Q_eval). Figure text in Figs. 2–4 is too small to read in print, and the Table I caption is a paragraph of statistics. Some sentences do not parse ("Each alternative removes the over-closure with something such a hand rarely has"). The Prop. 2 derivation refers to Eq. (2) where Eq. (3) seems to be meant.

Scores: originality 3 (pole-based framing of pad room and contact-surface motion is new to me), quality 3 (sound theory, careful statistics, model-limited evidence), clarity 2, significance 3. The work is a solid Stage 1 submission, so Accept at 6.
