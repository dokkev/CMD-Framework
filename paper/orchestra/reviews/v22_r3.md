ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper claims two things about one planar finger of a servo-driven hand. C1: remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute PIP (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. C2: a redesign for a thicker pad should keep the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame (equivalently, the motion c(q) of the distal face relative to the proximal face), not the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot keep it (closed-form lower bound), the CFL keeps it to 10–51% of that bound, and under a reused joint-space impedance setpoint this quantity predicts how much the redesign corrupts a measured interface effect.

I reviewed this as a kinematician and checked the mathematics by hand.
- Lemma 1: (I − R_q)^{-1} = ½(I + cot(q/2) R_{π/2}) is correct. The remark that the finite pole equals the instantaneous centre only for a fixed axis is also correct: differentiating P = R_qP + b gives (I − R_q)P′ = 0, so P′ = 0.
- Proposition 1: the two-reflection argument is correct. A rotation by q about P is a reflection in the vertical through P followed by a reflection in a line tilted by q/2. A convex set and its mirror image intersect iff the set meets the mirror line. The deep joint-side corner crosses first, which gives G ≥ 2(d+h−z)tan(q/2). The positive part is right for a pole below the face.
- Fig. 2 numbers check out: 5·tan60° = 8.66, 9·tan60° = 15.6, 4·tan60° = 6.93. So do the 3.46 mm/mm rate at 120° and the factor tan75°/tan45° ≈ 3.7.
- Proposition 2: the phalanx ends lie G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2) apart (twice the distance of r_{1,e} to the mirror line), so the denominator is right.
- The "lower the pole by δ" argument holds. The numerator and the denominator at q_req both grow by 2δ tan(q_req/2). I also checked that at the other evaluation poses the denominator grows by less (tan Q(1−cos q′) + sin q′ ≤ tan Q for q′ ≤ Q < 180°), so C̄_pad itself rises. The paper states this only at q_req.
- Eq. (6), ‖(I − R_q)Δp‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖, is right. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality, since the two pole shifts differ by Δt·e_n. Corollary 1 is correct, with equality when the axis does not slide along the face.
- The RCJ claim z = r tan(q/4) is right. For two equal rollers the finite pole lies on the bisector at distance r/cos(q/4) from the proximal roller centre, which gives x fixed and depth r tan(q/4).
- The statement that keeping c(q) exactly forces the pivots down by Δh is also right, since a generic four-bar coupler motion has only its two moving pivots as circling points.

I found no mathematical error. One useful consequence goes unstated: with c0 common, Δc(q) = (I − R_q)Δp(q) does not depend on which distal-face point is chosen. Δcontact is therefore a property of the joint, not of the point "below the fingertip". The paper should say so, because it is what licenses "c stands for any such point".

Whether the studies test the propositions:
- Study 1 (H1) is a clean falsifiable comparison. The 2R-remote ablation usefully separates remote actuation, which is enough at 90°, from the polycentric joint, which matters as flexion deepens. Eq. (2) is validated against exact clearance (median 0.05 mm). The extra CFL refinement is asymmetric, but the authors show the result holds without it.
- Two caveats on C1. (i) The 2R-DD result depends on the servo body dimensions, which the paper never gives. (ii) The CFL buys coverage with a worst-case mechanical advantage of 0.41–0.58 against 1 for both 2R variants. "More contact surface" is therefore a Pareto statement at free MA, and the paper says so only in the conclusion.
- Study 2 (H2) tests Corollary 1 directly. 2R-remote meeting the bound exactly at 90°/120° is a good sanity check, and the CFL at 10–51% of the bound supports the claim.
- The spacer alternative is dismissed because it works "only if every interface is known in advance". Strictly it needs only the maximum thickness. A design built for h_max plus spacers is a legitimate baseline, and its 8.6–18.2-point cost deserves a fairer framing.
- Study 3 (H3) is the weakest link between the propositions and the evidence. No proposition connects Δcontact to the interface-effect error, and the support is correlational (Spearman ρ 0.80–0.86 over 14–45 hands, with wide within-thickness intervals [0.41, 0.99] and [0.35, 0.98]).
  - The redesigns at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton are a good decorrelation device. The partial correlations against setpoint force and normal offset help.
  - The post hoc finding that the error's direction follows the face's signed normal offset (ρ = −0.99) suggests the mechanistically relevant quantity is the normal component of Δc near the setpoint pose, not the max-norm over [0, q_req].
  - With point contact and pad stiffness modelled as a contact spring, the redesign changes only geometry and transmission. Some predictive power of a geometric quantity is therefore partly built in.
  - A first-order model of the residual would turn the correlation into a mechanism. One such model: same J and same joint-space K0 give the same contact-space stiffness K0/‖c′‖² and the same overclosure.
  - Whether K0 is specified in joint or servo space also matters, because it determines whether the transmission really drops out.
- The title and C2 say "keep the contact Jacobian", but only the PIP column in F_p is kept. The MCP column changes with pad depth, and all studies hold the MCP. The scope is stated in the text but overstated in the headline claims.

For a Stage 1 extended abstract, the problem, hypotheses, falsification criteria and a Stage 2 hardware protocol are fully laid out, and the analysis is correct and insightful. I rate it a solid accept with the caveats above.
