THOUGHT:

My reading as a manipulation / contact-modelling reviewer. The paper makes three moves: (i) a planar kinematic argument based on the finite pole of the distal phalanx, giving a clearance law (Eq. 4), a coverage law (Eq. 5), the relation |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2) |Δp| (Eq. 6), a "sum rule" Δ_contact + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt| (Eq. 7) and a fixed-axis lower bound (Eq. 8); (ii) design searches (Studies 1 and 2) showing a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than the baselines and, when re-optimized for thicker pads, changes Δ_contact by only 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound; (iii) a simulation study (Study 3) arguing that when one flexion command is reused across redesigns, the error in a measured interface effect (friction, contact stiffness, crowning) tracks Δ_contact (Spearman 0.83, n = 15) and not Δ_skel.

The kinematics (i) are tidy and look correct. Lemma 1 is classical (Bottema and Roth). The mirror-image argument for Prop. 1 is neat, and the fixed-axis bound is a real if modest insight: a fixed axis cannot follow a thickening face. Studies 1–2 are design-space evidence. They are believable but depend on the optimizer and the packaging rules. I was asked to concentrate on Study 3. Study 3 carries the "why it matters" claim (C4), and that claim is what would make this interesting to RSS beyond finger kinematics. I have substantial concerns about it.

1. The experiment comes close to being circular. Each test placement is defined by the reference O: "the place along the face where O touches it with both pads at q0". Each hand then closes to q0 + 5° under a stiff joint impedance (K0 = 5 Nm/rad). Δ_contact is, by definition, the deviation of a hand's distal-face path from O's path. A hand whose distal face sits deeper into O's cylinder at q0 penetrates more under the same 5° overshoot and squeezes harder (Fig. 1: 17.4 N vs 4.5 N). A hand whose face sits farther away squeezes less or never touches ("redesigns whose distal face retreats 1.5–2.6 mm cannot hold the cylinder"). So the experiment largely measures "deviation from O's contact geometry leads to a different grip force under position control". That is the definition of Δ_contact pushed through a spring. It is not surprising, and the paper half-admits this ("as the mechanism predicts"). The non-trivial content is the size of the effect, not the ordering. The paper should frame the result that way, or design a test in which the placement is not defined by O's own contact kinematics.

2. Δ_skel is anticorrelated with Δ_contact by construction. Eq. (7) states that both cannot be small together when the face depth changes. The redesign set was built "at set values of Δ_contact, some keeping the skeleton as closely as possible and some changing it as much as possible". On that set, a near-zero or negative correlation of Δ_skel with the error (Fig. 1d reports ρ = −0.40) is forced by the sum rule plus the design selection. It is not an independent empirical finding. The headline "the change in skeleton motion does not predict it" is therefore weaker than stated. The partial correlation (0.88 controlling for Δ_skel) on 15 hands drawn from a hand-curated grid does not escape this. Also, "skeleton motion" is defined as the fingertip path measured from the link line. That is a somewhat strawman alternative: a practitioner reusing commands would more likely try to keep the pad-surface path, which is Δ_contact. Other plausible alternative predictors are never tested: the signed normal offset of the distal face at q0, the change in MA_wc, the change in contact location along the face, or simply pad thickness.

3. There is no ground truth for the "error". E(H) treats O's measured effect Δ(O) as the truth for a hand with 5–9 mm pads. The paper itself says contact mechanics legitimately change with the interface. So a non-zero |Δ(H) − Δ(O)| mixes genuine thickness-dependent mechanics with kinematic distortion. R still "errs" 41% at 9 mm, and the paper cannot say how much of that is a real effect of a thicker pad. An obvious oracle exists in the paper itself: the spacer design, which keeps Δ_contact = 0 by construction. Running it in simulation (it is infeasible only for coverage, which does not matter in sim) would give the irreducible error at Δ_contact = 0 for each thickness. The error could then be measured relative to that. Without it, the claim that R "keeps" the measured effect cannot be checked.

4. Pad thickness and pad material are not modelled as a deformable pad. "Pads are represented by a contact model". Material stiffness is a contact stiffness of 2.5→1.0 kN/m. Thickness then seems to change only geometry. Yet the motivation for thick compliant pads and tactile modules is their bulk deformation, patch growth and structural compliance, which scale with thickness. If a 9 mm pad has the same contact law as a 3 mm pad, the simulation removes the very mechanics that motivate the study. A related point: at 1.0 kN/m and the reported forces (several N to 17 N), the implied penetrations are millimetres to over a centimetre, comparable to Δ_contact itself. That raises the question whether the error is dominated by solver penetration rather than by the kinematics. The robustness check (tenfold contact stiffness, impedance ratio ×0.3/×3, doubled substeps) is reported only as R-vs-U paired wins, not as the effect on ρ or on the error magnitudes.

5. The statistics are thin and partly non-independent.
   - n = 15 hands (14 for stiffness, 6 for shape). The 95% interval for the friction ρ is [0.41, 1.00]. The upper bound pinned at 1.00 suggests a percentile bootstrap that is degenerate at small n. The method should be stated (resampling unit, number of resamples, handling of ties).
   - The hands fall into three thickness clusters and several design families (R, U, keep-skeleton, change-skeleton). Pooled Spearman over clustered data overstates the evidence. A thickness-stratified or mixed-effects analysis, with per-thickness ρ values reported, is needed. "It does so at each thickness" on about 5 hands per thickness is not a statistical statement.
   - The three interface changes are measured on the same hands, at the same placements, with the same controller. Their errors are driven by the same grip-force overshoot. "Pad stiffness and shape effects repeat this" is therefore not independent replication. Shape with n = 6 (why only 6?) and ρ = 0.94 adds little.
   - The error is a median over placements and directions of a ratio with |Δ(O)| in the denominator, which is unstable when O's effect is small. The number of placements is unclear: 21 q0 values × 2 radii × 4 directions, but the robustness section speaks of 22–24 paired placements. So is the filtering.
   - Excluding the three hands that cannot hold the cylinder is a selection step. The paper does report ρ = 0.81 with them ranked worst, which is good practice.
   - Δ_contact is a single maximum over q ≤ q_req, taken at one point below the fingertip. The grasps happen at specific q0 and at contact points elsewhere on the face. The natural, much stronger test is within-hand: does the per-placement error track |Δc(q0)| at the actual contact location? That would multiply the effective n and break the hand-level confounds.

6. Protocol choice. The paper argues that touch- or force-referenced closing is unclean because "the interface itself changes" the signals. Yet its own numbers show those protocols cut the error to a median of 20% (touch) and 3–7% (force), against R's 3–41% under the reused command. Actuator-side torque or current sensing does not depend on the pad interface in the same way a tactile signal does. The paper's own data suggest that a simple force-matched protocol largely removes the problem for any hand, including U. This weakens the practical case for redesigning the kinematics. At least, the argument for why force matching is unacceptable needs to be much more concrete. The impedance sweep (U: 44% at 0.1 K0 vs 119% at K0) likewise shows the effect is controller-gain dependent.

7. Studies 1–2. Results come from GA/NSGA-II with three seeds and no reported seed-to-seed variance. Gaps of 2–3 points in coverage may lie within optimizer noise. In Study 2, 2R-DD and RCJ do not keep c0 (Fig. 4d starts at about 5 and 9 mm). It is unclear why their redesigns are allowed to move the contact start point, and this inflates their Δ_contact. That is a fairness issue for the "CFL least in every cell" claim. "10–51% of the bound" compares the CFL with a bound that formally holds for a different joint class with Δd = 0, so it is a reference line, not a bound on the competitors. The text is mostly careful here, but the abstract is not.

Presentation. The writing is compressed to the point of opacity in places: S1/S2, the mount rule, T_i, "pole measured from the link line instead", the meaning of "keeps J". Fig. 1 is effective. Fig. 5b's overlapping markers and the "22–24 of 22–24" phrasing are hard to parse. Results text for Study 3 is placed after the conclusion header in the extracted text, though that may be a float-layout artefact. The paper fits the Stage-1 format: hypotheses, methods and a concrete Stage-2 hardware plan with an "either outcome is informative" statement are present. That counts in its favour.

Overall. The kinematic insight is original and the reframing is useful: hold the contact Jacobian column, not the skeleton, when comparing interfaces. But the simulation, which carries the significance claim, is close to definitional, has no ground-truth reference, uses a contact model that omits thickness-dependent pad mechanics, and rests on 15 curated, clustered hands. I lean reject at Stage 1 but could be persuaded. The issues are fixable within the Stage-1 → Stage-2 process: a spacer oracle, a deformable-pad model, within-hand analysis, stratified statistics, and an honest treatment of force-referenced protocols.
