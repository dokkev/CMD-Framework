THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a finger must be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface (pad, tactile module), the quantity worth preserving is the motion of the distal contact face relative to the proximal contact face (one column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton/fingertip motion. It supports this with (i) planar finite-pole kinematics: a clearance law, a coverage law, a relation ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||, and a fixed-axis lower bound 2 sin(q_req/2)Δh; (ii) design searches (GA/NSGA-II) comparing 2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ and a crossed four-bar (CFL) on pad coverage (Study 1) and on Δ_contact after redesign (Study 2); and (iii) a simulated grasp-and-pull study (Study 3) showing that, when one flexion command is reused, the error in measured friction/compliance/shape effects follows Δ_contact (Spearman 0.83) rather than Δ_skel.

My first impression: the framing is original and the problem (confounded interface comparisons because the hand changes with the interface) is real and under-discussed. The analysis is clean and the Study 3 protocol has good habits (gates fixed before runs, several protocols, robustness to solver parameters, partial correlations). The Stage-2 plan is concrete and says what each outcome would show. For a Stage-1 extended abstract the methodology is mostly there.

My main concerns, in order of weight:

1. Possible circularity in Study 3: the object-placement frame. The cylinder is placed "where the reference O touches it with both pads at q0". Fig. 1 shows R's cylinder shifted relative to O's (dashed), so placement appears to be relative to the contact faces, not the palm or skeleton. If the object is placed in the contact-face frame, then a hand whose distal face moves the same way relative to the proximal face will, by construction, reach first contact at the same command. Under stiff position control, the overclosure past first contact then sets the preload and hence the limit load. So the result "error follows Δ_contact" may follow mostly from choosing a contact-face-anchored placement plus a stiff position-controlled closure. With palm/skeleton-anchored placement, which is how many real benchmark fixtures work, I would expect Δ_skel or the face offset to dominate. The touch-referenced protocol supports this reading: once the first-contact offset is removed, the error follows Δ_skel (ρ = 0.88). The paper needs to state the placement frame explicitly and justify it, or show the result under a second placement convention. As written, the headline correlation reads as close to tautological: "the geometry that sets where contact begins predicts the error of a protocol that closes by a fixed amount from a fixed place".

2. The paper's own data weaken the practical case for redesign. Matching contact force leaves 3–7% error and closing from each finger's own touch leaves 20%, while R itself errs 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. The cheapest way to make interface comparisons fair seems to be a better protocol, not a re-optimized linkage. The paper says "the geometry an interface study must keep depends on what it reuses", which is honest, but then the significance rests on how common blind joint-command reuse is across hands. That is asserted, not documented: scripted grasps and frozen policies are cited generically, and learned policies are usually closed-loop. The contribution should be framed accordingly, or the paper should show a realistic reused-policy scenario where the protocol fixes are not available.

3. "Keeps" is overstated. The CFL reduces Δ_contact to 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound. It does not keep it. At 9 mm, Δ_contact = 4.31 mm at 90° and about 2.3 mm at 120°. In Study 3 these magnitudes correspond to 40–100%+ measurement error. There is no tolerance stating how small Δ_contact must be for a comparison to count as fair, so "keeps the contact-surface motion" in the title, abstract, C3 and the conclusion is not quantitatively anchored. Also, "within 10–51% of that bound" in the abstract reads as "close to the bound", when the meaning is "at 10–51% of the bound" (better than it).

4. Theory: correct but modest, and it rests on a few assumptions that are not well defined.
 - Lemma 1 is the textbook finite-rotation pole. Prop. 3/Eq. (6) is ||(I − R_q)Δp|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||. Corollary 1 then follows almost directly: a fixed axis is its own pole, and its distance to the face grows by Δh − Δd. Calling this a proof of impossibility is fine, but the bound is only as strong as the mount rule (Δd = 0, "as close as hardware allows"). A fixed-axis designer could shrink the joint hardware, or accept spacers. The paper addresses spacers (8.6–18.2 points of coverage lost), but not smaller hardware.
 - Frame ambiguity in Eqs. (3) and (7). F_p has its origin on the proximal contact face, which itself moves by Δh when the pad thickens. So c and r_C for different designs are expressed in different frames relative to the skeleton. Eq. (7) assumes "both start points kept", but under S1 the fingertip and the distal-face point differ in depth by the face depth t, so both cannot be kept when t changes. The text then says the fingertip relation uses the pole "measured from the link line instead". It needs one clear statement of which frame each Δ is measured in. This matters because the Δ_contact-vs-Δ_skel contrast is the central claim.
 - Props. 1–2 assume equal face depths (S1) and symmetric corners (S2). The validation on CFL designs (0.05 mm median) is reassuring, but it is not said how many designs satisfy S1/S2, or how large the error is for the other architectures.

5. Statistics and design selection in Study 3. The 18 hands were "chosen on design metrics so that Δ_contact, Δ_skel and C̄_pad vary apart". Fig. 1d reports ρ = −0.40 against skeleton change. A negative rank correlation between the skeleton change and the error suggests that the selection made Δ_skel and Δ_contact anti-correlated, which inflates the apparent contrast. The selection rule needs to be described, and the Δ_contact–Δ_skel correlation in the sample reported. n = 15 hands, clustered in three thickness groups, gives a CI of [0.41, 1.00]. The p = 10^-4 presumably treats hands as independent. Three hands that cannot hold the cylinder are excluded. That likely helps rather than hurts the claim, but it should be shown (e.g., as censored points). The shape result rests on 6 hands.

6. Simulation fidelity. Pads are a contact model: compliance is a contact stiffness of 1.0–2.5 kN/m, and shape is a 2 mm crown. A thick compliant pad deforms, and its effective contact location shifts under load, which is exactly the regime where geometric Δ_contact may stop being the dominant term. Isaac Lab 3 "with Newton and MuJoCo-Warp": which solver produced which result? The limit-load definition ("until the grasp gives way") also needs a threshold.

7. Structure and clarity. There are two threads: coverage (C1-coverage, C2, Study 1) and contact-motion preservation (C3, C4). C2 and Study 1 are only loosely tied to the main message, and they cost a full figure and a section in a 5-page abstract. The coverage result is also mixed: 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90° and for thin pads at 120°. The prose is terse and mostly readable, but the acronym load (CoDe, CFL, CFL ideal, 2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, RCJ ideal, O/R/U, MA_wc, S1/S2) is heavy. The title is long and reads like two slogans. Minor inconsistencies: Fig. 1 caption says U presses 3.9× harder (17.4 vs 4.5 N), while the text says 3.3×. Fig. 5b gives touch-referenced ρ = 0.44 (vs Δ_contact, n = 18), while the text gives ρ = 0.88 vs Δ_skel, which is not plotted. The Lemma 1 statement has a dangling "where and". The cylinder angle of 105° (Fig. 1) vs the generic q0 in the text should be reconciled.

Desired outcome: I think the idea deserves to be developed. For Stage 1, the methodology section is fleshed out and the Stage-2 plan is good. But the central empirical claim (C4) has a plausible circularity that the authors must rule out, and the significance argument is weakened by the protocol results. I lean towards rejection in its current form, but could be persuaded if the placement-frame issue is clarified convincingly.
