ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper asks which kinematic property a finger should keep when its contact interface (pad thickness or compliance) changes and the original command or policy is reused. Its answer is to keep the "contact-surface motion" (the path of the distal contact point seen from the proximal-pad frame, Δ_contact), not the skeleton (the fingertip path in the link frame, Δ_skel). The paper makes four claims. I rate the evidence for each below.

(C1) Analytic claim: "no redesign whose contact surfaces deepen keeps both" (Eq. 4: Δ_contact + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|), and a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion (Eq. 5).
- The finite-displacement pole algebra in Eqs. (1) and (3) is correct and elegant.
- Eq. (4) depends on Δt, which the text defines as "how far the contact surfaces deepen, not how much the pad thickens: a redesign that raises its pivots lets the surfaces deepen less". That makes the impossibility conditional on a quantity the designer can partly control. The paper never says what envelope or link-line constraint stops Δt from going to zero, for example by raising the whole proximal structure.
- Eq. (3) assumes "the same initial contact point c0". Yet Eq. (7) says "a moved initial contact point counts", so it is unclear whether Eq. (4) still bounds the Δ_contact that is actually optimized.
- The evidence is moderate. The algebra holds; how far it reaches is unclear.

(C2) Design claim: the crossed four-bar ("CoDe") gives more room than at-joint and keeps the most contact motion.
- Fig. 3 supports this within the paper's own design space: CoDe has higher coverage in 10/10 feasible conditions and keeps the most motion in 9/9.
- All of it is optimization in simulation and CAD. There is no hardware, and the claim of a modular, exchangeable interface ("exchangeable without disassembling the joint") is never shown.
- The evidence is adequate for the planar, PIP-only scope.

(C3) Empirical claim: the error in a measured pad effect follows Δ_contact, not Δ_skel.
- This is the strongest part: partial ρ of 0.86–0.91 with Δ_skel held fixed, a permutation test with Holm correction, two simulators, two contact models, and Table I.
- Weaknesses:
  - The correlation set is small (18 redesigns), and how it is built is unclear.
  - In Drake the correlation is much weaker: Fig. 4 shows ρ = 0.53 for the dial and 0.43 for the box pivot.
  - There are no dispersion measures in Table I.
  - The claim is "pre-registered" with no registration record.
  - The "compliant pad" is only a change in contact stiffness ("no pad deforms").
  - The reference itself is a counterfactual: O carrying a 7 mm pad's stiffness on its own 3 mm geometry.
- The bigger conceptual question is left open: why the right "true" pad effect should be the one measured on O's geometry. Part of a thicker pad's real effect is its geometry. Defining error relative to O builds the conclusion partly into the metric.

(C4) Policy-transfer claim: a policy learned on O keeps its performance on the redesign that preserves the motion its task uses.
- The evidence is weak:
  - Only two seeds, and CoDe's cube-rotation result varies widely between them (about 95% vs. about 57%).
  - The predictor changes from task to task (Δ_contact, Δ_skel, "palm shift"), and the choice looks post hoc.
  - On the relay dial, CoDe does worst. The paper explains this by placing that task "outside the claim".

My scores rest on three judgments. The idea is original and well motivated (a pole-based view of interface-invariant kinematics). The simulation study of C3 is careful. The physical validation, the policy evidence and the precision of the "contact Jacobian" framing fall short of the paper's framing as the "Design of a Contact-Centric Robot Hand".

Presentation and clarity items:

Figure 1
1. Fig. 1(c) and (d) use "Conventional" while the text, Fig. 4, Fig. 5 and Table I use "Coverage". Use one name everywhere.
2. Fig. 1 caption, "a conventional redesign … keeps neither": in Fig. 1(c) right, the Conventional curve sits below CoDe's, and Fig. 4 gives Δ_skel = 3.4 (Coverage) vs. 4.8 (CoDe). Coverage keeps the skeleton better than CoDe. Rephrase, for example to "keeps neither well", and give the numbers.
3. Fig. 1(c) panel title "contact-surface motion (contact Jacobian)": the plotted quantity is a path deviation in mm, not a Jacobian. Retitle it "contact-point path deviation".
4. Fig. 1(d): the floating label "run unchanged" and the arrow lengths (−41%, −39%, +48%) have no axis. Add a labeled horizontal axis (change in pull-out load, %).
5. Fig. 1(d) vs. Table I: Fig. 1(d) and Sec. IV-B1 give O −41% and Coverage +48%, which implies an error of about (48+41)/41 = 217%. Table I lists Coverage "compl. pad" as +125%. State whether Table I takes medians of per-placement errors, and reconcile the two.

Section III and the equations
6. Sec. III-C, "Equal paths mean equal contact Jacobians, so Δ_contact measures how far the contact Jacobian is from being kept": the converse fails, because a constant offset keeps the Jacobian but gives a nonzero Δ_contact. Either define Δ_contact as a path deviation or add a separate Jacobian metric. Make the same change in the abstract and the Introduction ("its contact Jacobian (how contact points move with flexion…)").
7. Eq. (2): the clause "taken from coverage" after "3.46 mm of gap at q_req = 120°" is unclear. State that 3.46 = 2 tan 60°, and say what "taken from coverage" means.
8. Eq. (4) derivation: say explicitly that it needs c0 (and r_C at q = 0) to be shared, and say how this squares with Eq. (7)'s "a moved initial contact point counts". Also state the envelope constraint that fixes Δt.
9. Eq. (9) is a 3-objective Pareto problem, but "CoDe is the design on the Pareto front … that keeps both best at near-best coverage" is not an operational selection rule. Give the rule, such as a coverage tolerance and a lexicographic order.
10. Eq. (8): define 𝓕(K, h, q_req) in words in the same sentence (collision-free, transmission-angle bound), and define 𝓕′ in Eq. (9) where it first appears, not two sentences later.

Section IV and Table I
11. Sec. IV-A: "they form the 18-redesign correlation set" is not reproducible from the text. Give the exact composition (how many held-Δ_contact redesigns per thickness, and the thicknesses). Also explain the separate "27 grid redesigns", "16 Drake redesigns" and "14 redesigns (0–6 mm)" sets, ideally in a small table.
12. "Pre-registered analyses fixed hands, statistic and criterion before any redesign was run": give a timestamped registry or commit reference, or drop "pre-registered" from the abstract.
13. Sec. IV-A, "Isaac Lab (MuJoCo-Warp, point contact)": say which Isaac Lab backend and version this is. It is unusual, and readers will assume PhysX.
14. Table I:
   - Add IQRs or bootstrap CIs.
   - Say which pad thickness, task and number of placements each column uses. The caption mentions none.
   - Add the noise floor ("O against itself … errs at most 6%") as a footnote, so that cells like −8% and +8% can be read against it.
   - Explain the empty cells. Drake point has no stiffness or compl. pad column, and Drake hydro has no convex column.
15. Sec. IV-B1, "CoDe's median error is closer to zero than Coverage's in eleven of twelve settings": name the exception (torque: Coverage 0% vs. CoDe +8%). Also note that the touch (−17 vs. −14%) and force (−4 vs. 0%) differences fall within the noise floor.

Figures 2 to 5
16. Fig. 2(a):
   - There is a stray horizontal blue segment at the top of the CoDe region, around 30 mm, for x ≈ 10–45. Fix or explain it.
   - The legend does not separate "Revolute" (a line on the axis) from the gray "contact faces" band clearly.
   - Add units to the "along the finger" axis label.
17. Fig. 3(c):
   - The y-axis runs down to −100% with "×" (infeasible) markers drawn on that line, which reads as a data value. Put the infeasible markers in a separate band.
   - Show b numerically, or at least for 7 mm at 120°.
18. Fig. 3(a), right: the markers (triangle, square, diamond, circle) at 3–9 mm overlap. Offset them horizontally, or use lines.
19. Fig. 4:
   - The Δ_contact and Δ_skel rows (1.1/6.6/3.9; 4.8/0.3/3.4) repeat identically under all four panels. Print them once.
   - Raise the font size of the E rows.
   - Report the n behind each ρ and whether it is significant. The dial (0.53) and box pivot (0.43) ρ values are weak, and the text ("keep this ordering") glosses over that.
20. Fig. 5:
   - Each panel lists a different predictor below its bars (Δ_contact, Δ_skel, palm shift). Say in the caption, or in Sec. IV-A, that the predictor for each task was fixed in advance, and how.
   - Add per-seed error bars or more seeds.
   - The y-label "share of O's performance kept" should define performance for each task.

Remaining text
21. Introduction, "keep the skeleton (… that hand design has always preserved)": cite support or soften it.
22. Contributions, third bullet, "a policy learned on the original hand transfers to the redesign that keeps the motion its task uses": soften this to match Fig. 5 (55–95%, two seeds).
23. Related Work is very short. Add:
   - literature on cross-embodiment and morphology-robust policy transfer;
   - classical path- and coupler-curve synthesis (Burmester theory), which bears directly on "keep the contact-point path";
   - contact kinematics, such as rolling contact and the Montana equations, alongside [14].
24. Conclusion, "holding the typical error near 10% up to 7 mm": also report the 9 mm figure (38%) in the abstract, so that readers see where the method stops working.
