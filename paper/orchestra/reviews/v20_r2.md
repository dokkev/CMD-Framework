THOUGHT:
I read this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methodology reviewer, so I focus mostly on Study 3 (the claim that keeping the contact Jacobian lets an interface be studied on its own) and on whether the statistics test what the hypotheses state. Studies 1 and 2 are geometric/optimization results, and the propositions (finite pole, clearance, coverage, fixed-axis bound) are elementary but correct and useful. H1 and H2 come with explicit failure criteria and pass them. Study 1's unequal search effort (CFL gets a neighbourhood refinement) is addressed: CFL leads without it, and its worst of six runs beats 2R-DD's best in 9 of 10 conditions. That is convincing.

Study 3 carries C2, the paper's more interesting claim. What I like: a falsifiable H3 with three reused-setpoint settings and four competing candidates; Spearman correlations with hand-bootstrap intervals, permutation tests and Holm correction; analyses labelled post hoc; partial correlations that control for the obvious mediators (setpoint force, the face's signed normal offset); a robustness check on contact stiffness (x10 either way), friction impedance (x3) and substeps (x2); an alternative scoring of hands that fail to hold; and an honest report of a failed prediction (the reversed share does not rise with Delta_contact). For a simulation study, the methodology is unusually careful.

My concerns are mostly about construct validity: does the simulation measure the physical effect the paper claims?

(1) The ground truth is a model counterfactual. The "true" interface effect is O's geometry with only the soft pad's stiffness. A physically thicker, softer pad changes its effective compliance, contact patch size and pressure distribution through its geometry. The paper models the pad as a lumped linear stiffness (2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m) inside MuJoCo's soft-contact model. That makes "pad thickness" mechanically inert apart from where the face sits, so pad thickness's weak correlation in Table I partly follows from how the model is built. The paper never says how a pad's stiffness is implemented (solref/solimp, a compliant prismatic layer, or something else), or whether contact is a point, a line or a patch for a cylinder on a flat pad.

(2) The mechanism is penetration at a reused position setpoint. The paper is clear that the error comes from over-closure: q0 + 5 degrees at stiffness K0, so a face that advances presses harder. The signed normal offset predicts the sign with rho = -0.99 (post hoc). Given that, the result that Delta_contact predicts the error under position/impedance reuse is close to what elementary contact mechanics predicts. The more informative finding is the partial correlation (0.64 [0.34, 0.86] with the setpoint force held fixed), and its interval is wide. Under the torque and touch protocols, the skeleton dominates instead (0.71, 0.88). So C2 is specific to the protocol. The paper says so, but the title and abstract read more generally.

(3) The sample is designed, and survivorship matters. The redesigns are NSGA-II front ends plus hands chosen "at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton most". Spearman rho over such a chosen sample (n = 14-45) depends on how the sample was chosen, and the hands are not exchangeable draws. Hands are dropped when they cannot hold (18 -> 15/14). E is a median over placements where both hands hold, which conditions on survival. The alternative scoring helps (0.81, 0.67), but the drop from 0.89 to 0.67 shows that the result is sensitive.

(4) E is a ratio of differences, |Delta(H) - Delta(O)| / |Delta(O)|. It can blow up when |Delta(O)| is small at some placements. The median partly guards against this, but the Stage 2 hardware plan inherits the problem with real noise. The plan uses 3 hands (R, M, U), 5 x 4 x 5 trials and a one-sided Page test, with no power analysis or noise propagation for E. Swapping covers on hardware also changes thickness and geometry, not only friction.

(5) "Pre-registered" in a purely computational study run by the same authors cannot be verified without a timestamped record. Still, holding out 27 new grid hands is a reasonable replication design.

(6) Smaller points. mu = 2.0 with an elliptic cone in MuJoCo is high. Slip is detected at 10 mm/s, and quasi-static loading assumes the 2 N/s ramp is slow enough. These are fine, but they are single choices. The robustness check reports only the R < U ordering, not the rho values.

Presentation is dense. Nearly every sentence carries a number, the Table I caption carries a methods paragraph, the Fig. 5 caption carries results that belong in the text, and Fig. 2's labels are hard to read. The writing is exact but makes the reader work.

Overall: the problem statement, hypotheses and methodology are fully developed, and the evidence goes well beyond what Stage 1 requires. The contact-mechanics validity issues are real, but they are limitations of a single-model simulation, which the paper acknowledges, rather than errors. The Stage 2 plan targets the right gap. I lean to accept with a 6.
