ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Hypotheses as I read them:
- H1: Suppose a finger is redesigned for a thicker contact interface and the original hand's command is reused (here a joint impedance setpoint). Then the error the redesign adds to a measured interface effect grows with the change in the distal contact surface's motion seen from the proximal pad (Δprox_distal), not with the change in skeleton/fingertip path (Δskel).
- H2: This is not only a preload effect. Δprox_distal still predicts the error once contact force is matched.
- H3: A policy learned on O keeps most of its performance on the redesign that preserves the motion its task uses.
- Design-side claims: (i) a pole analysis (Eqs. 1–4) shows a fixed axis cannot both make room for a thicker interface and keep the contact motion; (ii) a crossed four-bar (CFL) at the PIP places the pole near the contact faces, so it gives more coverage than at-joint, remote, and equal-roller RCJ joints, and keeps contact motion best.

Evidence for each:
- Theory: Eqs. 2–4 are correct and simple. Eq. 2 follows from c = p + R(c0 − p), so Δc = (I − R)Δp. Eq. 3 follows from the Δt frame offset, and the 3.46 mm/mm figure is 2·tan 60°. The analysis is tidy but elementary. Its main role is to show that the two quantities cannot both be kept, which motivates the comparison.
- H1: Isaac Lab (MuJoCo-Warp, point contact) pull-out on the full hand: four tests, Holm-corrected p < 0.001, partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with the skeleton held fixed, and ρ = 0.94 over 27 grid redesigns (stated as pre-registered). Drake repeats this with point and hydroelastic contact over four tasks (Table II). The relation weakens to ρ 0.43–0.53 for the dial and pivot. Table I shows the signed errors across interface changes and commands. This is the strongest part, and it is more than most Stage 1 abstracts show for their central hypothesis.
- H2: One partial correlation under an equal reused torque (partial ρ = 0.80), plus a re-referenced setpoint (42% → 14%, with the residual still following Δprox). This is supportive but thin.
- H3: Three PPO tasks, 7 mm only, mean of two training seeds. One task (relay dial with the fingertip end) goes against CoDe. It is declared "outside our claim" after the fact. The cube task is explained by a third quantity (proximal-pad shift against the palm, ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns). "The motion its task uses" is therefore picked per task after the results are known. As written, H3 is close to unfalsifiable.
- Design comparison: coverage +2.4 to +14.6 pp over at-joint in all 10 conditions where both are feasible. Remote matches CFL at 90° and up to 5 mm at 120°. Only equal-roller RCJ is compared. Remote-center-of-motion fingers built for thick skin [14, 16], the closest prior art, are not compared.

What my scores rest on:
- Value 2: The question of which kinematic property to preserve when the interface changes is legitimate and the framing is clean. But under a joint-space impedance setpoint, the force reaching the object is set by where the contact surface sits and how it moves relative to the commanded pose. That contact kinematics dominates contact outcomes is close to expected, and the "keep the skeleton" baseline reads partly as a straw man. A practitioner would re-reference the setpoint, and the authors show this already removes most of the error (42 → 14%). The scope is also narrow: planar finger, MCP fixed, PIP only, rigid pads, friction change as the main interface change, position-impedance commands only. With torque, force, or touch commands, all redesigns are within about ±15–30% (Table I).
- Feasibility 2: All evidence is simulated. The motivating interfaces (compliant, tactile, gel) never deform, and the conclusion admits that deformation moves the contact point, which could change the ordering. The reference D_O is a counterfactual in which O carries the new interface's properties on its own geometry. It is not clear that this counterfactual is physically realizable for the thickness-dependent effects (compliance, crowned interface), so the ground truth for those columns is a simulation construct.
- A key confound: CoDe "mounts shallower, so at 7 mm its surfaces sit only 1 mm deeper than O's". By Eq. 3, a 1 mm effective Δt lets a design keep both frames almost exactly. Much of CoDe's advantage may therefore come from mount depth, not from pole placement. It is unclear whether Skeleton and Coverage were allowed the same mount-depth/pivot-height freedom (F′).
- H3's evidence is two seeds per policy, with the motion metric chosen per task after the fact.
- The title and contribution 2 claim a robot hand, but no hand exists beyond CAD. The strongest competing submissions bring hardware.
- On the positive side, the pre-registered falsification criteria, the two simulators, and the preload control are good scientific practice. The paper would benefit greatly from a Stage 2, but as it stands the claimed-vs-shown gap and the narrow significance place it below the bar.
