CoDe Hand: Same Hand, Different Contact — Keep
How the Contact Surfaces Move, Not the Skeleton,
for Comparable Contact-Interface Studies
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review
Abstract—Contact interfaces such as thick compliant pads
and tactile modules decide how a robot hand touches an
object. To compare two interfaces, the finger must usually
be redesigned, because servos and joints occupy the space
an interface needs. The redesign changes the hand, so a
measured difference cannot be attributed to the interface alone.
We propose that a redesigned finger keep how its contact
surfaces move relative to each other, not its skeleton. In the
proximal-phalanx frame this motion is one column of the
finger’s contact Jacobian, and the path of a single point sets
it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns. We prove
that a fixed-axis joint keeps it only if its contact faces keep
their distance from the axis, and give a closed-form lower
bound on the change otherwise. A remote-actuated crossed
four-bar finger changes it least of four joint types, by 10–
51% of the fixed-axis bound, at near-best contact surface.
In simulation, when one flexion command or joint torque is
reused across redesigns for 5, 7 and 9 mm pads, the error
in measured friction, stiffness and shape effects follows the
change in contact-surface motion (Spearman ρ = 0.75–0.94).
The skeleton, coverage, thickness and transmission changes do
not predict it. Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand is
the next stage of this work.

I. Introduction
Contact is central to dexterous manipulation, and the
contact interface sets that contact. Compliant fingertips
change how an object is held [16, 12]. Vision-based
tactile sensors turn the contact patch into geometry and
force [19, 11]. Robot hands have become experimental
platforms [14, 15, 6], and most are built around servos
for maintainability. Servo bodies and joints occupy the
space an interface needs, above all around the joint. A
large or thick interface therefore costs range of motion or
contact surface, and carrying it is a redesign, not a module
swap [20, 4].
The redesign changes how the contact surfaces move
relative to each other. For a joint that turns about a
fixed axis this change is unavoidable when the pads sit
at their own depth. Interface studies often reuse the same
commands across hands: scripted grasps, frozen policies,
the same actuator targets. A measured difference then
mixes the interface with the hand’s change. One could
keep the skeleton instead, but the unchanged finger cannot
carry a thicker pad, and the object does not meet the
skeleton. It meets the contact surfaces.
In this work we propose CoDe (contact-centric design),
a servo-driven hand whose redesign target is how its
contact surfaces move relative to each other. Its servos

act remotely through a linkage, which frees the space
under the joints. Its PIP joint is a crossed four-bar linkage
(CFL), whose pole moves with flexion, so a thick pad
can sit under the joint. When a new interface asks for
a redesign, the finger is re-optimized to keep its contactsurface motion, not its skeleton motion (Fig. 1). This paper
analyses one planar finger of that hand. Each contribution
is a hypothesis with a stated way to fail:
C1 A bound. Finite-pole laws for the clearance, coverage
and contact-surface motion of planar finger joints. A
fixed axis keeps its contact-surface motion only if its
faces keep their distance from the axis, with a closedform lower bound otherwise (Sec. III).
C2 A finger. The CFL keeps more contact surface than a
servo-at-joint finger as pads thicken and the required
flexion deepens. It fails if both lose coverage alike
(Sec. IV-B).
C3 A redesign that keeps contact. Re-optimized for
thicker pads, the CFL keeps its contact-surface motion well inside the fixed-axis bound at near-best
coverage. It fails if it reaches the bound (Sec. IV-C).
C4 Why it matters. In simulation, with reused flexion
commands or joint torques, the error in measured
friction, stiffness and shape effects follows the change
in contact-surface motion, not the change in skeleton
motion. It fails if another design quantity predicts the
error as well (Sec. V).
Exchanging interfaces on one physical hand (C5) is the
next stage. The principle concerns grasps that use more
than one phalanx; in fingertip-only contact the object
meets the fingertip path. Keeping the contact-surface
motion does not keep the contact mechanics: those change
with the interface, and they are what an interface study
measures.
II. Related Work
Contact interfaces. Vision-based tactile sensors need
an elastomer, a camera and optics behind the contact
surface [19, 11]. Fingers are therefore designed around
them: GelSight Svelte shapes a finger around one camera [20], and a hand has been built to carry large-area
tactile sensing [4]. Thin skins can be replaced without
redesign [3], but thick interfaces come with their own
finger, so comparing them compares fingers.

Finger joints. Open, low-cost hands use fixed-axis servo
joints [15, 6]. Rolling contact joints are compact [9, 17].
Crossed four-bar joints give polycentric finger motion [2,
1], and linkage-driven hands integrate the drive [10]. None
of these is analysed for what it takes from the pads, or for
whether its contact-surface motion survives an interface
change. Co-design methods let the morphology change
freely for a task [18, 8]. We instead hold the contact-surface
motion fixed, so that a difference can be attributed to the
interface.
III. What Is Kept: The Pole of the Contact Surfaces

IV. Keeping the Contact-Surface Motion by Design
A. Architectures and setup
We compare four PIP joints. 2R-DD is a revolute joint
with the actuator at the joint. 2R-remote is the same joint,
belt-driven from an actuator on the proximal phalanx.
RCJ is a rolling contact joint of two equal rollers [9]. CFL
is CoDe’s crossed four-bar [2], driven through its proximal
pivot. All share one goal: Lf = 90 mm, the same actuators,
pad requirements and collision rules. CFL ideal and RCJ
ideal omit the joint actuator’s body and serve as upper
bounds. Mechanical advantage is not constrained.
Pads are 3, 5, 7 or 9 mm thick, and qreq ∈
{90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ }. Study 1 maximizes C̄pad over all design
parameters with a genetic algorithm and NSGA-II [7],
three seeds each, and refines CFL from neighbouring
designs. On 51 designs, coverage computed from pole path
and pads matches the evaluator within 10−13 . On the
CFL designs, Eq. (4) gives the joint gap within 0.05 mm
(median).
B. The CFL keeps more contact surface (Study 1)
Fig. 3 shows the best coverage per condition. CFL
exceeds 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both have
a design, by 2.4 to 14.6 points, and also without the
refinement. The spread grows with thickness and flexion.
From 3 to 9 mm, CFL loses 0.085–0.096 of C̄pad . 2Rremote loses 0.182 at 120◦ , and at 150◦ its 7 and 9 mm
designs cannot hold a cylinder. On the cylinder measure,
2R-remote leads at 90◦ , the two cross between 5 and
7 mm at 120◦ , and CFL leads at 150◦ . Eq. (5) explains
the split: CFL’s palmar pole lengthens the contour, while
2R-remote’s gap shortens when flexed. Measured as pad
length over the fixed Lf , CFL leads 2R-remote in every
condition beyond 3 mm.
C. The CFL keeps its contact-surface motion (Study 2)
From each architecture’s 3 mm Study 1 design M0 , we
minimize ∆contact and maximize C̄pad for 5, 7 and 9 mm
pads with NSGA-II. We report the coverage end of each
front, which gives up no coverage (Fig. 4). CFL and 2Rremote keep their mount depth, so the dotted line is
Eq. (8) with the faces moved by ∆h. 2R-DD falls below
it because its servo body already holds its proximal face

deep, so a thicker pad barely moves that face; it pays
for this reserve in coverage, trailing the CFL in every
condition. RCJ changes its roller radius, which moves
its pole. Spacers, which keep ∆contact = 0 trivially, are
excluded: they fill the interface’s space with a passive block
and cost thinner pads 8.6–18.2 points of coverage.
2R-remote meets the bound exactly at 90◦ and 120◦ .
CFL changes the contact-surface motion least among the
physical joints in every cell: by 0.35–4.31 mm, or 10–51% of
the bound. Its C̄pad stays within 0.2 points of Study 1’s
best. The other joints cannot close the gap by trading
coverage: giving up 2 points along their fronts, 2R-DD
and RCJ stay above the CFL in every cell where they
have a design. CFL absorbs the thicker pad by changing
its skeleton motion and its transmission, which control
compensates (flexion held within 0.07◦ ).
V. Why the Contact-Surface Motion Matters
Studies 1 and 2 show that the contact-surface motion
can be kept. We now ask why it should be kept, rather than
the skeleton or another property of the hand. Interface
studies compare hands under one protocol. Closing on
touch or force would use signals that the interface itself
changes, so the clean protocol reuses one set of commands,
as scripted grasps and joint-space policies do. We reuse the
flexion command through each hand’s own transmission
table, which undoes the transmission change (Sec. III).
We then test five candidate invariants, each of which a
designer might hold: the skeleton motion, the coverage, the
transmission, the pad thickness and the contact-surface
motion.
Setup. We simulate the CoDe hand model’s three CFL
fingers (qreq = 120◦ , MCP held) in Isaac Lab 3 with
Newton’s MuJoCo-Warp solver, at one flexion stiffness,
K0 = 5 N m/rad. A cylinder of radius 15 or 20 mm rests
on each hand’s proximal pad, free in the sagittal plane,
at the place along the face where the reference O (3 mm
pads) touches it with both pads at q0 (q0 = 20◦ to 120◦ in
5◦ steps); an object resting on a pad can only be placed
relative to that pad’s face. The PIP closes to q0 + 5◦ ,
the cylinder is released, and a load rises in one of four
directions until the cylinder slips (10 mm/s), loses a pad or
collapses the grasp. Three interface changes are measured:
pad friction (µ from 1.3 to 2.0), pad material stiffness
(contact stiffness from 2.5 to 1.0 kN/m) and pad shape
(flat to 2 mm crowned; ρ = 0.94 with ∆contact over six
hands). For a hand H, ∆(H) is the change of the limit
load, and the error is E(H) = |∆(H) − ∆(O)|/|∆(O)|,
the median over placements and directions. All thresholds
were fixed before the runs. The 18 redesigns for 3 → 5,
7 and 9 mm include, per thickness, R, which keeps the
contact-surface motion, U , redesigned for coverage alone,
and redesigns at set ∆contact that keep or change the
skeleton as much as possible. The unchanged finger with
thicker pads collides at 47◦ .

and the redesigns whose distal face retreats 1.5–2.6 mm
cannot hold the cylinder. Reusing O’s joint torque instead removes this over-closure: every hand then presses
within 0.89–1.26× O’s force. The error falls to 3–40%,
and ∆contact is still the only candidate that predicts it

protocol, R errs about half as much as U at each thickness
(5, 17, 12% against 9, 28, 23%): where the faces meet
the object matters at equal force too. Closing each finger
from its own touch, or matching the contact force, leaves
a median of 20% or 3–7%. Both need sensing that the
interface itself changes.
Robustness. R errs less than U at 20–24 of the 22–
24 paired placements under a tenfold change of contact
stiffness, the solver’s impedance ratio scaled by 0.3 and 3,
and doubled substeps.
VI. Conclusion and Next Stage
A redesigned finger should keep how its contact surfaces
move, not its skeleton. A crossed four-bar does so at nearbest contact surface, and in simulation this change, not the
skeleton’s, predicts how much a reused command distorts
a measured interface effect.
Limitations and future work. Our evidence is computational, with pads represented by a contact model; Stage
2 repeats it on hardware. Extending the principle to the
MCP calls for a polycentric MCP, and thicker camerabased tactile modules are the next interfaces.
Stage 2. One CoDe finger with exchangeable pads and
CFL links gives O, R and U . We will record what each
exchange needs (C5), track both faces to measure ∆contact
(C3), and repeat the grasp-and-pull test with two pad
surfaces (C4).
