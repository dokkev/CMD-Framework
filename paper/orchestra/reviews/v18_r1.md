THOUGHT:
I read this as someone who has built servo-chain hands and swapped pads and tactile skins on them. The paper makes two claims. C1: remote servos plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leave more room for proximal and distal pads than a servo-at-joint PIP. C2: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface, the thing to keep is the contact-surface motion c(q) in the proximal frame (the PIP column of the contact Jacobian), not the skeleton or fingertip path. If you keep it, the measured interface effect is close to what the original hand would show.

C1 is believable and the pole-based analysis (Prop. 1-2, Eq. 2) explains it well. A fixed axis's joint gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of face depth, while a virtual pole can follow the face down. The 2R-remote ablation is the most useful part of Study 1 for a designer: remote actuation alone is enough at 90 deg, and the polycentric joint is what pays off at 120-150 deg. The catch is that mechanical advantage is not constrained. The CFL optima have worst-case MA 0.41-0.58, which roughly halves PIP torque from the same servo. On the Dynamixel-class servos these hands use, that is a large cost, so the comparison with 2R-DD (MA 1) is not like-for-like. The limitations mention it, but it touches the headline of C1 and should be a constraint or a Pareto axis.

C2 is the more interesting claim. The mechanism is familiar to anyone who has reused joint position targets after changing a pad: a face moved toward the object over-closes and squeezes harder; a face moved away squeezes less. So Delta_contact predicting the error under a reused setpoint is close to expected. The real content is (a) Delta_skel does not predict it, and keeping the skeleton exactly gives 95-228% error, a clear negative result; (b) the closed-form fixed-axis bound (Eq. 8), with the CFL at 10-51% of it; (c) the reversal example (Fig. 1d), which is persuasive. Table I also shows the principle is protocol-dependent. Closing from own touch, Delta_skel (0.88) beats Delta_contact (0.44); under an ideal reused torque they tie (0.71 vs 0.69). So C2 holds for a reused position-impedance setpoint, and the abstract and C2 wording read more generally than that. The authors honestly report that the reversal share did not rise with Delta_contact as predicted; this weakens the reversal story as a Delta_contact effect.

Statistics: candidates are correlated by construction (thicker pad, larger Delta_contact, lower coverage). Redesigns at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton help, but 14-15 hands per main setting is small. A joint model or partial correlations for all candidates would support "more than any other candidate" better than marginal rankings. The pre-registered 27-hand grid is a good touch.

Fidelity: pads are a linear contact model (1-2.5 kN/m). Real 7-9 mm compliant pads are nonlinear and viscoelastic and wrap the object, so the effective face depth depends on load, which is exactly where "where the face sits" gets fuzzy. A real CFL also brings backlash and link compliance that would enter the measured effect. The Stage 2 protocol is concrete and falsifiable, but O plus three redesigns is a thin trend test, and bare vs textured covers test friction, not the stiffness change behind Fig. 1d.

Scope: spacers give Delta_contact = 0 at 8.6-18.2 points of coverage, and recomputing setpoints from the model fixes the error in software. The argument that policies and scripted grasps reuse targets is fair for zero-shot transfer, but it narrows the audience; say so.

Presentation is dense: many symbols arrive fast, and Figs. 3-5 are too small (Fig. 4 axis text barely legible). For Stage 1, the problem, hypotheses with failure criteria, theory, preliminary results and Stage 2 protocol are all present. The main issues can be fixed or scoped. I lean accept at 6.
