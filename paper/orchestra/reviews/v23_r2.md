ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper makes two claims about a servo-driven finger with a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint. C1: remote actuation plus a CFL joint keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion (H1, Study 1). C2: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface and the impedance setpoint is reused, the error in the measured interface effect follows the change in contact-surface motion (Delta_contact, the PIP column of the contact Jacobian), not the change in skeleton motion. A fixed axis cannot keep that motion (closed-form bound, Corollary 1), and the CFL comes within 10-51% of the bound (H2, Study 2; H3, Study 3). Everything is in simulation. A physical Stage 2 protocol is given with falsification criteria.

How strong the evidence is, claim by claim:
- C1/H1 is well supported for its scope. CFL beats 2R-DD in 10 of 10 conditions by 2.4-14.6 points. The worst of six CFL runs beats the best 2R-DD run in 9 of 10, which is about as strong a robustness check as an optimizer comparison can get with 3 seeds. The 2R-remote ablation splits "remote actuation" from "polycentric joint" cleanly. Props 1-2 explain the trend, and the authors say plainly that designs are judged by exact clearance, not by the symmetric law. One caveat is that coverage is a geometric proxy (an inscribed cylinder in the V). It says nothing about whether the extra coverage gives better grasps. Mechanical advantage is left free (0.41-0.58 against 1 at the joint), so the comparison is not iso-performance. This is acknowledged.
- H2 is supported. Lemma 1, Eq. (6) and Corollary 1 are correct and elementary (|I - R_q| = 2 sin(q/2)). The bound only holds with c0 kept, and Fig. 4's caption has to explain that 2R-DD and RCJ can fall below it by moving the contact point along the face. Without that offset the CFL is least in 7 of 9 cells. So "least in every cell" depends on how the comparison is normalised.
- H3 is the core, and the part my perspective scrutinises most. Strengths: the redesigned hands are built to decouple Delta_contact from Delta_skel (set Delta_contact, keep or change the skeleton most). Pre-registered tests are separated from post-hoc ones. Hand-bootstrap intervals, permutation tests and Holm correction are reported. The non-holding hands are handled both by exclusion and by being scored as the worst errors. Robustness is checked across 100x setpoint stiffness, 10x contact stiffness, 3x friction impedance and doubled substeps. A falsified prediction is reported honestly: the reversed share does not rise with Delta_contact. The partial correlations with setpoint force held fixed (0.64) and with the face's normal offset held fixed (0.69) address the obvious "it is just overclosure" alternative. That is good practice.
- My main reservations on H3 concern the contact model and what "true" means. (i) Pads are meshes in point contact, and pad stiffness is the contact's normal stiffness. In that model a pad's thickness enters only through where its face sits. So the headline "where the faces sit, not how thick the pad is" is partly built into the simulator. For the compliant pads that motivate the paper, thickness also changes the contact patch, conformity, torsional friction and enveloping. None of these exist in the model. (ii) The "true" effect of the softer 7 mm pad is a counterfactual (O's 3 mm geometry with the soft stiffness) that only a point-contact simulator can build. That is clever, but the -37%/-41% "true" loss is itself an artefact of the regime: under a reused overclosure, a softer normal stiffness gives a lower normal force and so a lower friction-limited hold. With real patch mechanics a softer, thicker pad often holds more. The "reverses a conclusion" example (48% more vs 41% less) is therefore a statement about the model, and should be framed that way. (iii) The mechanism is close to tautological. With the joint target reused, the squeeze force is K0 times the geometric overclosure, and Delta_contact is a geometric overclosure measure. The partial correlations help, but sizeable residual partials (0.64-0.69) are still expected from a purely geometric account. (iv) The samples are small and designed: 14-15 hands for the two O's-contacts settings, and within-thickness intervals of [0.41, 0.99] and [0.35, 0.98]. The bootstrap treats designed hands as exchangeable draws, which they are not. The 20% tolerance that defines "independent up to about 7 mm" is arbitrary. An interface effect smaller than about 20% of O's would be swamped by it.
- The Stage 2 protocol is concrete and falsifiable (Page trend test, 5 placements x 4 directions x 5 repeats, bare vs textured covers), and it sensibly changes only friction, which avoids the stiffness counterfactual. But it has only four hands, and Delta_contact and Delta_skel are perfectly anti-ordered by design. It does not say how sub-millimetre Delta_contact (0.9 mm for R) will be measured with markers against link tolerances and servo backlash.

Presentation: the argument is tight and the figures carry it, but the text is very dense. Key statistics live in captions (Table I, Fig. 5). Labels such as O/R/U/M, "grid", "placements" and "O's contacts" have to be decoded from compressed sentences. Fig. 1's "3.9x/0.7x" (one placement) and the text's "3.3x/0.75x" (median) are easy to mix up.

My scores rest on this. The Stage 1 requirements (problem, hypotheses with failure criteria, methodology, insights under negative results) are fully met. The geometric results are sound. H3 is well designed statistically but rests on a point-contact model that cannot represent the pad mechanics the paper is about. The physical Stage 2 study is therefore essential rather than confirmatory. I lean accept at 6.
