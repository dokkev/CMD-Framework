THOUGHT:
I read this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methodology reviewer. The submission is a Stage-1 extended abstract, so my bar is: are the problem, hypotheses and methods complete, are the preliminary results sound for what they claim, and is the Stage-2 plan able to produce insight even if it fails.

What the paper is. Two claims about a planar two-DoF finger. C1: remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger; the finite pole's depth relative to the pad face sets the joint gap (Prop. 1, Eq. 2) and the coverage (Prop. 2). C2: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker pad, what should be preserved is the path of the distal contact face in the proximal-face frame (Delta_contact), not the fingertip path (Delta_skel). Prop. 3 / Cor. 1 give a fixed-axis lower bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Dh - Dd|; Study 2 shows the CFL reaches 10-51% of it; Study 3 shows the error in a measured interface effect rank-correlates with Delta_contact (Spearman 0.75-0.94); Study 4 shows a sign reversal (U: +48% measured against -41% true at 7 mm).

Checks I ran. The numbers are internally consistent: 2 tan(q/2) = 2.0, 3.46, 7.46 mm/mm at 90, 120, 150 deg; Fig. 2's 8.7 = 5 tan 60, 15.6 = 9 tan 60, 6.9 = 4 tan 60; the contrast family's sqrt(3) Dh equals 2 sin(60 deg) Dh; p <= 0.0006 does clear 0.05/25 = 0.002; the abstract's "48% more / 41% less" matches the 7 mm row of Study 4. The geometry is elementary but correct and neatly organised around one quantity. Page budget is respected (content ends on p. 5, references on p. 6).

Where I am not convinced, in order of importance.

(1) The central empirical finding is close to built into the protocol. All hands share one contact model and the pad thickness enters the simulation only as geometry (plus k proportional to 1/h in Study 4). The finger is closed by a position command to q0 + 5 deg against K0 = 5 N m/rad, and q0 is defined on the reference O. A face displaced by Delta_contact then meets the cylinder at a different angle, so the preload changes roughly as K0 * (face shift / lever). With a 5 deg over-closure (about 0.44 N m), a 1 mm face shift at a 40 mm lever is about 1.4 deg, i.e. a ~30% change in grip force, and limit load under Coulomb friction scales with grip force. The paper says this itself ("Delta_contact sets where the faces meet the object and so the force", deviation-vs-error rho = 0.94). So under the reused command, Study 3 mostly measures a preload artefact that follows from kinematics; the correlation is a consistency check on the simulation more than a discovery. The authors deserve credit for the two controls: reused torque (error drops to 3-40%, rho = 0.75) and matched contact force (3-7%, "near the load resolution"). But those controls cut the other way for significance: a careful interface experiment normalises grip force (or closes to a current/force threshold), and then the residual is at the noise floor. The paper needs to argue which protocols in the literature actually reuse joint position targets across hands with different pads, and to be explicit that the principle is a remedy for position-reuse protocols, not for interface studies in general. The torque-reuse result (partial 0.80 with force fixed, post hoc, CI [0.37, 0.95], n = 16) is the scientifically more interesting one and is the least supported.

(2) The "true" effect in Study 4 is a modelling choice that favours the hypothesis. The reference is O carrying the soft pad "without a redesign". A 7 or 9 mm pad cannot sit on O without moving its faces, so I assume the counterfactual keeps O's 3 mm geometry and changes only stiffness. Then the ground truth is by definition "geometry of O's contact faces + new stiffness", and R, selected to have the smallest Delta_contact, is the hand closest to that definition by construction. A reader could equally define the true effect of a thicker pad as including the change in reach and contact location that thickness necessarily brings. The reversal itself (U presses harder, so a softer pad appears to hold more) is again the preload route. Also, the text reports R at 5 and 7 mm (-26, -55%) but not 9 mm; in Fig. 1d the 9 mm R bar looks like roughly -15% against a true -51%, a large error for the hand that is supposed to be faithful. If I read the figure correctly, this should be stated in the text, not left to the plot. Even at 7 mm, R's -55 against -41 is a 34% relative error.

(3) Contact model fidelity. The pads are a linear contact stiffness (2.5 kN/m) with Coulomb friction mu = 1.3 to 2.0 in MuJoCo-Warp. What makes thick compliant pads matter physically - contact-patch growth with load, the stiffening of a bonded elastomer layer as a/h grows, load-dependent friction of elastomers, torsional and moment resistance of the patch, viscoelastic relaxation - is absent. "Softer, thicker pad" is a spring constant scaled by 3/h. The robustness sweep (tenfold contact stiffness, impedance ratio, substeps) tests numerical sensitivity, not model form. This is acceptable for Stage 1 if stated, but the limitation sentence ("pads as a contact model") is too terse for a paper whose headline number is a sign reversal of a holding load. Simulation details that decide a limit-load measurement are missing: friction cone type, number of contact points on a cylinder-flat line contact, load ramp rate and step (the "load resolution"), the slip criterion, and whether loading is quasi-static.

(4) Statistics and design of the correlational study. Good practice is visible: thresholds fixed before runs, post hoc analyses labelled, bootstrap over hands rather than trials, Holm correction, failure criteria per hypothesis. But: (a) n = 14-16 hands for four of five columns, with intervals like [0.33, 0.99]; (b) the hands are not a sample but a constructed set ("redesigns at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton most"), and Eq. 7 forces Delta_contact and Delta_skel to trade off, so the negative rho for skeleton (-0.40 to -0.51) is partly structural; (c) Delta_contact scales with Dh, so thickness is collinear with it - the "holds at each thickness" statement comes with one partial coefficient and no per-thickness n or interval; (d) the claim "more than any of four other design quantities" is supported by "its interval excludes zero and theirs do not", which is not a test of a difference between dependent correlations; in the torque column thickness (0.50) and coverage (-0.52) are not obviously distinguishable from 0.75 at n = 16; (e) hands enter only if they "hold the cylinder" (15, 14, 14, 16 of 18), and E is a median over the placements and directions where both hands hold - both condition on the outcome, with a hand-dependent subset. The 45-hand fixed grid is the right fix and is the strongest evidence in the paper, but it is at one thickness (7 mm) and one interface change (friction); (f) E is an unsigned ratio with |Delta(O)| in the denominator and the size of Delta(O) is never reported, so I cannot judge whether 20% or 120% error is large in newtons.

(5) Stage-2 plan. One physical finger, three redesigns R, M, U, each built once, tested by a Page trend test over 5 placements x 4 directions x 5 repeats. The repeats and placements are pseudo-replicates of a single specimen per level of Delta_contact; hand-level nuisance (pad casting, link tolerances, backlash in the four-bar, belt/servo compliance) is fully confounded with the factor of interest, and with three ordered levels a monotone ordering has a 1/6 chance from specimen effects alone. The protocol tests only the reused-command route, where the outcome is nearly guaranteed by preload; it does not measure grip force, so it cannot separate the preload route from the "geometry itself" route that the paper claims at equal torque. The venue asks for the insight to be gained under negative results; the paper states falsifiers but not what would be concluded if they trigger (model form? protocol? principle?). The C1 hardware criterion ("fails if an exchange needs the articulation taken apart") is a serviceability check, not a test of coverage.

(6) C1 is narrower than the framing. H1 compares against 2R-DD, the baseline that obviously loses room to a servo body. The informative competitor is 2R-remote, which beats the CFL at 90 deg at every thickness and at 3-5 mm at 120 deg (Fig. 3). The CFL's advantage is deep flexion with thick pads, which is an honest but smaller claim than "at every pad thickness and required flexion". Coverage C_pad is a geometric proxy (V-inscribed cylinder) never linked to a grasp outcome; in Table I it is, if anything, unrelated to the measured quantities. Mechanical advantage (0.41-0.58 worst case), link strength, pivot count, lateral stiffness and backlash of a crossed four-bar under grasp loads are unconstrained.

(7) Terminology and clarity. What is kept is a position path c(q) of one face point under the PIP, measured as a max deviation in mm. That is not a Jacobian; the PIP column follows from the pole path, the MCP column is explicitly not kept, and the paper says the contact mechanics are not kept either. "Keep the contact Jacobian" in the title overstates. The paper is extremely dense: "impedance ratio", "start offset", "lever to the contact", "grid placements", "refines CFL from neighbouring designs", "degenerate designs (0.21, 0)" are used without definition, and Sec. V-B packs about a dozen statistics into each paragraph. Fig. 5 is too small to read the marker classes.

Decision reasoning. For Stage 1 the hypotheses are falsifiable and the methods are largely specified, the geometry is correct and useful, and the authors run more controls than most simulation papers. The main risk is that C2's empirical content reduces to "position-controlled grasps with a displaced face have a different preload", which force normalisation removes. I think this is fixable by reframing and by a Stage-2 protocol that measures grip force and replicates specimens. I land at a 6: above the bar for a Stage-1 abstract, conditional on those changes, with the significance of C2 as the open question.
