Same Motion, Different Contact: Design of a
Contact-Centric Robot Hand
Author Names Omitted for Anonymous Review

I. Introduction
As robots are expected to manipulate everyday objects,
the contact interfaces of their fingers shape both how
forces are transmitted and how contact is sensed: a
compliant pad conforms and spreads the force, and a
tactile skin reports the contact [9, 26, 15, 30]. Fingertip
modules swap readily, since the distal phalanx has a
joint on one side only [19, 5], but many grasps rest
on the phalanges and the joints between them [6, 11],
where adapting the hand to the interface changes its
embodiment. This is more than an integration cost: it
decides whether a measured benefit of an interface belongs
to the interface at all, and whether a command or policy
learned on one hand still means the same on the next.
The obstacle is the coupling between the interface’s
geometry and the finger’s kinematics. Servos at the joints
of accessible hand platforms [25, 1, 8] take the room
an interface needs, so each new interface needs custom
integration [31, 4], and a fixed axis trades interface
thickness against coverage and range of motion. So a basic
question stands unanswered: when the interface changes,
which kinematic property must stay unchanged? The
natural answer is to keep the skeleton, the joint axes and
the fingertip path. It is wrong: under the original hand’s
command, a redesign that keeps the skeleton almost
exactly departs from the original hand’s response to a new
interface as far as one that ignores kinematics altogether
(Sec. IV). Interface comparisons across redesigned fingers,

Abstract—Many research hands for dexterous manipulation
place a servo at every joint, which leaves little room for
a contact interface between the joints. When a finger is
redesigned for a thicker contact interface, the natural choice is
to keep its skeleton, the joint axes and the fingertip path. We
show that this is the wrong thing to keep, and propose contactcentric design: move the servos off the joints and redesign
the finger so that its contact surfaces, not its skeleton, keep
their motion. A finite-displacement pole analysis shows that
a fixed axis cannot keep this motion and that a crossed fourbar linkage keeps most of it. In simulation, when a redesign
reuses the original hand’s impedance setpoint, its departure
from the original hand’s response to a new interface grows
with the change in contact-surface motion (ρ = 0.94 over 27
redesigns, pre-registered), not with the change in skeleton,
in two simulators and two contact models. A redesign for
coverage alone even reverses an interface’s effect on pull-out
load. Policies learned on the original hand carry over to the
redesign that keeps the motion their task uses.

Our insight is that a finger should preserve how its
contact surfaces move, its contact kinematics [21], not how
its skeleton moves (Fig. 1(c)). Manipulation commands
are commonly joint targets executed through low-level
impedance control [25, 23]; if the surfaces move differently,
the same command and gains press the object with
different forces. Both the room between the surfaces and
their relative motion are set by where the pole, the point
about which the distal phalanx rotates relative to the
proximal one, lies relative to them, so a joint that places
its pole by design, apart from the joint hardware, can fit a
new interface and keep that motion. We call this Contactcentric Design (CoDe). The CoDe Hand (Fig. 1(a)) is

II. Related Work
Prior hands make room for interfaces or articulate
compactly: tendon and linkage drives take the actuators
off the joints [8, 18], rolling-contact and crossed fourbar joints give polycentric articulation [17, 27, 3, 2], and
remote-center-of-motion fingers carry thick skin across
their joints [14, 16]. They accommodate an interface but
do not ask what the finger’s motion must keep when it
changes. Where the hand and its use are coupled, past
work adapts the other side: co-design reshapes the hand

axis; a linkage’s pole can lie outside the structure and
move with q.

C. The CFL against three other joints
Four PIP articulations share one finger length and one
actuator (Fig. 3(a)): a revolute joint with the servo at the
joint (at-joint), the same joint belt-driven from a servo on
the proximal phalanx (remote, which separates the benefit
of moving the actuator from that of the articulation), an

RCJ with equal rollers [17], and the CFL [3]. Each architecture’s coverage optimum (Eq. (7)) is its own reference,
for 3–9 mm interfaces and qreq ∈ {90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ }; both
problems are solved by evolutionary search [10].
The CFL fits thicker interfaces than a servo-at-joint
finger. It covers more in all 10 conditions where both are
feasible, by 2.4–14.6 percentage points (Fig. 3(a)), because
its pole sits near the contact faces. Moving the servo alone
(remote) does as well at 90◦ and up to 5 mm at 120◦ , where
an axis’s gap is still small; at 150◦ its 7 and 9 mm designs
cannot hold a cylinder between both pads.
The CFL also keeps the most contact motion. It keeps
its contact-surface motion best of the four joints in all
nine conditions (Fig. 3(c)), because its pole can be placed
by the deeper surfaces.
IV. Experiment
A. Setup
The experiment asks what happens to an interface
study when the finger must be redesigned to carry the
new interface. We take the original hand O, a crossed
four-bar finger with 3 mm interfaces, run its command
unchanged on redesigns for 5, 7 and 9 mm, and measure
the interface’s effect on each. Three redesigns frame
the comparison: CoDe (Sec. III-C); Coverage, the same
mechanism optimized for coverage alone (Eq. (7)); and
Skeleton, which minimizes ∆skel instead of ∆prox
distal at
similar coverage. Further redesigns, held to a prescribed
∆prox
distal while ∆skel varies, pull the two apart and form the
correlation set; CoDe and Skeleton are scored outside it.
All use qreq = 120◦ .
The command is O’s impedance setpoint, a target
flexion held at a joint stiffness. Each hand runs it on the
same placements twice, with the original interface and
with a changed one (friction 1.3 → 2.0 unless stated),
and the change in outcome, DH , is the interface’s effect
as hand H measures it. On O this is the interface’s own
effect, because O’s kinematics do not change (O carries
the new interface’s contact stiffness on its own geometry).

next question is seen from where, and the answer depends
on what holds the object. With each Drake object placed

When the interface changes and a command is reused,
a finger should keep how its contact surfaces move, not
its skeleton. Placing the pole by design makes room for
thick interfaces and keeps that motion. Under a reused
command, the error follows the contact surfaces, and a
learned policy keeps what its task touches. The evidence is
simulated, and no interface deforms; a deforming interface
also moves the contact point. The finger is planar, the
MCP fixed, only the PIP redesigned. Contact at the
fingertip’s end follows the skeleton instead. The error
grows at 9 mm (38%). Next, built CoDe, Skeleton and
Coverage fingers with real 3 and 7 mm pads will repeat
the pull-out and the cube transfer on hardware, and
a deforming-pad model will test whether the ordering
survives contact migration within the pad.
