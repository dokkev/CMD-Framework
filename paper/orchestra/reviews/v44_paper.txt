I. Introduction
As robots are expected to manipulate everyday objects,
the contact interfaces of their fingers matter as much as
the mechanism: a compliant pad conforms and spreads the
force, and a tactile skin reports the contact [9, 23, 14, 26].
Servo-driven platforms have made learning-based dexterous manipulation accessible [22, 1, 8], but a new interface
on them needs custom integration for each pairing, such
as a whole-finger camera sensor [27] or a hand built
around large-area tactile skin [4]; fingertip modules swap
readily [18, 5], but many grasps rest on the phalanges
and the joints between them [6, 11]. Adapting the hand
to the interface instead changes its embodiment, so the
interface’s own effect can no longer be isolated.
The obstacle is the coupling between the interface’s
geometry and the finger’s kinematics. Servos at the joints
take the room an interface needs; without them, a fixed
axis still trades interface thickness against coverage and
range of motion; and remote-center-of-motion joints keep
a continuous skin [13, 15] but not the same kinematics
across interfaces. Which kinematic property should stay
unchanged when the interface changes, and how can a
finger keep it?
Our insight is that a finger should preserve how its
contact surfaces move, its contact Jacobian, not how its
skeleton moves (Fig. 1(c)). Learned policies command

Abstract—Many research hands for dexterous manipulation
place a servo at every joint. Such a hand has room for a
contact interface only at the fingertip, and redesigning its
finger for a thicker interface changes how the finger moves,
so the interface’s own effect cannot be measured. We propose
contact-centric design: move the servo off the joint, and when
the interface changes, redesign the finger so that its contact
surfaces keep their motion while the skeleton is free to change.
A finite-displacement pole analysis shows that a fixed axis
above the pad’s mount cannot keep that motion from the
same initial contact point, and that a crossed four-bar can.
In design studies, the resulting finger keeps more contact
surface than a servo-at-joint finger wherever both are feasible.
In simulation, a redesign that reuses the original impedance
setpoint misreports a pad’s effect by an amount that tracks how
far its contact surfaces move differently, not its skeleton, across
two simulators, two contact models and four tasks. This holds
with the contact-force difference held fixed or the setpoint rereferenced, so it is not only a change of preload. In simulation, a
redesign that ignores it reports that a more compliant, thicker
pad holds 48% more, when it holds 41% less.

II. Related Work
Tendon-driven hands free the joints of actuators [8],
rolling contact joints [16, 24], crossed four-bar joints [3, 2]
and linkage-driven hands [17] give compact or polycentric
articulation, and remote-center-of-motion fingers carry
thick skin across their joints [13, 15]. None has been
analyzed for whether its contact-surface motion survives
a change of interface. Co-design lets the morphology
change freely for a task [25, 12], whereas contact-centric
design holds the contact-surface motion fixed, so that the
interface remains the variable.
III. Design
A finger for exchangeable interfaces needs room for
thicker pads and, once redesigned, contact surfaces that
keep their motion, so that the same command presses the
same way. Both are set by the pole: no redesign keeps both
contact and skeleton motion, and a fixed axis cannot keep
the contact motion.

B. Finger Architecture
The MCP joint is directly actuated; the PIP servo sits
on the proximal phalanx and drives the crossed fourbar, leaving the space beneath the PIP for interfaces
(Fig. 2(b)). Pivots A, A′ are fixed in the proximal frame
F1 (origin at the MCP axis O, x along the link line) and B,
B ′ in the distal one; links A′ B (driven) and AB ′ cross, and
the distal pose follows from loop closure [19]. Each contact
interface is a module of thickness hi (a requirement) and of
free length and position, mounted at the shallowest depth
the structure allows, without spacers, and exchangeable
without disassembling the joint. The finger is planar, its
MCP held at zero to isolate the PIP, and every design
keeps a prescribed extended length Lf .

Four PIP articulations share a 90 mm finger and one
actuator (Fig. 3(a)): a revolute joint with the servo at the

Pads of 3, 5, 7 and 9 mm and qreq ∈ {90◦ , 120◦ , 150◦ }
are considered. Eq. (8) is solved by a genetic algorithm
and NSGA-II [10] (three seeds of each, population 200,
up to 1000 generations, the best of the six runs kept; the
CoDe runs spread by 1.3–3.4 points and are refined from
neighbouring designs), and Eq. (9) by NSGA-II from those
designs.
1) Room: CoDe covers more than the at-joint finger
in all 10 conditions where both are feasible, by 2.4–14.6
points (Fig. 3(a)). Remote actuation alone does as well at
90◦ and up to 5 mm at 120◦ , but at 150◦ its 7 and 9 mm
designs cannot hold a cylinder between both pads. The
worst-case mechanical advantage of CoDe is 0.41–0.58.
2) Preserved motion: CoDe preserves its contactsurface motion best of the four joints in all nine conditions
(Fig. 3(c)). Redesigned from its best 3 mm design for 5, 7

than every other front. The remote-servo joint sits on the

B. Results
1) The error follows the contact surfaces: The error
grows with ∆contact and with nothing else. In Isaac
Lab this holds in all four tests (friction, stiffness and a
reused torque over the 18 redesigns, friction over the 27
grid redesigns; one-sided permutation p < 0.001, Holmcorrected), also with ∆skel held fixed (partial ρ 0.86–0.91).
∆skel , coverage, thickness and transmission predict none.
Keeping the skeleton exactly does not help: a revolute

PIP with one axis for every pad (∆skel = 0) errs by 95–
228%. CoDe errs by 4, 10 and 38% at 5, 7 and 9 mm,
Coverage by 124–173%. The four Drake tasks keep this
ordering in both contact models (Fig. 4); Skeleton, which
keeps the fingertip but not the contact surfaces (Fig. 1(c)),
errs as much as Coverage or more (222% vs. 124%, Isaac
Lab pull-out, 7 mm). The fingertip tasks correlate least
(p ≤ 0.043 each, one-sided, uncorrected; neither survives a
correction over the four tasks). Across simulators, contact
models and pad changes (Table I, 7 mm pull-out), CoDe’s
median error is closer to zero than Coverage’s in eleven
of twelve settings, and its typical error E is smaller in
all twelve (torque: Coverage’s median 0%, E 28% against
11%). Swapping in the thicker, compliant pad itself, with
O as the reference carrying the pad’s compliance (its
contact stiffness) on its own 3 mm geometry, O holds 41%
less, CoDe 39% less and Coverage 48% more; over the
27 grid redesigns this error follows ∆contact (ρ = 0.94,
pre-registered).
2) Not only preload: Preload sets the sign of the error,
but the geometry matters beyond it. Under an equal
reused torque the contact forces stay within 0.84–1.45
times O’s, and ∆contact still predicts the error with the
force deviation held fixed (partial ρ = 0.80; 0.79 over
the 27 grid redesigns). Re-referencing the setpoint from
the kinematics, so that the fingertip contact point sits
where O’s does, matches the preload at that point and
cuts the median error of the 16 Drake redesigns from 42
to 14%, but what remains still follows ∆contact (ρ = 0.73,
p = 0.0007; ∆skel : 0.18), because the other contact points
still move differently. Matching the contact force itself
leaves 3–7% but needs force sensing at both pads; closing
at sensed touch leaves an error that follows the skeleton.

V. Conclusion
Remote actuation and a crossed four-bar PIP joint make
room for thick pads beyond the fingertip, and preserving
the contact-surface motion keeps the error a redesign adds
to an interface measurement near 10% up to 7 mm (38% at
9 mm) for a reused setpoint on the pads. The evidence is
simulated, with rigid pads, a planar finger and two policy
seeds, and the four-bar halves the worst-case mechanical
advantage. A hardware test of three 7 mm redesigns comes
next.
