ASSESSMENT NOTES:
The paper makes two linked claims about one planar finger of a servo-driven hand.

(1) Room: a remotely actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute joint (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. The analysis is good. Lemma 1 and Propositions 1-2 give closed-form clearance and coverage laws in terms of pole depth z(q), and they explain why a pole on the contact face is best (Fig. 2c). The optimization study (Fig. 3a-c) agrees: CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both are feasible, by 2.4-14.6 points. The claim is falsifiable and stated as such. The caveats are that coverage is the authors' own cylinder-based metric, only three GA/NSGA-II seeds were run per cell, and the 2R-DD baseline depends on one assumed servo package. The claim is believable, but its size depends on the baseline's geometry and on the metric.

(2) Redesign invariant: when a pad is thickened, the redesign should keep the contact-surface motion c(q) (the PIP column of the contact Jacobian), not the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot do this (Corollary 1 gives a lower bound), and the CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. The kinematic part (Prop. 3, Eqs. 7-9) is clean and correct as far as I can check. It is also fairly elementary: it follows directly from the pole description of planar relative motion. The empirical part is the core of the experiment. Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured friction or stiffness effect correlates with Δ_contact (rho 0.80-0.86, Holm-corrected, pre-registered). The result holds in a second simulator (Drake, point and hydroelastic contact) and in two more open-loop tasks. The pre-registration, the multiple-comparison correction, the partial correlations and the out-of-family "revolute with one axis" control (95-228% error) are better statistical practice than this venue usually sees.

My main reservation is mechanistic. The paper itself shows (Sec. IV-B1) that the error enters almost entirely through preload: E ≈ |N_H/N_O - 1|. Δ_contact counts the starting face offset, and that offset sets how hard a fixed position-plus-stiffness command presses. The finding therefore comes close to saying "keep the faces where they were and a reused position setpoint produces the same preload." That is true and useful, but it is narrower than "the contact Jacobian is what matters." The paper's own protocol ablations make this visible. Under re-zeroing at touch, which is common practice, the skeleton predicts the error better (0.88 vs 0.44). Under an ideal torque both predict it about equally (0.69 vs 0.71). Matching contact force removes most of the error (3-7%). So the prescription depends on the protocol. The paper says so in Sec. IV-B4 and in the conclusion, but the title and abstract present it more generally. The head-to-head lead of Δ_contact over Δ_skel is also not significant with 14-16 hands. The redesign families were built to span Δ_contact while keeping or changing the skeleton, which may shape the correlation structure.

The "reversed conclusion" example (+48% reported vs -41% true) is a strong motivation. I would like it shown for more than one interface swap. All evidence is in simulation, with no in-hand rolling. Stage 2 is a concrete, pre-registered physical test with a stated refutation criterion. That suits an extended abstract, but it means claim (2) rests only on simulation.

Presentation: the paper is very dense. The notation is heavy (F1 vs Fp, t vs h, S1/S2, N_H, q0, the 2R-DD/2R-remote names), and some terms are used before they are defined. The abstract packs too many numbers into long sentences. Fig. 3 and Fig. 4b-d are informative but very small. The "Stage 2" subsection under the Conclusion is an odd place for it. One point looks inconsistent. Corollary 1 says the bound holds "wherever the new axis is placed along the face," but Sec. III-E reports a fixed axis "whose contact point may slide" at 0.59-0.80 of the bound, i.e. below it. Presumably c0 is not kept in that case, but the paper should say so.

Scores: the analysis is original in how it frames things (pole placement relative to the pad face) and in the methodological point about confounds in interface studies. The evidence for claim (1) is solid. Claim (2) is supported, but only under one actuation protocol, and the mechanism mostly reduces to preload. Clarity holds the paper back. Overall I judge it a solid, borderline-positive extended abstract.
