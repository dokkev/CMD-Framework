ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and how well each is supported:

1. Kinematic analysis (Sec. III-A). The claim is that the pole's location relative to the contact surfaces governs both the clearance between thick pads (Eq. 2) and the contact-surface motion after a redesign (Eq. 3), and that the contact-surface motion and the skeleton motion cannot both be preserved when pad depth changes (Eq. 4), with a lower bound for a fixed axis (Eq. 5). These are clean, elementary planar-kinematics results, and they frame the problem well. Eq. (3) and (5) follow directly from the rotation-about-pole form. Eq. (4) is plausible (a triangle inequality on a point displaced by Delta t), but it is stated without derivation, and its assumptions (equal Delta t on both phalanges, preserved initial positions of both points, contact point "directly below the fingertip") are restrictive. Eq. (2) assumes the pole is midway between the pad corners. The analysis is correct as far as I can check, but it is a lens rather than a deep theoretical contribution. The novelty lies in pointing it at interface-swap experiments.

2. Design studies (Sec. III-D). The claim is that the CFL keeps more coverage than 2R-DD in all 10 feasible conditions and has the smallest Delta_contact in all 9 redesign conditions, at 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound and with almost no coverage cost. Fig. 3 supports this well. The 2R-remote control, which separates actuator relocation from articulation, is a good addition, and it shows honestly that relocation alone matches or beats the CFL on coverage at 90 deg and for thin pads at 120 deg. Weaker points: three GA seeds with no reported spread or convergence; a coverage metric (an inscribed cylinder averaged over three angles) that is specific and only lightly justified; and Delta_contact measured at a single point (below the fingertip) on the distal pad only. The pole-depth trend (2.4 to 7.4 mm as the surface depth goes from 6 to 12 mm) is a nice check that the optimizer does what the analysis predicts.

3. Evaluation (Sec. IV). The claim is that, under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Delta_contact and not Delta_skel, across simulators, contact models and tasks. The experimental design is the strongest part of the paper. Redesigns are optimized to decouple Delta_contact from Delta_skel. Partial correlations, permutation tests, Holm correction and a pre-registered test are reported. Replications run in Drake with hydroelastic contact and on two more tasks. Alternative protocols (force matching, re-zeroing at touch, ideal torque) are reported, including where the claim weakens. The "reversed conclusion" example (+48% vs. a true -41%) is a compelling motivation.

My main reservation is that the core finding is close to expected by construction. Sec. IV-B1 says it directly: under a joint-space impedance setpoint, a pad face that is displaced changes the preload, and pull-out load scales with preload, so E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1|. A metric that measures where the contact face sits after a redesign will then predict preload error, and a fingertip-trajectory metric will not. This is a useful demonstration, but much of it is a consequence of the chosen protocol (open-loop, position-referenced, quasi-static pull-out). The paper itself shows that the conclusion changes under other protocols. With re-zeroing at touch the error follows the skeleton, and with ideal torque it follows both. So the result is narrower than the abstract's framing ("the effect of the interface itself cannot be measured"). It holds for setpoint-reuse protocols, not for interface evaluation in general. A closed-loop or learned policy, which the introduction uses as motivation, is not tested.

Other points behind my scores:
- All evidence is simulated. The CoDe Hand exists as CAD, and Fig. 1(c) interfaces are illustrative. For an extended abstract this is acceptable, but it limits significance. The hardware plan in the conclusion is concrete (three redesigns with predicted errors of 21/72/124%), which is good.
- The "interface effect" is limited to friction and stiffness changes measured by pull-out load. Sensing interfaces, which the introduction emphasizes (tactile, GelSight), are not addressed by the metric or the experiments.
- The headline remedy still leaves sizeable error: R errs by 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. The conclusion's "near 20% up to 7 mm" is accurate but sits at the edge.
- A simple software alternative is not discussed: offsetting the joint setpoint or gain per redesign to restore the contact-face position or preload. Force matching (3-7%) needs sensing, but a model-based setpoint correction might not. The paper should argue why a mechanical redesign is preferable to such a correction.
- Statistics: points within a setting share designs and placements, so they are not independent. The n (14-45) mixes redesigns with grid placements. The redesign set was built to span Delta_contact, which inflates rank correlations relative to a natural design distribution. This is fine for testing predictiveness but should be acknowledged.
- Clarity: the writing is compact and mostly precise, but dense. Terms such as "O's placements", "the grid", "a face that advances", "own h" and "settings" assume context the reader lacks. Table I and Fig. 4(b) need more self-contained captions.

Overall: a well-motivated and carefully executed preliminary study with a clear and new framing (contact-centric redesign as a control variable for interface experiments). The analysis is modest, the central experimental result is partly implied by its protocol, and there is no hardware yet. For the RSS extended-abstract track, where preliminary experiments are acceptable, I lean towards acceptance.
