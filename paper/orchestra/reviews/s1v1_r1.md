ASSESSMENT NOTES:

I read all six pages of the PDF (text, equations, Figs. 1–5, Tables I–II) and the reviewer instructions. I read no other files.

**The hypotheses as I read them**
- **H1 (core):** A finger is redesigned for a thicker interface and then driven by the original hand's impedance setpoint. Its error in measuring an interface effect grows with the change in contact-surface motion, Δ^prox_distal (the distal pad path seen from the proximal pad). It does not grow with the change in skeleton, Δ_skel (the fingertip path in the base frame).
- **H2:** This dependence is not just a preload change. Δ^prox_distal still predicts the error once contact force is matched.
- **H3:** A policy trained on the original hand O keeps its performance on the redesign that preserves "the motion its task uses".
- **Design hypothesis (Sec. III):** A fixed-axis PIP cannot keep the contact motion when the pads deepen (Eqs. 3–4). A crossed four-bar (CFL) whose pole can be placed near the contact faces both makes room (Eq. 1) and keeps the motion.

**Evidence for each**
- **Kinematic analysis.** The analysis is clean and correct as far as I can check.
  - Eq. 1's 3.46 mm/mm is 2·tan 60°.
  - Eq. 2 follows from rotation about a common pole with the same c0.
  - Eq. 3 follows from the frames shifting by Δt.
  - This is the most solid part of the paper. It is, however, elementary planar kinematics; its novelty lies in how it is applied, not in the theory.
- **CFL vs. other joints.** The design comparison (Fig. 3) is credible but modest.
  - The CFL beats at-joint by 2.4–14.6 pp.
  - The CFL beats remote by only about 3 pp at 7 mm/120° (70 vs 73%).
  - The CFL keeps contact motion best in all nine conditions. This is partly by construction, since the CFL is the only architecture with a movable pole.
- **H1 is the best-supported claim.**
  - The correlation set holds Δ^prox_distal fixed while Δ_skel varies, which separates the two variables.
  - ρ = 0.94 over 27 redesigns, with partial ρ up to 0.91, in four Isaac Lab tests, Holm-corrected.
  - The Drake tasks repeat the result under point and hydroelastic contact, and a self-consistency floor of 6% is reported.
  - This is careful design for a Stage 1 abstract.
- **Weak spot: H1 may be close to a tautology.**
  - The object is placed relative to the proximal pad, and the command is a joint stiffness setpoint. The force on the object is then essentially set by where the distal pad sits relative to the object and the proximal pad, so Δ^prox_distal predicting the error is close to built in.
  - Sec. IV-B2 supports this reading: with the object fixed to the palm, the relevant variable becomes Δ^base_distal.
  - The general conclusion is "keep the geometry the object sees". That is the expected outcome, and the opposing view ("keep the skeleton") is something of a strawman. The paper does not show that practitioners actually redesign fingers to keep the fingertip path.
- **Weak spot: baselines.**
  - The most common practice is missing: bolt the thicker pad onto O, possibly with reduced range of motion, or recalibrate the setpoint.
  - Re-referencing alone already cuts the Drake error from 42% to 14%, which weakens the practical need for a redesign whenever commands are hand-tuned.
- **H2.** The support is reasonable but thin: one partial ρ = 0.80 under a matched-torque condition. The force-matching procedure is not described.
- **H3 is the weakest.**
  - "The motion its task uses" is chosen per task after the fact:
    - pad relative motion for finger-walk;
    - fingertip path for the relay dial;
    - proximal-pad position relative to the palm for cube rotation (ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns, a variable not in Eq. 6).
  - As stated, H3 can absorb almost any outcome. It needs an a priori rule mapping a task to its invariant.
  - There are only two training seeds.
  - There is no comparison with standard remedies: fine-tuning, or training with kinematic domain randomization.
  - There is also a tension in the Drake results: Box pivot is a fingertip-contact task, yet Skeleton does worse there than CoDe (27% vs 7%) and Δ_skel does not correlate with the error (ρ = −0.16). This is not reconciled with "fingertip-end contact follows the skeleton".
- **Validity gap between claim and evidence.**
  - The paper motivates compliant, tactile and gel interfaces, but every result uses non-deforming pads in rigid-body simulation. The interface change is mostly a friction coefficient (1.3 → 2.0).
  - The Conclusion acknowledges that a deforming pad moves the contact point. That is exactly the regime where Δ^prox_distal, defined for a fixed surface point, may stop being the right invariant.
  - The "CoDe Hand" exists only in CAD, and the hardware plan is in a single sentence.
  - Against competing submissions with hardware and many tasks, the evidence is thin.

**What the scores rest on**
- **Value 2.** A careful, pre-registered answer to a narrow question, whose main finding is close to mechanically inevitable under the chosen setup. Its relevance to cross-embodiment transfer is real but undeveloped.
- **Feasibility 2.** H1 is well supported in simulation. H2 is thin. H3 is post hoc and hard to falsify. The step from rigid simulation to deforming real interfaces, which is the paper's own motivation, has no plan beyond one sentence.
