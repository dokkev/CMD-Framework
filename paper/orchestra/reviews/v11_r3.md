THOUGHT:

Intuition. The paper takes a real but rarely stated methodological problem: when a robot finger has to be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface (compliant pad, tactile module), a measured interface effect mixes the interface with the redesign. The proposed fix is a redesign invariant. Keep the relative motion of the two contact faces (the PIP column of a "contact Jacobian" written in the proximal-phalanx frame), not the skeleton. The paper supports this with (C1) finite-pole kinematics: clearance and coverage laws plus a fixed-axis lower bound; (C2) a coverage comparison of four PIP joint types; (C3) a redesign study showing a crossed four-bar (CFL) changes its contact-surface motion least; and (C4) a simulation study in which the error of reused commands/torques follows Delta_contact. The hypotheses come with stated failure conditions, thresholds are fixed in advance, and several robustness checks are reported. That fits a Stage-1 RSS submission well. The physical validation (C5) is deferred, which the venue allows.

Checking the math. Lemma 1 is correct: (I - R_q)^{-1} = 1/2 (I + cot(q/2) R_{pi/2}). Proposition 3 follows directly: Delta c = (R_q - I)(-Delta p), and ||R_q - I|| = 2 sin(q/2). The "lower the pole by delta" argument after Prop. 2 also checks out at q_req: dD = 2 delta tan(q/2)(1 - cos(q/2)) + 2 delta sin(q/2) = 2 delta tan(q/2), the same change as the numerator, so C_pad rises while it is below 1. Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality on the two pole shifts. Corollary 1 is immediate. So C1 is correct but elementary: finite-displacement poles are textbook (Bottema and Roth). The novelty is the framing (what a joint "takes from the pads") rather than new kinematics. That is fine for RSS if the framing pays off, but "We prove" in the abstract oversells it slightly. S1/S2 (equal depths, corners equidistant from the pole) are restrictive. The 0.05 mm median agreement on CFL designs is reassuring. The claim that a physical pin cannot sit palmar of the pad mount is an engineering argument, not a theorem: lugs that take pad width, or a pad split around the joint, are possible design choices.

Main concerns about significance and evidence:

1. The problem may be largely solved by cheaper means the paper sets aside too quickly.
   (a) Spacers. Shimming the thinner interface to the thicker one's face depth keeps Delta_contact = 0 exactly on one physical hand. That is literally "same hand, different contact". The paper excludes spacers in one sentence because they cost the thinner pad 8.6-18.2 coverage points. For an interface comparison that cost may be acceptable or even desirable, because both interfaces then sit at the same coverage. The paper needs a clear argument for when the CFL redesign beats a shimmed hand built for the thickest interface.
   (b) Protocol. The paper's own numbers show that contact-aware protocols nearly remove the problem: matching contact force leaves 3-7% error, and closing from each finger's own touch leaves 20%. The R designs still err by 21% and 41% at 7 and 9 mm under the reused command. So the principle matters mainly for open-loop reused commands and torques. The motivation names scripted grasps, frozen policies and current-controlled servos, but gives no evidence of how common naive reuse is in interface studies. I am not convinced a careful experimenter would reuse a position command across pads of different thickness. Over-closure from a thicker pad is obvious.

2. C4 is partly built into the setup.
   (a) Placements are defined by where O touches the cylinder. That favours hands whose faces move like O's. The authors partly address this with the hand-independent grid, but there rho drops to 0.60 and Delta_skel reaches 0.46. The recovery on "26 redesigns that keep 80% of placements" reads as a post-hoc subset. Was the 80% threshold among the pre-fixed ones?
   (b) The redesign set is hand-picked to span Delta_contact ("redesigns at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible"). By Eq. (7), keeping the contact motion forces a skeleton change, so the main-setting Delta_skel correlation (-0.40, -0.51) is negative partly by construction. The partial correlation (0.88) helps.
   (c) All pad thicknesses share one contact model, so a thicker pad has no thickness-dependent mechanics. In that world, the object only "sees" the face geometry. That Delta_contact predicts the error is then close to a geometric tautology. The other candidates (pad thickness with three levels, transmission that is already compensated by construction, coverage) are weak competitors.
   (d) The samples are small: 14-16 hands, and n = 6 for the shape change. Yet the abstract's range "rho = 0.60-0.94" includes the n = 6 value without saying so.
   (e) The error metric |Delta(H) - Delta(O)|/|Delta(O)| blows up when O's effect is small. The median helps, but per-direction distributions of Delta(O) should be reported.

3. The architecture comparison has confounds.
   (a) C2 is posed against 2R-DD (servo at the joint), so it mainly tests actuator relocation, not the joint type. The fair baseline is 2R-remote, and it beats CFL on the stated objective at 90 deg and at 5 mm/120 deg. The text then switches to "pad length over fixed L_f", on which CFL wins. Switching metrics when the objective loses invites a metric-shopping reading.
   (b) Mechanical advantage is unconstrained and only a transmission-angle window is imposed. "Control compensates" the transmission kinematically, but not torque capacity, so the CFL designs may be weak or backlash-sensitive in practice. Some report of MA ranges is needed.
   (c) CFL has more geometric parameters than a 2R or RCJ. Matching a reference relative motion better with more freedom is expected (classic four-bar motion generation), so the result should be framed as such.
   (d) Delta_contact includes start offsets for 2R-DD and RCJ. Without them CFL wins only 7 of 9 cells, yet the abstract states "changes it least of four joint types" unqualified.
   (e) Three seeds per optimisation is thin, with no variance reported.

4. "Keeps" is undefined. CFL's change is 0.35-4.31 mm, i.e. 10-51% of the bound. The simulation shows about 4 mm of Delta_contact gives roughly 100% error, and R at 9 mm (2.3 mm) still gives 41%. No tolerance is stated for Delta_contact to count as "kept", so C3's success criterion ("fails if it reaches the bound") is very weak.

5. Scope. The analysis is planar, single-finger, with the MCP held at 0 and a cylinder hooked against one finger. The paper says the MCP column is not kept. In a real hand, the palm, other fingers and the thumb also meet the object, and the MCP lever changes with pad depth. The conclusion does note the MCP extension.

Writing. The paper is dense and number-heavy, with very short sentences that often drop the needed context. Examples: "refines CFL from neighbouring designs", "matches the evaluator within 1e-13" (which evaluator?), "Downward markers: degenerate 2R-remote designs (0.21, 0)", "Spacers are excluded (Sec. IV-C)" before spacers are introduced. Further issues:
- The title is long, has two subtitles, and "Same Hand" contradicts the content: the hand is redesigned.
- "Contact Jacobian" is used for the relative pose derivative of two faces, which overloads the standard term (the map from joint rates to contact-point velocities). The paragraph proving that J determines c and vice versa is trivial and could go.
- "Pad dimensions are requirements" and "coverage is the objective" sit uneasily with Fig. 2, where pad length varies (32/25/34 mm). Is l_i fixed or optimised?
- Fractions (0.085) and percentage points are mixed.
- Table I's column header structure ("grid fric.", "O's contacts fric. stiff.", "Torque") is hard to parse in the text. The bold marking, which carries the main claim, is not visible to me.
- C1 is listed as "a hypothesis with a stated way to fail" but is a theorem.
- The abstract packs about 12 numbers and is hard to follow on a first read.
- Fig. 1 works well as a teaser. Figs. 2 and 4 are clear.
- The Stage-2 plan (one finger, O/R/U pads and links) is sketched in two sentences. The guidelines ask what insights would come from negative results, and this is not discussed explicitly.

Overall. This is a careful, falsifiable, honestly hedged Stage-1 paper with a nice unifying idea and correct (if elementary) kinematics. The simulation evidence is suggestive but partly constructed. Practical significance depends on protocols and alternatives (spacers, contact-aware closing) that the paper itself shows reduce the problem. This is borderline for Stage 1. I lean towards rejection (5) because the central "why it matters" claim is not yet argued against the obvious alternatives. I could be persuaded if the authors address the spacer/protocol argument and tighten the C2 baseline and the C4 statistics.
