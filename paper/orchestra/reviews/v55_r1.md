ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper claims that when a finger is redesigned for a thicker contact interface, it should preserve the motion of its contact surfaces relative to each other (the contact Jacobian in the proximal-pad frame, Δ_contact), not the skeleton or fingertip path (Δ_skel). It supports this with four pieces of evidence: (i) a finite-displacement pole analysis; (ii) a crossed four-bar PIP that places its pole near the pad surfaces; (iii) comparative design studies against at-joint, remote, and rolling-contact joints; (iv) simulation studies showing that the error in a measured pad effect tracks Δ_contact and not Δ_skel.

How strong each claim is:

1. **Theory (Eqs. 1–5).** The analysis is correct and elegant within its assumptions. |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| follows from Eq. 1. The triangle-inequality bound Δ_contact + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt| is sound, given that Fp translates rigidly by Δt relative to F1 and that the initial contact point and fingertip are the same. The fixed-axis bound (Eq. 5) holds even if the axis moves along the finger. Two caveats:
   - The impossibility is conditional on the same initial contact point c0. Fig. 3(c) concedes that an at-joint design can beat the bound from a different c0, and Δ_contact deliberately counts a moved c0. So "a fixed axis cannot keep that motion" is weaker than the abstract makes it sound.
   - The bound is tight only at q_req. It also measures depth deepening Δt, which the designer can partly cancel by raising pivots. The paper says so, but then the "no redesign keeps both" message depends on a quantity the design can control.

2. **Design studies.** The room claim is credible: CoDe covers more than the at-joint finger by 2.4–14.6 pp in all 10 conditions where both are feasible. The preservation claim (Fig. 3c) is plausible.
   - The most relevant prior alternative, remote-center-of-motion fingers that carry thick skin [13, 15], is not in the comparison, even though the introduction names it as the competing approach.
   - Every architecture is scored against its own coverage-optimal reference. That makes "contact motion kept" comparable only in a normalized sense.

3. **Central empirical claim.** The weakest point is conceptual, not statistical. The ground-truth "pad effect" is D_O: the effect measured on the original 3 mm hand O carrying the new pad's contact properties on its own geometry. That hand cannot physically exist, since the premise of the paper is that O has no room for the thick pad. A redesign that reproduces O's contact kinematics will, almost by construction, reproduce O's response to a contact-parameter change. So "error tracks Δ_contact" is close to tautological under this reference.
   - The authors defend the choice in one sentence ("an interface study asks what the pad does to the original hand"). It is a defensible convention, but not the only one. With a thicker pad, the geometry change is part of the pad's real effect.
   - The headline Fig. 1(d) reversal (−41% on O vs. +48% on Coverage) is striking. Under a different reference it could instead read as "Coverage measures the effect of thick pads on a hand that can carry them."
   - This is the main reason the significance claim is not, to me, adequately supported.

4. **Statistics.**
   - The number of redesigns in each correlation set is never given. ρ = 0.94 "pre-registered" and the Fig. 4 ρ values (0.43 and 0.53 for two of the four Drake tasks) cannot be judged without n.
   - The Fig. 4 correlations for the dial and the box pivot are weak, yet the text says the Drake tasks "keep this ordering." Ordering three named hands is a much weaker claim than correlation.
   - Errors are normalized by |D_O|. When D_O is small this inflates values (e.g., 388%, 347%).
   - Several CoDe entries in Table I are exactly 0%, which deserves an explanation (quantization, or medians over few placements?).
   - The pre-registration is asserted but not documented.

5. **Preload control.** "Not only preload" is a good addition: partial ρ = 0.80 with force deviation held fixed, and re-referencing the setpoint still leaves ρ = 0.73.

6. **Fairness of the Skeleton comparison.** CoDe's success is attributed to its objective (adding Δ_MCP) and to freed pivot heights (F′). The paper does not state whether Skeleton and Coverage were optimized over the same enlarged feasible set, so the comparison may not be like-for-like.

7. **Policy transfer.** This part is weak and partly post hoc. Each of the three tasks follows a different quantity (Δ_contact, Δ_skel, palm shift), each chosen after the fact. There are only two seeds, and pad walking fails in Drake even on O. The contribution bullet ("transfers to the redesign that keeps the motion its task uses") is therefore barely falsifiable as stated.

8. **Simulation only.** Neither contact model deforms the pad, yet the motivation is compliant pads and tactile skins. A deforming pad moves the contact point, which interacts directly with the claimed mechanism. The modular-finger contribution is shown only as CAD; no hardware is built.

My scores rest on these points. The theory is original and clean, and the simulation program is broad (two simulators, two contact models, preload control). But the central empirical claim depends on an unrealizable reference that partly builds in the conclusion, n is not reported, and there is no physical validation. Presentation is very dense; notation and naming are hard to follow.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. **Fig. 1 naming.** Fig. 1(c,d) labels the coverage-only redesign "Conventional," while the text, Table I and Figs. 4–5 call it "Coverage." Use one name throughout.
2. **"CoDe" means two things.** It labels the crossed four-bar architecture in Fig. 3 and Sec. III-D, and also the specific contact-preserving redesign hand. In addition, the "Coverage" hand is the CoDe architecture's coverage optimum. The reader has to untangle all three. Consider "four-bar" for the architecture.
3. **Sec. III-C equation flow.** Eq. (7) and the sentence "Every design is collision-free over [0, q_req], keeps a usable transmission angle and shares the actuator" are split across the column and around Fig. 2. Eq. (9) uses F′ before it is defined. Define F′ before Eq. (9).
4. **Δ_MCP introduced too early.** It first appears in Sec. III-A ("(Δ_MCP, Eq. (7))") before Eq. (7) defines it. Move or forward-reference it explicitly.
5. **Eq. (3) assumption.** The same-c0 assumption should be stated as an explicit condition of Eqs. (4) and (5), and the abstract's "cannot keep" qualified accordingly.
6. **Fig. 3(c) metric.** 100(1 − Δ_contact/b) is hard to parse, and negative values ("farther than a fixed axis") are unintuitive. Plot Δ_contact directly with the bound b as a line, or explain the normalization in the text.
7. **Table I clarity.** Blank cells (Drake point has no compl. pad column; Drake hydro has no convex column) are unexplained. The "Other commands" group does not say which simulator or contact model it uses. Explain the 0% entries.
8. **Missing sample sizes.** Report n for every ρ: in the abstract (ρ = 0.94), in Sec. IV-B, and in the Fig. 4 captions.
9. **Two error statistics.** "Median error" (Table I) and E (median |error|) are both used, and the text alternates between them, e.g., "E 222% vs. 124%." A one-line definition near Table I and consistent use would help.
10. **Fig. 4 is too small to read.** The E and ρ annotations under each panel are tiny, and circle versus diamond markers are hard to tell apart in print. Enlarge them, or move the numbers into a table.
11. **Fig. 2(a).** The shaded "contact faces, 3–9 mm pads" band and the region boundaries are hard to read. Say in the caption what the upper CoDe boundary (the curve near 30 mm) means.
12. **Fig. 5.** "palm shift" appears without a symbol, while the other two tasks use Δ_contact and Δ_skel. Relate it to Δ_MCP if it is the same quantity.
13. **Undefined terms.** "Grid of placements shared by further redesigns" and "correlation set" are introduced tersely. A short sentence on how the grid and the decoupling redesigns were generated, with their count, would make Sec. IV-A self-contained.
14. **Abstract density.** The abstract crowds in many claims (pole analysis, design studies, ρ, two simulators, preload, reversal). Prioritize the central claim and its evidence.
15. **Introduction and Fig. 1.** "redesign that keeps the skeleton exactly misreports … as badly as one that ignores kinematics" should cite the specific numbers (E 222% vs. 124%) or point to the result where they appear.
16. **Typesetting.** Hyphenation artifacts ("contactsurface," "Servodriven," "fourbar") appear in the contribution list and Sec. III-B. Check the line breaks in the final PDF.
17. **References.** Some entries are inconsistent: page numbers appear for only a few, arXiv formatting varies, and [12] is ICLR 2026, so check that its status is final.
