ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper makes two claims about servo-driven robot fingers that carry exchangeable contact interfaces (thick pads, tactile modules), plus one claim about methodology.

(1) Room. Remote actuation with a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP joint keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute finger (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and required flexion. Evidence: per-joint optimization (GA/NSGA-II, three seeds) over 4 thicknesses x 3 flexions, with the CFL ahead of 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both are feasible, by 2.4-14.6 points. Propositions 1-2 give a clean geometric reason: the pin-joint gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of face depth, and coverage rises as the pole moves down toward the face. This claim is well supported against 2R-DD. Fig. 3a-b shows, however, that the belt-driven revolute (2R-remote) matches or beats the CFL in coverage at 90 and 120 degrees. On coverage alone the CFL is only needed at 150 degrees. Its real advantage lies in claim (2). The abstract and contribution bullets credit "remote servos and a CFL" jointly and do not separate the two.

(2) Redesign. A redesign for a thicker pad should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), the PIP column of the contact Jacobian, rather than the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot, with the closed-form bound Delta_contact >= 2 sin(q_req/2)|Delta t|. The CFL reaches 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. The theory (Lemma 1, Prop. 3, Eq. 8, Corollary 1) is correct but elementary: it is the planar-displacement pole plus a triangle inequality. Its value is in framing, not depth. The optimization evidence (Fig. 3d-g) is convincing for the CFL against 2R-remote. The comparison with RCJ and 2R-DD depends on the start-offset convention ("least in 7 of 9" once the offset is removed).

(3) Methodology. Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect (friction or stiffness change -> pull-out load) tracks Delta_contact (Spearman 0.80-0.86), not Delta_skel. This holds in Isaac Lab, again in Drake with point and hydroelastic contact, and in grasp acquisition and dial turning. The error can flip the sign of an interface conclusion (+48% reported vs. -41% true). The statistics are careful for a workshop-length paper: pre-registration, Holm correction, hand-bootstrap intervals, partial correlations, and the admission that the lead over Delta_skel is not significant with 14-16 hands. My main reservations:
 - The mechanism is close to by construction. The paper itself shows E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1| to first order. A reused setpoint with a displaced face changes the preload, and a friction-limited pull-out load scales with preload. So the experiment mostly confirms that Delta_contact predicts the preload change. That is useful, but less a finding about "the contact Jacobian" than about where the face sits at the commanded angle.
 - The result depends on the protocol, and the realistic alternatives undercut its reach. With force matching the error drops to 3-7%, lower than the best CFL redesign R at 7 mm (21%). Under re-zero-at-touch, the protocol practitioners actually use with current or contact thresholds, the skeleton predicts the error (rho 0.88) and Delta_contact does not (0.44). Section V acknowledges this, but the abstract states the Delta_contact claim without that qualifier.
 - The redesign population (R, U, "keep/change skeleton most", 27 grid redesigns) is chosen by the authors. The rank correlations therefore partly reflect how the design space was sampled. The sign of the Delta_skel correlation flips between settings (-0.40/-0.51 vs. +0.22), which points to a sampling artifact.
 - All evidence is simulation with a planar finger and the MCP held. No hardware is shown, although the title says "CoDe Hand". The Stage 2 plan is pre-registered with a stated refutation test, which is good practice for an extended abstract.

Presentation is the weakest part. The prose is extremely compressed ("Only a joint whose pole moves can."). Many symbols and labels are introduced in passing or not at all: r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, the hands O/R/U/M, "the grid", "O's own placements", and "points" versus percent. Fig. 2c (3D surface) and Fig. 4b-d (tiny log-scale scatters) are hard to read at print size. Fig. 1's caption omits panel (a). I had to reread Sec. IV several times to reconstruct the protocol. The ideas are sound and the evaluation is unusually disciplined. The work meets the bar for a preliminary extended abstract, with modest significance limited by the preload-centric mechanism and the protocol dependence.
