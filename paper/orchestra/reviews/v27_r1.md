ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Reviewer r1 (robot-hand designer; has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them).

The paper makes two claims about a planar two-DoF servo-driven finger whose PIP is a crossed four-bar (CFL) driven by a servo on the proximal phalanx.

(C1) Room. Remote actuation plus a CFL keeps more two-phalanx pad coverage C̄_pad than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every pad thickness (3-9 mm) and required flexion (90/120/150 deg). The geometry is well argued. Lemma 1 and Props. 1-2 give a clean reason: a fixed axis's joint gap grows by 2 tan(q_req/2) per mm of face depth, while a virtual pole can follow the face. The optimization (Eq. 10) compares each joint at its own best design, with 3 seeds, a 2R-remote ablation that separates "remote actuator" from "polycentric joint", and idealized upper bounds. CFL beats 2R-DD in 10/10 conditions by 2.4-14.6 points, and its worst run beats 2R-DD's best in 9/10. That evidence holds up for the metric as defined. As a hand designer I have three reservations. (i) Mechanical advantage is left free. The CFL's worst-case MA is 0.41-0.58 against 1 for 2R-DD, so with the same servo the CFL finger delivers roughly half the PIP torque. Thick soft pads need more squeeze, not less, so leaving MA out of the comparison favours the CFL. A coverage-vs-MA front, or a run with an MA floor at a typical payload, is needed before "keeps more contact surface at every thickness" can be read as a design recommendation. (ii) The pin-joint baseline is restricted by an assumption: "full-width pads leave no room for lugs", so a pin must sit above the pad mount. Narrower pads with side lugs, or split pads, are common practice, and they would let a pin sit lower. The 2R baselines may be weaker than what a practitioner would build. (iii) Coverage is a symmetric cylinder-in-the-V overlap at three poses. It is a sensible proxy, but it rewards one grasp type.

(C2) Keep the contact Jacobian, not the skeleton. The paper defines Δ_contact, the change of the PIP column of the contact Jacobian expressed as a distal-face point path in the proximal-face frame. Prop. 3, Eq. 8 and Cor. 1 show that a fixed axis cannot keep it when the face deepens, with a closed-form lower bound. They also show that keeping both skeleton and contact motion is impossible for Δt > 0. This is the most original and useful part of the paper. It gives a precise, checkable statement of something hand builders handle informally when they "keep the finger the same" after an interface change. Sec. III-E reports CFL redesigns at 10-51% of the bound with almost no coverage loss, and spacers give Δ_contact = 0 at a cost of 8.6-18.2 coverage points.

The simulation evidence for C2 is broad for a Stage 1 paper. It spans Isaac Lab (Newton, MuJoCo-Warp) and Drake with point and hydroelastic contact, two interface effects (friction, stiffness), a placement grid, and three extra tasks. The tests are pre-registered, the paper uses one-sided permutation tests with a Holm correction, and it reports a null in in-hand rolling. Δ_contact orders the error with ρ = 0.80-0.94, and the redesigns that keep the skeleton exactly err 95-228%. The sign-reversal example (U reports +48% where the truth is -41%) is a good illustration of why the problem matters. My concerns:
- The mechanism looks like preload. Under a reused position setpoint at stiffness K0, a face that moves differently closes earlier or later, so the squeeze force changes (R 0.75x, U 3.3x of O), and holding load scales with squeeze. That makes the core result close to expected by construction. The partial correlation with setpoint force held fixed (0.64) is post hoc. The paper's own "other protocols" paragraph shows that matching contact force leaves only 3-7% error, which is better than R's 21-41%.
- The servo-hand framing matters here. Servo hands do have present-current readings, so a torque-matched protocol is not exotic. The paper reports it (9-53%, following the skeleton), but it should discuss why the reused position setpoint is the protocol interface studies should be designed around.
- The claim is overstated. "A redesign that keeps its contact Jacobian lets the pad be studied on its own" does not match R's own errors of 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. In my experience, pad comparisons often turn on effects of 20-50%, so these residuals are as large as the effects being measured. The defensible claim is ordinal: Δ_contact predicts how much a redesign biases the measurement.
- The contact model is far from real pads. Point contact on rigid faces, with pad stiffness as a scalar, is a long way from a 7-9 mm silicone pad with a contact patch, hysteresis and shear. The hydroelastic check helps, but the hands rank only loosely alike across the two models (ρ = 0.39). Defining c(q) at a single point "below the fingertip" is also fragile for area contact.
- Scope is narrow. The finger is planar, the MCP is held at 0, the objects are cylinders, and in-hand rolling fails. Tactile modules appear in the title-level motivation, but no tactile module is analysed. Tactile modules bring cabling across the joint and a rigid PCB backing, and the crossed links may obstruct both.

Stage 2 plan. The falsification plan is concrete and falsifiable. It uses one finger with swappable pad modules and CFL links, and four hands (O; R, M and U at 7 mm) along which Δ_contact rises while Δ_skel falls. That design separates the two hypotheses well. The plan measures Δ_contact with markers including backlash and pad compression, uses bare and textured covers, has 5x4x5 trials, and states predictions (21/72/124%) and a one-sided Page trend test. Missing pieces:
- The hand "M" is never defined.
- There is no power analysis. The paper does not estimate how large O's own friction effect is relative to trial-to-trial noise in pull-until-slip on a planar slide.
- Link swap and reassembly tolerance is not budgeted. Pivot play in a printed four-bar can plausibly add sub-mm variation, which is comparable to R's 0.9 mm.
- Four hands make for a coarse trend test.
- There is no plan for servo backlash or compliance inside the K0 loop.

Presentation. The content runs onto page 6 (about 5.6 pages), and the PDF is 8 pages including references. The venue sets a hard limit of 5 content pages plus 1 page of references, so this must be fixed. Figure 1 is a placeholder. The prose is very compressed, and several terms are used without definition: x0, r_{2,s}/r_{1,e}, "the refinement", "transmission table", "mounting rule" (only partly defined), and M. Fig. 2 and Fig. 5 are informative. Fig. 2c's 3-D surface is hard to read at print size.

Scores. Soundness: the theory is sound, and the simulation is careful and multi-model. The headline claim is stronger than the residual errors, and MA is not controlled in C1 (3). Presentation: the page limit is violated, Fig. 1 is a placeholder, and the text is dense with undefined terms (2). Contribution: the Δ_contact framing and the fixed-axis bound are a real conceptual contribution for interface studies on servo hands (3). Overall 6, Accept for Stage 1, conditional on fixing the length and softening the "studied on its own" claim.
