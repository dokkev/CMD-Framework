THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a finger must be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should preserve the relative motion of the two contact faces (one point c(q) on the distal face, written in a frame attached to the proximal face), not the link skeleton. It builds this on classical planar finite-pole kinematics: (i) a clearance law (Eq. 4) and a coverage law (Eq. 5) showing the joint gap grows with the depth of the face below the pole; (ii) Prop. 3 / Eq. 6, ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Δp||, so face motion is preserved iff the pole keeps its place relative to the face; (iii) Eq. 7, a thicker pad at fixed mount depth cannot keep both skeleton and contact-surface motion; (iv) Corollary 1, a fixed-axis lower bound 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δh − Δd|. It then shows by optimization (Studies 1–2) that a crossed four-bar (CFL) with a virtual, palmar pole retains coverage and stays at 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound, and by MuJoCo-Warp simulation (Study 3) that the error in a measured interface effect (friction, stiffness, shape) under a reused command or reused torque rank-correlates with Δ_contact and not with the other candidates.

My intuitions:

1. The analytic part is the strongest piece. I checked the key relations. Lemma 1 is the standard finite pole. Eq. 6 follows directly from c = p + R(c0 − p). Eq. 7 is the triangle inequality on the two pole shifts that differ by Δt. The statement that lowering the pole by δ adds 2δ tan(q_req/2) to both the numerator and the denominator of Eq. 5 checks out with half-angle identities (−ΔG(1−cos) + 2δ sin = 2δ tan), so the conclusion "the best joint turns about a point on the face" is sound under S1/S2. The Fig. 2 numbers (8.7 = 5 tan 60°, 15.6 = 9 tan 60°, 6.9 = 4 tan 60°) agree. These results are simple but useful, and they make the fixed-axis limitation precise. However, the mathematics is classical (Bottema and Roth), and the novelty is the framing, not the kinematics.

2. The central empirical claim (C4) is close to true by construction, and the paper does not acknowledge this. In the simulation the MCP is held, the cylinder is free in the sagittal plane, it is placed relative to the proximal face, and "pads of every thickness share one contact model". If φ = q on every design and the PIP stiffness acts on q, then two hands with identical c(q) present the object with identical face geometry and identical contact physics, up to pad ends and distal-tip geometry. E → 0 as Δ_contact → 0 is therefore a consequence of the definition, and a monotone trend is expected. The Spearman ranking of "candidates" mostly confirms that the authors defined the right sufficient statistic for their own simulator. The informative questions are different. How much Δ_contact can be tolerated for a given error? Does the conclusion survive when the contact model differs with thickness, as it does for real thick compliant pads? The second question is exactly what the simulation removes. The real-world confound, that a 9 mm elastomer behaves differently from a 3 mm one, is assumed away. This is precisely the regime the paper is motivated by.

3. The candidate comparison is shaped by how the redesigns were sampled. Redesigns were generated "at set Δ_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". Combined with Eq. 7, which couples Δ_contact and Δ_skel through Δt, this sampling induces the negative Δ_skel correlations (−0.40, −0.51). It also makes "skeleton does not predict" partly an artifact of the design of experiments. On the hand-independent grid, Δ_skel correlates positively (0.46), and the authors attribute this to placements lost. Also unclear: are the negative Δ_skel intervals excluding zero? If so, the skeleton change "predicts" the error with the opposite sign, which needs explanation. Hands that cannot hold the cylinder are excluded (3 of 18). These are likely high-Δ_contact failures, so their exclusion should be reported, as it could either strengthen or bias the correlation. The shape result is reported over only six hands and is not in Table I.

4. The magnitude of the remaining error undercuts the practical message. Under the reused command, R, the design built to keep the contact-surface motion, still errs 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm on the friction effect. An interface study that misreports a friction effect by 41% is not "comparable". The paper frames R's error as "least", but it does not state what error a study can tolerate. Under the reused torque the numbers are better (5, 17, 12%). Contact-sensing protocols (closing from touch at 20%, matching force at 3–7%) achieve similar or lower error without any redesign principle. This raises the question of whether the right fix is the protocol rather than the kinematics. The paper should engage with this honestly.

5. The motivation is asserted rather than evidenced. "Interface studies often reuse the same commands across hands" has no citation. The paper does not show a single published interface comparison that was confounded in this way. The related work cites tactile fingers that were designed around their sensors (Svelte, All the feels), but those are not cross-interface studies. For an RSS audience the significance hinges on this, and it is the weakest link.

6. C2 is framed so that it is easy to pass. The falsification criterion compares the CFL against the weakest baseline (2R-DD). The belt-driven 2R-remote beats the CFL by 2–4 points at 90° and at 120° for thin pads, and the text softens this as "keeps as much". RCJ ideal also matches or exceeds the CFL in several cells. C2 as stated holds, but the comparison that matters (CFL vs 2R-remote) is mixed. The paper's real argument for the CFL is C3, and the paper should say so rather than present C2 as a win.

7. The modelling assumptions drive the bound. Corollary 1 is tight only because the pad is mounted "at the shallowest depth clear of its joint hardware" (Δd = 0) and because "full-width pads leave no room for lugs". A smaller pin or bearing, slotted pads, or side lugs would let a fixed axis recess the face. The spacer alternative is dismissed in two sentences, despite being the obvious practical solution when the set of interfaces is known: it gives Δ_contact = 0 at a cost of 8.6–18.2 coverage points. Coverage itself (the inscribed-cylinder overlap C̄_pad) is an idiosyncratic metric, and the paper never argues that it is the right proxy for grasp quality.

8. Scope. Study 3 varies designs essentially within the CFL family, plus one revolute contrast evaluated against its own 3 mm finger, not against O. The MCP is held throughout. The MCP column of the contact Jacobian, which unavoidably changes with pad depth, is excluded. In a real hand the proximal face relative to the palm and to the other fingers matters for multi-finger grasps.

9. Writing. The paper is compact and adopts a disciplined short-sentence style, and the hypotheses-with-failure-criteria structure is exemplary for a Stage-1 submission. However, the abstract and the introduction are overloaded with numbers and coined phrases before they are defined ("near-best contact surface", "10–51% of the fixed-axis bound", "start offset", q_req). "Contact surface" is used both as a noun for the pad face and as a quantity ("keeps more contact surface" = coverage). The title is too long and reads as two slogans. Fig. 3's "(0.21, 0)" and Fig. 4's "top squares" are cryptic. Sec. V's setup is hard to parse: "the CoDe hand model's three CFL fingers" against "one planar finger", the place where the shape ρ appears, and the relation of the 18, 15, 45 and 26 hand counts all need care. Calling c' the "PIP column of the finger's contact Jacobian" is a slight abuse: c is a fixed body point, not the contact point, and the contact point moves. The paper also misses directly relevant prior work. It does not cite the contact-kinematics literature (Montana's contact equations; Kerr and Roth) or path/function-generation synthesis of linkages, and matching c(q) with timing is exactly a function-generation problem. Positive figure notes: Fig. 1 is an effective teaser, and Fig. 2 makes the clearance law immediately clear. Fig. 4d usefully separates start offset from motion.

Overall: a clean, honest-looking Stage-1 paper. It has a crisp analytic core, explicit falsification criteria and a concrete Stage-2 protocol. The main reservations are that C4 is largely implied by the simulation's assumptions, that the problem's real-world prevalence is not demonstrated, and that the residual error of the recommended design remains large under the paper's main protocol. Given RSS Stage 1 allows preliminary experiments and the methodology is fully specified, I lean to a borderline accept. The Stage-2 physical test (R vs U with real compliant pads) is the experiment that will decide whether the principle matters.
