ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper claims that when a dexterous finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the property to keep is the motion of the contact surfaces relative to each other (a contact Jacobian seen from the proximal phalanx), not the skeleton (joint axes, fingertip path). The claim has three parts.

(i) Analytical (Sec. III-A). Eq. (3) is the standard identity for a planar rotation about a finite-displacement pole: Δc = (I − R_q)Δp, so ‖Δc‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖. It is correct when both designs share c0. Eq. (4) follows from the poles in F_p and F_1 differing by a pure translation Δt. Two assumptions are left implicit:
- The fingertip version of Eq. (3) needs the same initial fingertip position as well as the same initial contact point. Without both, the bound does not follow as written.
- F_p must translate rigidly by Δt, with no rotation, relative to F_1.

Eq. (5) also holds only from the same initial contact point and with the axis depth fixed, and Fig. 3(c) concedes that at-joint designs can beat it by moving c0. So "a fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion" is conditional. The analysis is neat and correct but elementary. Its value is in the framing, not in new kinematics.

(ii) Design studies (Sec. III-D). The crossed four-bar beats the servo-at-joint revolute finger in coverage in all 10 jointly feasible conditions (2.4–14.6 pp) and preserves contact motion best in all nine conditions. This is plausible and well shown in Fig. 3, but it depends on the authors' own coverage metric (inscribed cylinder, Eq. 6) and on NSGA-II search. Robustness to the metric and to the optimizer is not shown, and the remote-actuation baseline matches CoDe for much of the range. So the room advantage comes partly from moving the servo, not from the articulation, which the paper partly concedes.

(iii) Simulation evidence (Sec. IV). This is the central empirical claim: the error in a measured pad effect, under a reused impedance setpoint, tracks Δ_contact and not Δ_skel. The internal evidence is careful:
- two simulators and point vs. hydroelastic contact;
- a correlation set built to decouple Δ_contact from Δ_skel;
- partial correlations and Holm-corrected permutation tests;
- a self-noise baseline (6%);
- an equal-torque control, and a re-referencing control that answers the obvious preload objection.

My concerns are about what this evidence can support:

(a) Definition of ground truth. The "true" pad effect is defined as O's response with the new pad's contact stiffness on O's own geometry. For a thicker pad, geometry is part of the pad's effect. The paper therefore measures how well a redesign isolates the material change, which partly builds in the conclusion that the hand closest to O's contact geometry wins.

(b) No pad deforms. A study of compliant interfaces in which compliance is only a softer point contact or a hydroelastic layer has weak external validity, and the Limitations admit that deformation moves the contact point, which is exactly the quantity being preserved.

(c) The size of the correlation set is never given. The headline ρ = 0.94 and the Fig. 4 ρ values (0.43 and 0.53 for two of the four tasks) cannot be judged without n. "Pre-registered" cannot be verified (there is no registry or timestamp).

(d) The equal-torque column of Table I shows Coverage at 0% error (CoDe +8%) and small Coverage errors under the other commands. Most of the misreport therefore seems specific to position or impedance setpoint reuse, and re-referencing the setpoint removes about two-thirds of it (42 → 14%). This weakens the practical significance: a simple recalibration does most of the work. The policy argument ("no setpoint to re-reference") carries the rest, but it rests on two seeds with large seed variance (the cube Skeleton bars range from about 65% to 20%).

(e) The policy results are task-dependent by design (Skeleton wins the fingertip-end task). That is honest, but it narrows the claim to contact on the pads.

(f) No hardware. The "CoDe Hand" platform and the "modular finger" contribution are CAD only.

Overall this is a thoughtful, well-controlled simulation study with a clean conceptual message. The main claim is supported inside its simulation setting, but its significance and external validity are limited by the circular reference definition, non-deforming pads, missing sample sizes, and the absence of any physical prototype.

Presentation and clarity items:

1. Naming is inconsistent. Fig. 1(c,d) uses "Conventional" for the hand that Sec. IV calls "Coverage". Use one name throughout, or state "Conventional = Coverage" in the Fig. 1 legend itself, not only in the caption.
2. Δ_MCP is introduced in Sec. III-A ("defined in Eq. (7)") before its definition, and its name suggests a joint quantity, while it is actually the contact point's deviation in F_1. Consider a name such as Δ_loc, or define it where it is first used.
3. Eq. (6) writes L_f(M, q), but Sec. III-B says every design keeps the same extended length L_f. Clarify whether L_f depends on q, or drop the arguments.
4. Table I: the "convex" columns are not explained. Is this a MuJoCo/Drake contact-solver option or an object shape? The blank cells (e.g., Coverage "compl. pad" under Drake point) should be marked "n/a" with a reason. Bold is used for CoDe's entries without a stated meaning.
5. Fig. 4: "ranks averaged" across the two contact models is unclear, and n is missing. The ρ for Δ_contact vs. Δ_skel would read better as a separate small row with n.
6. Fig. 3(c): the y-axis "contact motion kept (%)" runs down to −100%, and negative "kept" percentages are hard to interpret. Explain in the panel itself that below 0 means worse than a fixed axis. The caption's parenthetical about at-joint designs starting from a different contact point is important and deserves a sentence in the main text.
7. Sec. III-D cites "10 conditions where both are feasible" and "all nine conditions" in consecutive paragraphs. State explicitly which conditions are excluded and why (e.g., 3 mm references excluded from preservation).
8. Sec. IV-A "Measure" is very dense: D, H, E, signed error, the "not circular" argument and pre-registration all sit in one paragraph. Splitting it and adding a one-line formula for E would help.
9. The abstract packs in many claims (pole analysis, design study, ρ, two simulators, preload, reversal). The phrase "a fixed axis above the pad's mount" is hard to parse without the body. Consider simplifying.
10. Fig. 2(a): the grey band "contact faces, 3–9 mm pads" and the CoDe region reaching +30 mm with a ceiling artifact at the top are hard to read. Indicate what the region boundaries represent.
11. Fig. 1(d) reports changes in median loads, while Table I uses medians of per-placement errors. The text notes this, but a reader comparing −39% vs. −41% with Table I's 0% will be confused. Put the note in the Fig. 1 caption.
12. Fig. 5: put the "palm shift" quantity under the cube task in the same visual format as Δ_contact and Δ_skel, and state seed-level numbers or error bars.
13. Several sentences are long and rely on colons and semicolons (e.g., the Introduction's "It sounds simple, and its natural answer, ... is wrong"). Shorter sentences would improve readability.
14. Define the "grid" of redesigns (Sec. IV-B1, "over the grid redesigns") where it first appears in the Setup.
