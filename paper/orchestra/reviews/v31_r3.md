ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: mechanism kinematics. I checked the lemma, propositions, corollary and bounds line by line, then the design optimization and whether the experimental claims follow from them.

Central claims. (1) A remote-actuated crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute (2R-DD) at every thickness and flexion. (2) A redesign for a thicker pad should keep the contact-surface motion c(q), the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-face frame, rather than the skeleton. A fixed axis cannot do this (closed-form bound), and the CFL gets to 10-51% of that bound. (3) Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect is predicted by Delta_contact and not by Delta_skel.

Math check (re-derived):
- Lemma 1. The fixed point of y -> e^{iq}y + b is b/(1-e^{iq}) = (b/2)(1 + i cot(q/2)). The extracted text reads "2b(1 + i cot q/2)". If the typeset version is b/2, it is fine. If it is 2b, it is wrong by a factor of 4.
- Prop. 1. Correct. Under S1-S2, rotation by q about P equals reflection in the line through P tilted q/2 from the normal. The tilted line descends away from the joint, so the joint-side face corner is the binding point, and G >= 2(t - z) tan(q/2) follows. Unequal pad lengths do not break the argument. The proposition implicitly needs t - z >= 0. For a pin on the link line, 2 tan 60 deg = 3.46 mm/mm checks out.
- Prop. 2. Correct. The chord between the phalanx ends after rotation about a pole at depth z is G cos(q/2) + 2z sin(q/2). The "same amount" statement checks: the denominator grows by 2 delta tan(q_req/2) at q_req, and by less at smaller q, so the conclusion holds for the averaged objective too.
- Prop. 3 and Eq. (7). Correct: ||(I - R_q) Delta p|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Delta p||. Eq. (8) is a sound triangle inequality between the face frame and the link-line frame. Corollary 1 is correct for an axis on the link line. The rolling-joint remark is also correct: for two equal rollers the finite pole sits at depth r tan(q/4), and I confirmed this.
- The numbers in Fig. 3d-g agree with Eq. (9): 8.49, 10.39 and 11.6 mm at 9 mm, and the CFL at 10-51% of the bound.

Where the claims run ahead of the math:
(a) The paper calls Delta_contact a Jacobian measure, but it compares positions, not Jacobians. J = [c'; 1] depends only on c'(q). Delta_contact is a max-norm on c(q) and deliberately includes the start offset c0. Two designs with identical Jacobian columns but a shifted c0 get a nonzero Delta_contact. Conversely, the paper's own mechanism for the error is preload (E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1|), which depends on where the face sits at q0 and on the lever arm at q0, not on the whole path over [0, q_req]. The "contact Jacobian" framing is therefore stronger than what the metric and the evidence support. A split of Delta_contact into a placement term c(q0) and a Jacobian term c'(q0), each correlated with E, would settle this.
(b) Three pole objects are used interchangeably. Lemma 1 and Prop. 3 use the finite-displacement pole P(q) (from q = 0). The Jacobian sentence uses the instantaneous centre p_hat. "Why the CFL" and Fig. 2b mix them, and the Fig. 2a caption plots the instantaneous centre. These coincide only for fixed axes.
(c) The intro says "Only a joint whose pole moves can" keep the motion. By Prop. 3, what is needed is a pole path that can be translated by Delta_t in the link-line frame, below the pad mount. That is a packaging constraint (a virtual pole), not motion of the pole with q. Sec. III-B3 states this correctly; the intro does not.
(d) Prop. 2 says coverage rises until the pole reaches the face. Yet the CFL optima stay 3.6-4.6 mm above the face, and the paper does not say which constraint stops them (transmission angle, link interference, servo body). Without that, the law describes an ideal but does not explain the optima. "CFL ideal" and "RCJ ideal" in Fig. 3 are never defined. At 3 mm, 2R-remote exceeds the "CFL ideal" curve, so it is unclear what that curve bounds. Fig. 2c's virtual-pole plane (~0.85-0.9) also does not match Fig. 3's ~0.8.
(e) Reflected stiffness. K0/(dtheta/dq)^2 drops the tau_theta * d^2theta/dq^2 term. Under preload with the CFL's nonlinear transmission, that term changes the effective joint stiffness in the grasp, and Delta_contact may be picking up part of this effect.
(f) The Corollary 1 premise "a pin cannot sit below the pad's mount" is a modelling convention: the axis is on the link line by definition. The bound is a statement about that convention. A pin lowered within the phalanx with a deeper mount trades room, and this is not explored. RCJ beats the bound once its roller may grow, which shows how much the bound depends on what is held fixed.

Evidence. The coverage comparison (10/10 conditions, +2.4 to +14.6 pp) is clean and well posed. The redesign result is a correct application of the bound. The simulation correlations are consistent across two simulators, two contact models and three tasks, and use pre-registration, Holm correction and partial correlations.

Two points limit what follows from the experiments. First, the redesign set is constructed by the authors to span Delta_contact, and Eq. (8) forces Delta_contact and Delta_skel to trade off, so the candidates are collinear by design. The partial correlations help, but with 14-16 hands the lead over Delta_skel is not significant, as the paper admits. Second, the residual error of the contact-preserving redesign R is 17-41% in Isaac Lab and up to 104% at 9 mm with thickness-matched soft pads. "Studied independently of the finger" is therefore too strong. The defensible claim is "less dependent, and ranked correctly". The reversal example gives U's figure (+48% vs -41%) but not R's.

Scores rest on: correct and elegant planar kinematics (Lemma 1, Props. 1-3, Cor. 1); a sound and useful coverage result; and a contact-motion claim whose metric and naming do not quite match the Jacobian story, with a residual error that undercuts "independently". The work is simulation only, with hardware deferred.
