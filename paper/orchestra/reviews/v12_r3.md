THOUGHT:

Intuition. The paper asks a real and under-discussed methodological question: when a thicker contact interface (compliant pad, tactile module) forces a finger redesign, what should be held constant so that a measured difference can be attributed to the interface? The answer is "how the contact surfaces move relative to each other," expressed in the proximal-phalanx frame. Because the distal rotation equals q on every design, this reduces to the path of one distal-face point, c(q), which is set by the pole of the distal phalanx. I like the framing. The pole analysis is clean and the simulation is organized around falsifiable claims, which suits an RSS Stage-1 abstract. My concerns fall into four groups: (i) the mathematics is elementary, and some of the "bound" language oversells it; (ii) the simulation design may favour Δ_contact by construction; (iii) the practical significance depends on a particular, naive experimental protocol; (iv) the writing is very compressed, so several definitions and figure annotations are hard to follow.

Checking the theory.
- Lemma 1 (finite pole) is textbook (Bottema & Roth), and it is cited as such.
- Prop. 3, ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Δp||, is just ||(I − R_q)Δp||. It is correct but one line. Eq. (7) follows from the triangle inequality, because the two frames differ by a palmar translation Δt. Corollary 1 then holds trivially for a fixed axis, since the pole is the axis. Calling C1 "a bound" with "a closed-form lower bound" is accurate but sounds weightier than it is. The real insight is qualitative: a physical pin must sit above the pad's mounting surface, so a pole that follows a deeper face must be virtual. That argument is nice and should be the stated headline of C1.
- Prop. 2: I checked the claim that lowering the pole by δ shrinks G by 2δ tan(q_req/2) and lengthens the denominator by the same amount at q_req. dD = 2δ tan(q/2)(1 − cos(q/2)) + 2δ sin(q/2) = 2δ tan(q/2). It holds. Then (N + a)/(D + a) > N/D when N < D. The argument is only evaluated at q_req, while C̄_pad averages three poses, and S1/S2 (equal depths, symmetric corners) are strong. The conclusion "the best joint turns about a point on the contact face" should be qualified accordingly.
- Prop. 1's proof sketch relies on the mirror construction, which needs S1/S2. Fine as a sketch.
- Fig. 2 numbers check out: 5 tan 60° = 8.66, 9 tan 60° = 15.6, 4 tan 60° = 6.9. Fig. 4 percentages also check out: 0.35/3.46 ≈ 10%, 4.31/8.49 ≈ 51%.

Definitional issue with Δ_contact. c(q) is "the point on the distal face below the fingertip." If a redesign changes the distal length, c0 shifts along the face line while the face, as a surface, has not moved. Δ_contact then registers a change that is partly a relabelling of which point is tracked. Fig. 4d shows exactly this: RCJ starts 9.3 mm off at q = 0, and the text says 2R-DD and RCJ "start apart because their contact point moved along the face." Much of the CFL's advantage over RCJ and 2R-DD in Study 2 is therefore start offset. The paper concedes this: without the offset, CFL is best in 7 of 9 cells. The metric should separate motion normal to the face (which changes where the object is met) from tangential relabelling. Alternatively, the optimizer should pin c0 for all architectures.

Study 1. CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 shared conditions, but 2R-DD is the weakest physical baseline. 2R-remote beats CFL at 90° and at thin pads at 120°. C2's failure condition is phrased only against the servo-at-joint finger, which makes it easy to pass. RCJ (physical) is far below RCJ ideal, and the paper never explains why; presumably the joint actuator body costs it. Mechanical advantage is unconstrained, which matters for a servo hand: a CFL redesign that keeps contact motion by distorting the transmission could lose grip force. Three GA seeds is thin, and no seed variance is shown.

Study 2. The fixed-axis bound is met by construction for 2R-remote, because it keeps its mount depth. Beating the bound is therefore a low bar for C3 ("fails if it reaches the bound"). A stronger test would compare against what an unconstrained fixed axis can do by also re-choosing its mount depth or axis location under the same packaging rules. At 150°, 2R-remote exceeds the bound (13.8, 22.9 mm, "degenerate"). That is allowed for a lower bound, but it should be explained in one sentence. The useful quantitative result is that CFL stays at 10–51% of the bound while losing ≤0.2 points of coverage. 51% at 90°/9 mm is not "keeping" the motion. The abstract's phrase "keeps the change within 10–51%" is awkward and slightly spun.

Study 3 (the core empirical claim, C4).
- Placement frame. The cylinder is placed where O touches it, measured along the proximal face, which is the origin of F_p, the frame in which Δ_contact is defined. In the hand-independent grid, the placements are again along the proximal face. A hand whose c(q) differs from O's will, by construction, meet that cylinder at a different command and over-close. So Δ_contact predicting the reused-command error is close to built into the protocol. The proximal pad is a reasonable anchor when the object rests on it, but this is a modelling choice that drives the result. A placement anchored to the palm or to the skeleton is the natural control, and it would show whether Δ_contact still wins or Δ_skel takes over.
- Candidate confounding. The redesign set is stratified at set Δ_contact, keeping or changing the skeleton "as much as possible." Eq. (7) forces a trade-off between Δ_contact and Δ_skel at fixed thickness, so the negative ρ for Δ_skel (−0.40, −0.51) is partly induced by the sampling design. Thickness and coverage are near-constant within strata, so their weak ρ is unsurprising. The partial correlation (0.88 given Δ_skel) helps. The claim "the only candidate whose interval excludes zero in every setting" is still weaker evidence than it sounds, with 14–16 hands and designed covariates.
- Protocol dependence and significance. Under reused torque, errors fall to 3–40%. Closing from each finger's own touch leaves 20%, and matching contact force leaves 3–7%. The large errors (50–160%) are therefore mostly over-closure under a stiff position command. The paper should argue clearly why a designer should redesign hardware instead of adopting a contact-sensing protocol. It is a strength that the authors report this, and R still roughly halves U's error under reused torque (5/17/12 vs 9/28/23%), but these are small absolute differences on 3 hands each.
- Generality. All Study-3 redesigns are CFL, plus one fixed-axis contrast. The predictor is never tested across joint types, which is what the principle claims to be about.
- Possible inconsistency. The text says "R errs least at each thickness." In Fig. 1d / Fig. 5a, the 9 mm redesign at Δ_contact ≈ 3.5 mm sits at ≈39%, visually at or below R's 41%. Please confirm.
- The normalization E = |Δ(H) − Δ(O)|/|Δ(O)| inflates when Δ(O) is small. Report Δ(O) per interface change and setting.
- "Three CFL fingers" with a cylinder "free in the sagittal plane" resting on "each hand's proximal pad" is unclear. Is this one finger per trial, or three fingers in parallel?
- Pads of every thickness share one contact model. This isolates geometry, which is the point, but it also removes exactly what makes thick interfaces different. The authors acknowledge this for Stage 2.

Writing. The paper is dense to the point of opacity in places. Examples:
- The title is long, with two subtitles.
- The abstract phrase "keeps the change within 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound at near-best contact surface."
- Undefined or late-defined symbols: x0, T_i, S1/S2, and "near-best contact surface."
- Fig. 3's caption "(0.21, 0)."
- The C4 failure criterion is inverted. It should fail if Δ_contact does not predict, or if Δ_skel predicts as well. The current wording, "if another quantity predicts in every setting," is an odd test.
- "Contact Jacobian" is used for one column in the proximal frame, which is non-standard. It is defined, but readers from grasping (Montana, Murray–Li–Sastry) will expect something else.

Positives: the hypotheses carry explicit failure conditions, the Stage-2 protocol is concrete and sensibly restricts the physical test to R vs U (equal pads), the figures are clean, and the fixed-axis-keeps-skeleton contrast (95–228% error) is a memorable demonstration that skeleton preservation is the wrong target.

Related work is minimal but focused, as the venue asks. Montana-style contact kinematics and the prosthetic/finger centrode literature would be directly relevant to the pole argument.

Overall. This is a coherent Stage-1 submission with a clear thesis, correct (if simple) analysis, and preliminary simulation evidence. The key empirical claim could be partly an artifact of the placement frame and of how the redesign set was sampled, and its practical relevance depends on a naive protocol. I lean slightly positive because the Stage-1 bar requires fleshed-out hypotheses and methods, which the paper delivers. Overall 6, Accept, with clear requests.
