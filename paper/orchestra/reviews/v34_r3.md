ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper argues that when a robot finger is redesigned to carry a thicker or different contact interface, the motion of the contact surfaces, not the motion of the skeleton, is what should be kept the same. It makes three claims.

(1) Kinematic analysis (Sec. III-A). The finite-displacement pole (Eq. 1) controls both the clearance between adjacent pads (Eq. 2) and the deviation of the contact-point path (Eq. 3). No redesign can preserve both the contact-surface motion and the skeleton motion when the pad depth changes (Eq. 4), and a fixed axis held at the same depth has a lower bound on its contact-path deviation (Eq. 5). These results are short, correct as far as I can check (for example, 2 tan 60° = 3.46 mm/mm, and |I - R_q| = 2 sin(q/2) gives Eqs. 3-5), and they frame the problem well. The tools themselves are classical planar kinematics; what is new is applying them to contact interfaces. One point is unresolved. Eq. 5 assumes that the axis stays at the same depth. The paper never explains why a revolute redesign cannot simply move its axis down by Δt. That would give Δ_contact ≈ 0 at the cost of Δ_skel. The phrase "a fixed axis above the pad's mount" hints at a structural reason, but it is not stated. Without it, the claim that a fixed axis cannot preserve the motion reads as stronger than what is shown.

(2) Design studies (Sec. III-D). The coverage claim is supported but should be read narrowly. The CFL beats the servo-at-joint 2R-DD in all 10 feasible conditions by 2.4-14.6 points. However, 2R-remote matches or beats the CFL at 90° and up to 5 mm at 120° (Fig. 3a,b), so the articulation itself only clearly pays off at deep flexion and with thick pads. The abstract correctly compares against a servo-at-joint finger only. The redesign claim is stronger. The CFL stays at 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound, its whole Pareto front dominates the other fronts at 120°, 3 to 7 mm, and its advantage mostly survives when initial-position differences are excluded (7 of 9 conditions). Two caveats:
- The CFL has far more design freedom (four pivots plus the fingertip) than the 2R and RCJ variants, so "pole freedom" and "more parameters" are partly confounded.
- The optimizer is run with three seeds, but no seed-to-seed spread is reported.

The coverage metric (an inscribed cylinder, averaged at three angles) is tied to cylindrical power grasps.

(3) Simulation evaluation (Sec. IV). Under a reused joint impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect follows Δ_contact (Spearman ρ 0.67-0.88 across two simulators, point and hydroelastic contact, and three tasks) and not Δ_skel. The design that creates independent variation in Δ_contact and Δ_skel is good. So are the partial correlations, the controls (coverage, thickness and transmission do not predict the error), the Holm correction and the pre-registered ρ = 0.94. My main concern is that the core result is close to mechanically expected. Under a joint-position impedance controller, the preload depends on where the pad face sits at the commanded angle, which is essentially Δ_contact near q0. The authors partly address this: the partial ρ of 0.69 with the face offset held fixed suggests the full path matters, not just the face position at the grasp. Still, the finding is better presented as a quantified design rule than as a surprising discovery. The scope also matters a great deal. With re-zeroing at touch, the error follows the skeleton (ρ 0.88). With ideal torque control, it follows both. The third contribution bullet ("preserving contact-surface motion, rather than skeleton motion, reduces the influence of redesign") is worded more generally than these results allow. It holds only under the reused-setpoint protocol, which the Conclusion and Sec. IV state correctly. The headline reversal (+48% vs. -41%) is real but comes from U, a design optimized for coverage only, which is an extreme case. The more convincing statement is that skeleton-preserving redesigns also reverse the sign. In Fig. 4(b), R itself reports -55% against a true -41%, a sizable relative error. All evidence is from simulation. Point contact on 7-9 mm soft pads is a coarse model, although the hydroelastic replication helps.

Clarity is reasonable in Secs. I-III but drops in Sec. IV:
- The results are very compressed and use undefined terms ("a face that advances", "the grid", "O's placements").
- Table I mixes settings, with dashes where no partial correlation is available.
- Several legend items in Fig. 3 ("CFL ideal", "RCJ ideal") are explained only in the caption.

For an extended abstract, the hypotheses and methods are complete and the preliminary evidence is substantial. My scores rest on a well-posed and useful framing, sound kinematics and a careful simulation study. They are held down by the near-expected nature of the main effect, the narrow protocol it holds under, the confound between design freedom and pole freedom, and the lack of hardware.
