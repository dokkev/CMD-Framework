ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and evidence:

1. **Analysis (Sec. III-A).** When a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface, it cannot keep both its contact-surface motion and its skeleton motion (Eq. 4). A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion from the same initial contact point (Eq. 5).
   - The derivation is short and, as far as I checked, correct. P(q)=b/(1−e^{iq}) is the standard finite-displacement pole. The pole coordinates in F_p and F_1 differ by the frame offset, whose change is Δt, so ‖Δp‖+‖Δp₁‖ ≥ |Δt| follows from the triangle inequality. The figure 3.46 = 2 tan 60° checks out.
   - The bound is tight only when both designs share their initial contact point (in F_p) and their initial fingertip (in F_1). The paper says this for Eq. 5 but not clearly for Eq. 4.
   - The result is elegant but elementary. Its value lies in the framing, not in technical depth.

2. **Design (Sec. III-C/D).** The CFL gives more room than an at-joint finger and preserves contact motion best.
   - The room claim is supported against the at-joint finger (2.4–14.6 pp).
   - Against the remote (belt) baseline the advantage is small or absent at 90° and 120° up to 5 mm. At 7 mm/120° it is 73% vs. 70%. So much of the "room" benefit comes from moving the servo, not from the articulation.
   - The preservation claim (Fig. 3c) is clear and convincing within the planar, PIP-only model.

3. **Main empirical claim (Sec. IV).** Under a reused impedance setpoint, the error in the measured pad effect tracks Δ_d^p, not Δ_skel.
   - The decisive control is the Skeleton redesign. It keeps the fingertip path (0.3 mm) better than CoDe keeps the contact path (1.1 mm), yet errs far more (E 222% vs. 10%). That is the strongest evidence in the paper and the right experiment.
   - Partial correlations controlling for Δ_skel and for contact-force deviation help rule out simple confounds.
   - The result is also close to expected. Under joint impedance with rigid contact, the contact force on the object is set by where the contact points sit and how they move under the setpoint. Keeping that path the same should keep the response the same. The paper's contribution is to show this cleanly and to show that the skeleton is the wrong invariant, which is useful but modest.

4. **Policies (Sec. IV-B2).** A policy transfers to the redesign that keeps the motion its task uses. This is the weakest part.
   - There are three tasks, two seeds, and a different predictor per task (Δ_d^p, Δ_skel, palm shift), each identified after the fact.
   - On the relay dial CoDe is not the best hand.
   - Pad walking does not transfer to Drake even on O.
   - The ρ = −0.98 over 14 redesigns for the cube is striking, but it rests on a post-hoc predictor.

What my scores rest on:

- **All evidence is simulated, and no pad deforms.** A "thicker, compliant pad" is a softer point contact or a thicker hydroelastic layer. The headline reversal (Fig. 1d: O −41%, CoDe −39%, Coverage +48%) is in a point-contact MuJoCo-Warp model.
  - That a softer, thicker pad lowers pull-out load on O is itself counter to physical intuition and to the cited soft-fingertip literature. It suggests the "pad effect" being preserved may be a model artifact.
  - A paper titled "Design of a Contact-Centric Robot Hand" at RSS needs at least one physical measurement on the CAD-shown hand. Examples: pull-out with two real pads on O vs. CoDe vs. Coverage prints, or measured contact-point paths.
- **The reference counterfactual is not physical.** O "carries the new pad's contact stiffness on its own geometry", so O can never actually carry a 7 mm pad. The paper treats any geometric contribution of a thicker pad as error. That is defensible for attributing material effects, but it should be argued: practitioners comparing interfaces may want the geometric effect included.
- **Statistics are thinly reported.**
  - ρ type (Spearman or Pearson) is not stated.
  - Redesigns come from related optimization families, so they are not independent.
  - e_H divides by |D_O|, which inflates errors when D_O is small (388%, 254%).
  - "Pre-registered" is asserted without any description of where or what. An anonymized statement of the registered hypothesis and threshold would suffice.
- **Table I has caveats the text glosses.**
  - Under "torque", Coverage's median (0%) is closer to zero than CoDe's (+8%).
  - Under "touch" they are close (−17% vs. −14%).
  - CoDe reaches ±18% in several settings and 38% at 9 mm. "Near 10% up to 7 mm" is accurate only for E in Isaac Lab.
- **"Contact Jacobian" is a loose name for what is measured.** Δ_d^p is a maximum path (position) deviation of one contact point, not a Jacobian difference. Identical paths from identical c0 imply identical Jacobians, but small Δ_d^p does not bound the Jacobian difference.

Overall: a well-motivated, nicely framed and internally careful simulation study with a clean central control (Skeleton). But it has no hardware validation, uses non-deforming pad models for claims about compliant pads, and the policy evidence is post hoc and limited. It is close to the bar, but I lean reject for RSS.

PRESENTATION AND CLARITY ITEMS:

1. **Abstract wording.** "A redesign for coverage alone reports that a thicker, more compliant pad raises the pull-out load" reads oddly: a redesign does not "report". Rephrase, e.g., "on a coverage-only redesign the same command measures a rise in pull-out load where the original hand measures a fall."
2. **Hand names are used before they are defined.** O, CoDe, Skeleton and Coverage appear in Fig. 1 and Sec. I and are defined only in a sentence at the end of the contributions. CoDe is pinned down only in Sec. III-D. Introduce the four hands once, early and explicitly; consider a small table listing each hand's objective and its Δ_d^p / Δ_skel / C̄_pad at 7 mm.
3. **"CoDe" names three things.** It is the design philosophy, the hand platform ("CoDe Hand", which includes O), and the specific contact-preserving redesign. Readers will confuse "the CoDe Hand" (Fig. 1a) with "CoDe" the redesign. Use distinct names.
4. **"Contact Jacobian" vs. Δ_d^p.** The paper calls the preserved quantity a contact Jacobian but measures a max path deviation. State explicitly that equal paths from equal c0 imply equal Jacobians, and that Δ_d^p is the proxy actually used.
5. **Three related symbols.** Δ_d^p, Δ_d^1 and Δ_skel appear together, and Eq. (9) uses Δ_d^1, which is neither "contact motion" nor "skeleton". A short sentence or diagram showing the three deviations on Fig. 2(b) would help. The contributions say no redesign "keeps both" contact and skeleton motion, while Eq. (9) keeps Δ_d^p and Δ_d^1; clarify that Δ_d^1 is not the skeleton.
6. **Eq. (3)/(4) assumptions.** State explicitly that Eq. (4) assumes shared initial contact point (F_p) and shared initial fingertip (F_1). The paper says "for two designs with the same initial contact point" for Eq. (3), but the fingertip version needs the same condition.
7. **Sec. III-A heading order.** "A. Pole" is laid out below text that already begins Sec. III, and "B. Finger Architecture" follows mid-column. In the PDF, the subsection text about the pole begins before its heading in reading order. Check float placement so the subsections read in order.
