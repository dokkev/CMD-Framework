THOUGHT:
The paper argues that comparisons of contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, vision-based tactile modules) on robot fingers are confounded because carrying a different interface forces a finger redesign. It proposes holding the "contact-centric kinematics" c(q), the motion of the distal contact face seen from the proximal face, fixed across interfaces. It then analyses the joint-pad conflict through the classical finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement. This is a Stage-1 RSS submission, so I judge it on whether the problem, hypotheses and methodology are fully worked out, and on whether the work yields insight even if the results come out negative. Preliminary results are acceptable.

What I like. The finite-pole lens is simple and unifying. Props. 2-4 and Cor. 1 are correct as far as I can check: |I - R_q| = 2 sin(q/2) gives Eq. (4), and the slope 2 tan(q*/2) gives 2.00/3.46/7.46 mm per mm. Together they turn an informal packaging intuition ("thick pads fight the joint") into closed-form laws that apply to any planar joint. The paper is unusually honest. It reports where its own design loses (2R-remote leads at 90 deg; the 120 deg crossover is within CFL run spread), states falsification branches for each hypothesis, and records the mechanical-advantage change rather than hiding it. Checking the analysis against the evaluator (agreement within 1e-13, SAT test agreement within 1e-9) is good practice. Table I and Fig. 4 are internally consistent with the stated bound (for example 2 sin 60 deg x 2 = 3.46).

Where I am unconvinced.

(1) The central premise is asserted, not shown. The paper's motivation is that embodiment changes (Dc, skeleton motion, MA) confound interface measurements, but no evidence or planned experiment quantifies how much a given Dc, say 1-4 mm, matters for any grasp or sensing outcome. The paper itself declines to set a Dc tolerance and defers this to H3. Without it, "CFL keeps the contact path at 10-51% of the bound" cannot be called preservation or non-preservation. 51% of the fixed-axis bound at 90 deg and 4.3 mm at 9 mm look like substantial changes. That makes H2's "supported, partially" hard to falsify. The Stage-2 plan should include a direct test of the premise, for example the same interface on two fingers with deliberately different Dc to measure the outcome sensitivity.

(2) H2 is partly tautological as framed. Cor. 1 proves that a fixed axis cannot beat the bound at a given Delta-d, so "only a joint that moves its pole" is a theorem, not a hypothesis. The empirical content is how far below the bound a pole-moving joint gets, and whether it can do so without losing coverage. That is worth stating directly. The comparison also mixes references, since each architecture is measured against its own 3 mm design. 2R-DD and RCJ reach below the Delta-d = 0 line by remounting, which muddies the "same rules" comparison.

(3) The architecture comparison is biased toward CFL. (a) The search is asymmetric: CFL alone gets re-seeded searches, and the text says "extra effort can only raise CFL". Several key differences (the 120 deg crossover, gaps of a few points) are of the same order as the CFL spread of 1.3-3.4 points. (b) MA is unconstrained. CFL runs at MA_wc of about 0.42-0.56 against 1 for 2R, so CFL can trade transmission for coverage, and the headline "CFL 0.739 vs 0.593" at 150 deg is not like-for-like. (c) Belt and link occlusion and other-layer collisions are not counted, which favours 2R-remote and CFL in ways that are not quantified. At minimum, I would want a run with an MA floor and equal search effort per architecture.

(4) The coverage objective is idiosyncratic. It counts the overlapping face interval at which an inscribed cylinder touches both faces, averaged over only three poses and normalized by contour length. It is neither motivated nor validated against grasp capability, and the stated limitations list "cylinder coverage". Since H1's conclusions depend on this metric (Eq. (3) explains the 120 deg split purely through the contour terms), a sensitivity check is needed, for example with raw pad length (l1+l2)/Lf, which Fig. 1 also reports, or with a denser Q_eval.

(5) The rigid-face abstraction ignores what thick interfaces actually do. Compliant pads and gel sensors deform, so the effective contact point moves into and along the face as the load changes. The paper acknowledges that contact mechanics differ by interface. However, keeping the c(q) of the undeformed face may not be the right invariant for soft interfaces, and this should be discussed.

(6) H3, the hypothesis most relevant to RSS, is thin. "Two to three interfaces", "e.g." choices, an unspecified motion-tracking method, an unspecified grasp protocol, trial counts and statistics, and a success criterion of agreement "within repeatability". For Stage 1 the methodology is supposed to be fully worked out. As written, H3 is closer to an intention than a protocol.

(7) Clarity. The paper is very dense: many acronyms, unexplained notation (T_i, X, x0, S1/S2), and two coverage metrics shown side by side in Fig. 1 (percent labels versus the caption's C_pad). The S1-S2 assumptions are violated at the optima by up to 3.4 mm, so Eqs. (2)-(3) are only estimates. Table I is hard to parse.

(8) Related work. It cites only directly relevant literature, which suits the format. Missing: Montana's contact kinematics, since "contact-centric kinematics" invites comparison; prior anthropomorphic and polycentric finger designs that deliberately place the instantaneous centre; and tactile fingers with swappable fingertips or sensor modules.

Overall, the work is an interesting, well-executed analytic contribution with honest preliminary computation. Its value for an interface-study community hinges on a premise it has not yet tested, and the H3 protocol is underspecified for Stage 1. The CFL-favouring search and the unconstrained MA weaken H1/H2. Borderline. Because the format values well-posed hypotheses and the theory is clean, I lean slightly negative but could be persuaded if the protocol were sharpened.
