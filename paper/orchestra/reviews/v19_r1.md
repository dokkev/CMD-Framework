THOUGHT:
I read this as someone who has built servo-chain hands and compared pads on them. The paper raises two practical problems I have run into myself. First, in a servo-at-joint finger, the servo body takes up the space a thick proximal pad would need. Second, when you swap in a thicker pad, the hand changes too, so a measured difference mixes the effect of the pad with the effect of the redesign. Both problems are real and seldom stated this clearly. The pole-based framing (Lemma 1, Prop. 1-3, Cor. 1) is a compact way to tie the two problems together. A fixed axis has its pole pinned, so its gap grows as 2 tan(q/2) per mm of face depth. A crossed four-bar can move its virtual pole toward the face. That argument is clean and correct as far as I can check it.

C1 (room for thick pads) is well supported in simulation. The CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions by 2.4-14.6 points. The ablation against 2R-remote is useful because it splits the gain into "remote actuation" and "polycentric joint". It also shows that most of the CFL's advantage over a belt-driven fixed axis appears only at 120-150 deg. My main concern as a builder is what the paper leaves unconstrained: mechanical advantage (worst case 0.41-0.58), backlash and friction in the crossed links, link stiffness and wear, and the space the links themselves need near the pads. Halving fingertip force on a geared hobby servo matters for payload. The authors list this as a limitation, but C1 would be stronger if mechanical advantage were a constraint, or if the Pareto cost were plotted.

C2 (keep the contact Jacobian) is the more novel claim and the less settled one. The evidence is Spearman rho = 0.80-0.86 between Delta_contact and the error E of a reused impedance setpoint. This uses 14-45 redesigns, one contact model, one task (a cylinder held between proximal and distal pads, pulled in four directions) and simulation only. Several of the sharpest numbers are post hoc and labeled as such: rho = -0.99 with the face's signed normal offset, and the partial correlations. I appreciate the labeling and the pre-registered 27-design grid (rho = 0.94). Even so, the headline "an interface can be studied independently of the finger" is stronger than the data. At 9 mm the CFL still has Delta_contact = 2.3 mm and errs 41%. So the recommended architecture does not fully deliver C2 across the tested range, and the 1 mm tolerance is an empirical fit to this one task. The claim also depends on the reused-setpoint protocol. The authors show that torque or touch protocols remove most of the error, at the cost of sensing. A tactile-hand practitioner would ask whether the problem is better solved with that sensing than by kinematic redesign. The paper answers this only briefly (servo hands rarely have it).

The term "contact Jacobian" is used narrowly. It means the PIP column at a distal-face point in the proximal frame, and the MCP column is explicitly not kept. That is defensible, but the title and abstract suggest more. The Fig. 1d reversal (a softer pad "holds 48% more when it holds 41% less") is a striking hook, but it is one pad pair, one setpoint and one task.

For a Stage 1 abstract, the problem, hypotheses (H1-H3), methodology and Stage 2 hardware protocol are laid out. The protocol uses swappable pad modules and CFL links on one finger, markers for Delta_contact, a planar slide, and a Page trend test. It is concrete and falsifiable, and it states what failure would look like. That meets the venue's bar. Presentation is the weakest part. The text is very dense, the abstract is packed with numbers, Fig. 2 and Fig. 4 are hard to read at print size, and many symbols are introduced quickly. Related work leaves out the underactuated-finger literature (e.g., Birglen/Gosselin-style analysis of linkage-driven fingers and contact-force distribution over phalanges), which bears directly on contact location in linkage fingers.

Overall: a solid, original framing with a correct small theory and a convincing simulation for C1. C2 is a promising but preliminary claim that rests on one model and one task. Hardware validation is planned and well specified. I lean accept for Stage 1.
