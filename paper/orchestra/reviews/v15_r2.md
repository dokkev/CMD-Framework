THOUGHT:

I am reading this as a contact-mechanics and experimental-methodology reviewer. The paper has two parts. C1 is a kinematic design claim: remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP leaves more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint finger, supported by GA/NSGA-II searches and closed-form clearance/coverage results (Props. 1-2). C2 is a methodological claim about interface experiments: when a finger is redesigned for a thicker interface, the quantity to preserve is the relative motion of the contact faces (Delta_contact, the PIP column of the contact Jacobian in the proximal-face frame), not the skeleton. It is supported by a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1, Eq. 8), a Study 2 redesign search showing the CFL stays at 10-51% of that bound, and a Study 3 simulation in which the error in a measured interface effect (change in limit load for a friction, stiffness or crown change) tracks Delta_contact.

My overall view: the geometry is clean and, as far as I can check, correct. Eq. 6 follows from the finite pole, Eq. 7 from the triangle inequality, and the 2 tan(q/2) clearance law is intuitive and useful. The Stage-1 requirements are mostly met: each hypothesis has a stated failure condition, the statistics are more careful than usual for this venue (one-sided permutation tests, hand-level bootstrap, Holm correction, pre-fixed thresholds, robustness sweeps over solver parameters), and the Stage-2 test is named. My concerns are about how far the Study 3 evidence supports C2 as a statement about real interface experiments. That is the claim the title and abstract sell.

Main concerns, in order:

(1) Nearly circular in simulation. Pads of every thickness share one contact model (a penalty/soft-constraint contact with a fixed 'contact stiffness'), the cylinder is free in the sagittal plane, and the MCP is held. Under those conditions a redesign with Delta_contact = 0 reproduces O's contact geometry relative to the object up to a rigid translation, so E(R) -> 0 almost by construction. The nontrivial content is the dose-response (error grows with Delta_contact) and the negative result for Delta_skel. That is worth having, but it is closer to a consistency check of the definition than an independent test. In real hardware the dominant change from a 3 mm to a 7 mm elastomer pad is mechanical: layer compliance, contact patch growth, pressure distribution, hysteresis. That change is outside the model. The authors acknowledge this for Stage 2 ('Real 3 and 7 mm pads differ in compliance'), but the abstract's phrasing ('the error in measured friction, stiffness and shape effects follows...') reads as broader than a rigid-geometry/penalty-contact result.

(2) Magnitude of Delta_contact against pad deformation. The R vs U differences are a few mm of face displacement. A 7 mm silicone pad under a grasp preload deforms by a comparable amount, and its effective contact point shifts along the face under tangential load. The rigid-face kinematic invariant may therefore be swamped by pad deformation in hardware. The paper should at least estimate pad indentation at the simulated grasp forces and compare it with Delta_contact.

(3) The mechanism is mostly preload, which is a known confound with a known fix. The paper shows the error runs through where the faces meet the object and hence the normal force: U presses 3.3x harder, and the distal force ratio follows Delta_contact. Reusing O's torque drops the error to 3-40%, and matching contact force leaves 3-7%. Careful interface studies (e.g., in the tactile-sensing literature) already control normal force. So the practical message 'keep the contact Jacobian' competes with 'control the normal force', and the paper's own numbers say the latter removes most of the error. The residual geometric effect at equal torque (R 5/17/12% vs U 9/28/23%) is the more interesting finding. It is post hoc and modest, and the abstract does not mention it.

(4) The predictor ranking is shaped by how the designs were sampled. The 15-16 hands per setting were constructed: R, U, and redesigns 'at set Delta_contact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible'. So Delta_contact was deliberately spread. Coverage was optimized to near-best, which restricts its range. Thickness has three levels with ties. Transmission is by design undone by the command table. Eq. 7 forces a negative Delta_contact-Delta_skel coupling at fixed thickness. Ranking Spearman rho across candidates whose ranges were set by the designer is not a fair horse race. The direct comparisons separate Delta_contact from the alternatives in only 7 of 12 bootstrap intervals, yet the abstract says it 'ranks first among five design quantities in every setting'. That is a point-estimate statement and should be qualified. The negative rho for Delta_skel (-0.40, -0.51) is likely a sampling artefact of Eq. 7 rather than evidence. The partial correlations help, but n = 15 with designed families is thin.

(5) Selection and normalization of E. E is a median over placements and directions 'where both hands hold', and only redesigns that hold the cylinder enter (15 of 18). Hands with large Delta_contact are the likeliest to drop out or lose placements, so both the hand-level and placement-level conditioning can bias rho. The relative error divides by |Delta(O)|. For the stiffness change (2.5 to 1.0 kN/m) Delta(O) may be small, which inflates relative errors. Absolute effect sizes, Delta(O) per interface change, and the number of retained placements per hand should be reported.

(6) Unclear role of the fixed-axis contrast family. It keeps Delta_skel = 0 with Delta_contact = sqrt(3) Delta_h. It is said to 'separate' Delta_skel, but its 95-228% errors are reported against its own 3 mm finger, not against O. I could not tell whether it is among the 15 hands of Table I. If it is, the reference differs. If it is not, the separation claim rests on a different experiment.

(7) The Stage-2 plan is concrete but weak as inference. There is one R and one U build at 7 mm. Placements x directions x repeats are pseudo-replicates of a two-hand comparison, so the paired Wilcoxon test tells us about two physical builds, not about the Delta_contact law. It also cannot separate geometry from build tolerances (link play, pad mounting). A stronger and cheap design is to mount O's 3 mm pad material on 4 mm rigid spacers in both R and U. That gives the same face depth with pad mechanics identical to O, which isolates geometry in hardware, and the true-thickness pads can then be added as a second factor. The C1 hardware test ('fails if an exchange needs the articulation taken apart') is nearly tautological for the design and does not test the coverage claim. Measuring coverage or contact area on the cylinder would.

(8) Scope and terminology. The setting is a two-phalanx planar pinch of a cylinder with the MCP held. Calling one column of relative face kinematics 'the contact Jacobian' is a stretch relative to Montana's contact kinematics, which include surface curvature and rolling. The tactile-sensor motivation (GelSight, DIGIT) is not exercised: the interfaces studied are friction, contact stiffness and crown, with no sensing. The CFL roughly halves mechanical advantage (0.41-0.58), which matters for grasp-force studies with fixed servos and should be weighed in C1.

(9) Clarity. The prose is very compressed. Settings ('O's contacts', 'grid', 'Torque') are defined in running text only. Table I mixes 14-45 hands without saying which designs. Figs. 2 and 5 are hard to read at print size. S1/S2 in Props. 1-2 are restrictive, and it is not stated how often the optimized CFL designs satisfy them (the 1.6 mm maximum gap error suggests not always). The three-seed GA results have no seed spread, so the 2.4-point minimum CFL margin over 2R-DD cannot be judged against optimizer variance.

Strengths to credit: an original framing, the pole as a single quantity that governs both room and motion, a useful closed-form bound for fixed axes, honest falsification criteria, solver robustness sweeps, a hand-independent grid control that addresses the most obvious circularity, a torque-reuse protocol that tests the mechanism, and a specific Stage-2 test. For a Stage-1 extended abstract, the methods are largely complete.

Decision reasoning: C1 is reasonably supported within its geometric model. C2's simulation support is internally careful, but it uses a contact model that deliberately removes the physics that makes thick pads matter, and the predictor ranking is partly baked in by the sampling. Stage 1 allows preliminary evidence, and the hypotheses and Stage-2 test are clearly stated, so I lean towards acceptance. The abstract's claims should be narrowed, and the Stage-2 design should be strengthened to separate geometry from pad mechanics. Overall 6, Accept, confidence 4.
