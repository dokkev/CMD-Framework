ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper makes three linked claims. (i) Analytical: where the finite-displacement pole of the PIP joint sits relative to the contact surfaces sets both the gap adjacent pads need (Eq. 2) and how much the contact-point path changes on redesign (Eq. 3). A change in contact-surface depth cannot preserve both the contact-point path and the fingertip path (Eq. 4), and a fixed axis at constant depth has a lower bound on contact-path deviation (Eq. 5). (ii) Design: a servo-remote crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute finger (2R-DD) in all 10 feasible conditions, and when redesigned from 3 mm to 5/7/9 mm pads it stays at 10-51% of the fixed-axis bound with almost no loss of coverage. (iii) Evaluation: when one impedance setpoint is reused across redesigns, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect (pull-out load change under a friction or stiffness swap) tracks Delta_contact and not Delta_skel. This holds in Isaac Lab (Newton / MuJoCo-Warp) and in Drake (point and hydroelastic contact) and for three tasks. A coverage-only redesign turns a -41% true effect into a reported +48%.

Evidence for (i) is clean and elementary. Eqs. 1-5 follow from planar finite-displacement kinematics and are stated with their assumptions (equal depth change on both phalanges, preserved initial positions). The 3.46 mm/mm figure (2 tan 60 deg) makes the trade-off concrete. The analysis is simple, but it reframes the problem usefully and motivates the mechanism choice. Fig. 2(c) supports the claim that the CFL can reach poles on the contact faces of thick pads, though the "95% of closing designs" envelope depends on how the design space was sampled, which is not described.

Evidence for (ii) is good but narrower than the abstract implies. The coverage advantage is stated against 2R-DD. The fairer baseline for "the articulation matters" is 2R-remote, which the paper admits matches or beats the CFL at 90 deg and up to 5 mm at 120 deg (Fig. 3(a,b) shows 2R-remote above the CFL across most of those panels). The CFL's coverage benefit over remote actuation alone is therefore confined to deep flexion and thick pads. The redesign result (Fig. 3(d-i), Pareto front at 120 deg, 3 -> 7 mm) is the stronger design finding: the whole CFL front dominates the other fronts. The optimization (GA/NSGA-II, three seeds) gives no sense of seed variance and no convergence evidence. Mechanical advantage is reported but not constrained, and the 0.41 worst case is lower than the RCJ.

Evidence for (iii) is the most extensive and the most interesting part of the paper, but I have a conceptual concern. The authors say the error "enters through the preload", with E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1|. Under a joint-space stiffness controller with a fixed setpoint, the normal force on a pad is set by how far that pad's contact surface penetrates (or falls short of) the object at the commanded angle. That is essentially the contact-point displacement, i.e. Delta_contact. Delta_skel is measured at the fingertip in F1 and has no mechanical link to pad preload. A strong Delta_contact correlation with a null Delta_skel correlation is therefore close to expected by construction once the protocol is fixed. It is not a broad empirical discovery about interface evaluation. The value lies in quantifying the magnitude (95-228% for a skeleton-preserving revolute redesign, sign reversals) and in showing that common design choices (coverage-only, skeleton-preserving) fall into this trap. The protocol ablations (re-zeroed at touch follows the skeleton; ideal torque follows both; force matching nearly removes the error) are honest and useful. They also show that the claim holds only for the reused-setpoint protocol, which the paper does scope. The redesign set is engineered so that Delta_contact and Delta_skel vary independently, which is good design, but n is 14-18 per setting (45 on the grid), and the redesigns were chosen by the same optimizer whose metric is being validated. Even the best redesign R still errs by 21% at 7 mm and 41% at 9 mm. The conclusion's "near 20% up to 7 mm" is accurate, but that is a large error next to a -41% effect: R reports -55%.

What is missing for the stated aims: no hardware (Fig. 1 is CAD, and the interfaces in Fig. 1(c) are illustrative), no learned policy (the introduction motivates the work with policies that issue joint targets, but all tasks are open-loop), and the MCP is held fixed throughout. For an extended abstract with preliminary experiments these gaps are acceptable, because the hypotheses and methods are complete and the hardware test is pre-specified with predicted errors (21, 72, 124%). Pre-specifying those predictions is a commendable practice.

Clarity is the main presentation weakness. The text is dense and uses undefined or late-defined shorthand: "O's placements", "the grid", "faces", "a face that advances", U and R before they are clearly introduced in Results, and "own h". Table I mixes simulators and protocols, and n differs between columns without explanation. Section IV.B packs many statistics into few sentences. The related work is very short and makes a strong blanket claim ("None has been analyzed...").

My scores rest on a clear and useful reframing (pole relative to contact surfaces rather than skeleton), a solid design study, and a well-instrumented, multi-simulator evaluation. Against this, the central experimental correlation is close to mechanically implied, the coverage advantage over remote actuation alone is limited, and there is no physical validation.
