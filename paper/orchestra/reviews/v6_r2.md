THOUGHT:

My background is robotic manipulation and contact modelling, so I read the paper mainly through its Study 3 (Sec. V). Study 3 carries the "why it matters" claim (C4) and with it the paper's significance. The kinematic analysis (Sec. III) and the design studies (Sec. IV) are the supporting machinery.

What the paper argues. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the redesign should keep the motion of the distal contact face in the proximal-phalanx frame, c(q), not the skeleton/fingertip path. In planar terms this means keeping the finite pole of the distal phalanx relative to the contact face. For two fingers with the same c0, ||Δc(q)|| = 2 sin(q/2) ||Δp(q)||. A fixed axis therefore cannot keep c(q) under a thicker pad unless the pad is recessed, which gives a closed-form lower bound (Eq. 8). A crossed four-bar (CFL) has a moving pole, so it can stay at 10–51% of that bound with little loss in coverage. In simulation, reusing one flexion command across 15 redesigns, the error in a measured interface effect (friction, contact stiffness, crowning) has rank correlation ρ = 0.83 with Δcontact, and the other four candidate invariants are uncorrelated.

Intuitions.

1. The theory is clean and correct as far as I can check. Lemma 1 is classical (finite rotation pole, Bottema & Roth). Eq. (6) follows at once from it. Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality on the two pole shifts. Corollary 1 then follows. The geometric content is not deep, but using it on pad clearance, coverage and contact-face motion is new to me and useful. Fig. 2 makes Prop. 1 easy to see. Spot checks: 5 tan 60° = 8.66 and the 3.46 mm/mm slope at 120° are consistent.

2. Study 3 is the selling point, and as a contact-modelling reviewer I have serious doubts that it shows what the text says. My main worry is that the result is close to built in by the protocol.
   - The cylinder is placed where O touches it.
   - The PIP closes to a fixed overclosure q0 + 5° under a joint-space spring K0 = 5 N m/rad.
   - The load then ramps until failure.

   Under this protocol the grip force is set by how far the commanded distal face passes into the object. That interference is exactly what a change in distal-face trajectory changes: Δcontact is a max-norm displacement of a distal-face point at fixed q. The paper shows the mediator itself: U presses 3.3× (Fig. 1: 3.9×) harder than O.

   The measured "effect" is a change in raw limit load. Under Coulomb friction the friction-limited load scales with normal force, so the friction effect Δ(H) scales roughly with grip force, and E(H) ≈ |F_N(H)/F_N(O) − 1|. The finding thus reduces largely to "a redesign that changes the interference at a fixed joint command changes the grip force". That is true but close to tautological. The interesting question is whether Δcontact predicts the error beyond the grip-force mismatch, or whether it is just a geometric proxy for it. The paper reports neither the correlation of E with the grip-force ratio nor a force-normalized interface metric (e.g., limit load / normal force, or an estimated μ). If the effect were normalized by grip force, much of the error might vanish. That would turn C4 into a statement about a specific, rather unfavourable, measurement protocol.

3. The "five candidates" comparison is partly a strawman by construction.
   - **Transmission.** K0 is a joint-space (PIP) stiffness, and the command goes through each hand's own transmission table, which inverts the transmission. In the simulator, dθ/dq then cannot affect the outcome. ρ ≈ 0 for transmission is guaranteed, not found. A servo-side stiffness, which is what a real position-controlled servo gives, would make the joint stiffness scale with the square of the transmission ratio. That would be the honest test.
   - **Skeleton.** Δskel is negatively coupled to Δcontact through Eq. (7), and the design set was built from redesigns "at set Δcontact that keep or change the skeleton as much as possible". This deliberately anti-correlates the two. The negative ρ for Δskel and its CI near-excluding zero on the negative side are therefore expected from the sampling design. The partial correlation (0.88) helps but is computed on n = 15 designed points.
   - **Coverage and pad thickness.** By design these vary little or are fixed per stratum.

   A fairer test would sample redesigns more neutrally, for example the whole Pareto front or random feasible redesigns, not points engineered to separate the two predictors.

4. Statistics.
   - n = 15 (friction) and n = 14 (stiffness) hands, from 18 designs. The designs are not independent draws: three thickness strata, and R/U pairs chosen by the authors.
   - The "95% intervals over hands" ([0.41, 1.00]) look like percentile bootstrap. An upper limit of exactly 1.00 is a symptom of resampling duplicates with small n. BCa or Fisher-z intervals, or a permutation test, would be more appropriate.
   - "The interval of every other candidate contains zero" is not a test that Δcontact predicts better than Δskel. That needs a test of the difference of dependent correlations (Steiger/Williams, or a bootstrap of the difference).
   - Five candidates × three effects with no multiplicity statement.
   - "Positive at each thickness" rests on n ≈ 5 per stratum.
   - Each hand's error is a median over 21+ placements × 4 directions, which collapses within-hand variance. A mixed-effects model (placement and direction as fixed or random effects, hand as the unit) would use the data far better and show how consistent the effect is across placements and failure modes.
   - E(H) divides by |Δ(O)|. Where the friction effect on O is small, for instance for failure modes not limited by friction such as "loses a pad" or "collapses the grasp", the ratio blows up. The paper should report how Δ(O) is distributed and whether the median is driven by such placements.
   - The robustness line ("20–24 of the 22–24 paired placements") is hard to read. 21 values of q0 × 2 radii does not give 22–24, so I cannot reconstruct what was paired.

5. Contact-model fidelity.
   - "Pad material stiffness" is a contact-spring stiffness (2.5 → 1.0 kN/m). "Shape" is a 2 mm crown on presumably rigid geometry. These are point-contact soft-constraint models, not deformable pads. For thick compliant pads, which are the motivating case, contact area, pressure distribution and hysteresis are the main effects, and MuJoCo-type soft contacts do not capture them.
   - μ = 1.3–2.0 is high. Limit loads under MuJoCo's soft constraints depend on solref/solimp, the cone type (pyramidal vs. elliptic) and slip creep. The robustness checks (×0.3 and ×3 impedance ratio, ×10 contact stiffness, doubled substeps) are welcome, but they leave out cone type, regularisation and an independent solver.
   - "Newton and MuJoCo-Warp" is ambiguous. Is this one solver stack, or two whose results were cross-checked?
   - The scene setup is unclear. "Three CFL fingers" but "a cylinder rests on each hand's proximal pad, free in the sagittal plane". Is this a single-finger proximal/distal envelope or a three-finger grasp? How are out-of-plane forces handled?

6. Fig. 1 vs. text consistency.
   - R "grips at O's level", but the panel shows 3.0 N vs 4.5 N: 33% lower, while U is 3.9× higher. The asymmetry is fine, but "at O's level" overstates it.
   - The text says 3.3× (median), Fig. 1 says 3.9× (one placement). Labelling the latter as one placement would help.
   - Fig. 1d shows ρ = 0.83 with p = 0.0001. Table I gives a CI with lower limit 0.41. Both are fine, but p from a designed sample of 15 overstates certainty.

7. Design studies (Sec. IV).
   - Study 1 uses only three seeds per optimizer, with CFL-specific refinement. The authors note CFL wins without refinement, which is good.
   - C2's claim is against 2R-DD. 2R-remote is the stronger baseline and leads at 90° on the paper's own objective; the text discloses this.
   - MA_wc is reported as "not constrained", yet a four-bar tuned only for coverage and Δcontact can have poor transmission angles and low MA, which matters for any force-based interface study. Since Study 3 uses joint-space stiffness, this cost never appears in the evidence. The paper should at least report MA_wc for R vs. U vs. O.
   - Comparing CFL to the fixed-axis bound ("10–51% of the bound") is a reasonable reference, but it is not a bound for the CFL itself. The phrase "of what a fixed axis must" in the abstract is fine. "Well inside the fixed-axis bound" in C3 could mislead.

8. Venue fit. RSS Stage 1 allows preliminary experiments, and hardware is explicitly deferred. The problem, hypotheses and method are laid out, and the Stage-2 plan (O, R, U on one physical finger, tracking both faces, grasp-and-pull with two surfaces) is concrete. That is a strength. But the guidelines also ask for the insights to be gained even under negative results. The current Study 3 design cannot separate "contact-surface motion matters" from "position-commanded grip force at fixed overclosure matters". Under the latter reading the practical recommendation would be "use force/touch-referenced protocols", which the paper itself shows cut the error to 3–20%, rather than "redesign with a CFL".

9. Clarity. The text is dense and terse, with very short sentences that sometimes drop needed definitions:
   - What the "transmission table" is.
   - What q0 means operationally.
   - What "R" was optimised for in Study 3 (the Study 2 redesign?).
   - What the 18 vs. 15 vs. 14 counts are.
   - What S1/S2 cover in practice.

   The title is long and slogan-like. Fig. 2's caption is interleaved with the text in the extraction, which is probably a layout artefact. The figures themselves are clear and well designed.

Overall: a neat, correct kinematic insight with a well-motivated design study. The causal/statistical evidence for why it matters is weaker than the text claims and partly built in by the protocol. I lean towards reject at this stage (5) but could be persuaded, since Stage 1 allows preliminary experiments and the theory is sound. Concrete fixes:
- (a) Report force-normalized interface metrics and the grip-force mediator.
- (b) Use servo-side stiffness so that transmission is a real candidate.
- (c) Use a neutral redesign sample and a test of the difference of dependent correlations.
- (d) Fit a mixed-effects model at the placement level.
