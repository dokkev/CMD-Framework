THOUGHT:
The paper argues that comparisons of contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, tactile modules) on robot fingers are confounded, because carrying a different interface forces a redesign of the finger. It proposes the "contact-centric kinematics" c(q), the path of a distal-face point seen from the proximal face, as the thing to hold fixed. It then analyses every planar PIP joint through the finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement from extension. The finite-pole lens is classical (Bottema & Roth), but using it to get closed-form pad-clearance (Eq. 2), coverage (Eq. 3) and contact-path (Eq. 4) laws is clean and, as far as I know, new in the hand-design literature. I checked the numbers: 2 tan(q/2) gives 2.00/3.46/7.46 and 2 sin(q/2)·Δh gives 2.83/3.46/3.86 at Δh = 2 mm, so the arithmetic in the abstract and Table I is consistent. The authors also verify the analysis against the evaluator (1e-13, 1e-9, first-contact within 1°), which I appreciate. Honest reporting of where their own design loses (2R-remote leads at 90°) and the explicit "if false" branches for every hypothesis fit the Stage-1 format well.

My main concerns are about whether the evidence supports the central claim, not about the geometry.

(1) The invariant is asserted, not justified. The motivation says a thicker pad changes the contact-point Jacobian and thereby confounds the interface study. But c(q) is the relative motion of one face point, taken at one location below the fingertip. It does not pin down the Jacobian with respect to the actuator, the mechanical advantage or the transmitted force, and it does not pin down the fingertip workspace relative to the palm/MCP. MA is explicitly "reported, not constrained", yet no MA values appear for any design. For a CFL redesign, MA can change substantially while Dc stays small, and that would re-introduce exactly the force-transmission confound the introduction names. So the paper needs to argue why c(q) is the sufficient (or at least the dominant) invariant, or else report the other quantities as covariates.

(2) The Table I / Fig. 4 bound is not the bound that was proven. Corollary 1 is stated in terms of |Δh − Δd|, with equality only when the axis does not move along the face. It also claims to hold for rolling joints of fixed radius. Yet 2R-DD and RCJ fall below the plotted 2 sin(q/2)·Δh line. The text explains this by "mount depth shrinks" and "the RCJ pole rolls". That means the plotted line ignores Δd. It is not a lower bound for those designs, and the RCJ case seems to contradict the corollary as stated. Consequently "CFL achieves 10–51% of a provable fixed-axis bound" overstates things: CFL may also be reducing mount depth. The fair comparison is against 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δh − Δd| for each design, or with Δd held at zero for all architectures.

(3) H2 has no acceptance threshold. "Keeping the contact-centric kinematics" is never quantified: how many mm of Dc is small enough for an interface comparison to be valid? Without one, 0.35–4.31 mm is reported as "partial" after the fact, and the hypothesis is hard to falsify. The relevant scale would be the contact-patch size, the pad compliance, or a downstream grasp-metric sensitivity.

(4) The paper never shows that the confound matters in practice. The core premise is that interface comparisons on redesigned fingers reach wrong conclusions. Even a small simulated grasp/contact study would supply the missing link. In it, two interfaces would be compared once on fingers re-optimized freely and once on Dc-matched fingers, to see whether the ranking or effect size changes. As it stands, all results are about coverage and path geometry, which are proxies.

(5) Optimization and fairness. The values are best-found, not optimal, and CFL runs spread by 1.3–3.4 points. That spread is comparable to the 1.9–4.1-point 2R-remote lead at 90° and to the 120° crossover. The 2R-remote "loss of 0.740" at 150° comes from degenerate designs (C̄ = 0.21 and 0), so it partly reflects search failure or a coverage-definition artefact, and quoting it in the abstract is misleading. The 2R-remote actuator pose is fixed (10/15 mm, 15°) while CFL's geometry is free. Links and belts on other layers are not checked against pads, which may favour the linkage. Study 2 uses only 2 seeds.

(6) Presentation. The paper is dense, with undefined symbols (L_vp, r_{2,s}, r_{1,e}, x0 introduced late) and a duplicated "clearance, clearance" in the contributions. MA "21 for RCJ" is presumably 1/2 or 2. The color mapping is inconsistent: 2R-DD is orange and RCJ green in Fig. 1, but green and orange respectively in Figs. 3/4, and 2R-remote is purple vs red. In Fig. 4(c) the off-scale 2R-remote points use the 2R-DD triangle marker. Fig. 1 prints pad fraction (77%) while the caption lists C̄_pad (0.739); this is correct but easy to confuse. The title says "Hand", but everything is a single planar finger with the MCP fixed.

(7) The Stage-2 protocol is reasonably specified (exchange cost, reached flexion, tracked face motion, common grasp protocol) but lacks quantitative success criteria. It does not specify the number of trials, objects, the tracking accuracy needed to resolve ~1 mm Dc differences, or how "keeping the predicted motion" will be decided.

Overall: elegant, verifiable kinematic insight and a well-structured Stage-1 plan with negative-result branches. However, the headline H2 evidence leans on a mis-specified bound, and the chosen invariant is not shown to remove the confound that motivates the work. I lean toward rejection in the current form, but these are fixable within Stage 1 (re-derive or re-plot against |Δh − Δd|, report MA, define a Dc tolerance, add a small downstream sensitivity check).
