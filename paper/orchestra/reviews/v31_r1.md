ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: I am a robot-hand designer who has built servo-chain hands and run pad and tactile comparisons on them, so I read this asking whether the design would change how I build fingers and run interface studies.

The paper makes two claims about a planar servo finger with the MCP held fixed.

(1) Room for pads. Remote actuation plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint 2R (2R-DD) at every pad thickness and flexion. The evidence is reasonably strong for an optimisation result. Each joint is compared at its own GA/NSGA-II optimum under a shared goal (L_f = 90 mm, same actuators, a mounting rule, feasibility to q_req). CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 shared conditions, by 2.4-14.6 points (Fig. 3a-c). Props. 1-2 explain why: the pole can follow a deepening face. Fig. 3a-b also shows that 2R-remote matches or beats the CFL at 90 deg and at thin pads at 120 deg. The gain is therefore mostly remote actuation at mild flexion, and the polycentric joint matters only at deep flexion. The paper says this, which is good. Coverage is measured as an inscribed-cylinder overlap evaluated at only three poses. This is a reasonable proxy for "room", but it is not the same as usable pad contact area. I am also missing practical costs that a builder pays. Worst-case mechanical advantage is 0.41-0.58, which roughly halves the fingertip force against direct drive on a torque-limited servo. Link stiffness, backlash in the four pins and lateral width are not reported either.

(2) Keep the contact-surface motion, not the skeleton. Prop. 3 / Eq. (7) is the clean kinematic fact: contact-surface displacement equals 2 sin(q/2) times the pole shift. Eq. (8)-(9) give a lower bound for fixed-axis redesigns, and the CFL redesign reaches 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. The empirical claim is that, under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured friction or stiffness effect tracks Delta_contact. This is supported in two simulators, with point and hydroelastic contact, in three open-loop tasks, and with pre-registration and Holm correction. That is unusually careful for a workshop-length paper.

My main reservations about claim (2) are these:
(a) The mechanism is preload. The authors show E ~ |N_H/N_O - 1|, so the result is essentially "a position-controlled finger whose face moves presses differently". In my own pad comparisons we fix grasp force, either with a current or force threshold or by calibrating preload. Table I and Sec. IV-B4 show that under those protocols Delta_skel, not Delta_contact, predicts the error, and that force matching leaves only 3-7%. The prescription is therefore conditional on one protocol. The paper argues this protocol is common in servo hands, but that is asserted with one citation, not shown.
(b) The hand population was constructed to span Delta_contact and Delta_skel. With 14-16 hands, Delta_contact's lead over Delta_skel is not significant; only the 45-hand grid separates them.
(c) "Kept" still means 20% error at 7 mm. At 9 mm it means 41% with point contact and 104% with hydroelastic pads as soft as they are thick. That is comparable to many interface effects one would want to measure, so "studied independently of the finger" is stronger than the numbers.
(d) Everything is in simulation. The 0.9 mm Delta_contact of the 7 mm redesign is the same order as the servo backlash, pin clearance and pad thickness tolerance in hobby-servo hands. The Stage-2 hardware plan is well designed: it uses markers, a load cell for preload, and a refutation criterion with a Page trend test. However, it is only a plan.

The 48%-vs-41% reversal is a vivid and useful demonstration that the confound matters.

Presentation: the abstract and introduction are clear. Sec. III-B is dense: S1/S2, the mounting rule, "the goal", and the O/R/U/grid labels are introduced in passing. A hand designer will need several passes. The paper omits build-relevant details: servo model, link dimensions, pad material and how pads attach. Fig. 2c's 3-D surface is hard to read. In Fig. 4b the panel title and the "20%" label overlap the markers. Figs. 1, 3 and 4a are good.

Scores: the design analysis is solid and moderately original, and the experiment is careful but narrow and simulation-only. I lean accept as an extended abstract.
