ASSESSMENT NOTES:

Central claims and evidence:

1. **Theory (Sec. III-A): what a redesign can keep.** When both contact surfaces deepen by Δt, no redesign keeps both the contact-point motion (Δ_contact) and the fingertip motion (Δ_skel): Δ_contact + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|, Eq. (4). A fixed axis cannot keep the contact motion from the same initial contact point, Eq. (5).
   - The derivation is short and checks out. With a common initial point, ‖Δc‖ = 2 sin(q/2)‖Δp‖. The frame offset gives Δp − Δp₁ = Δt·e_z, and the triangle inequality finishes the bound. 2 tan 60° = 3.46 is correct.
   - The bound is elementary. It also rests on assumptions the paper only half states: a pure translation between F_p and F₁, the same initial contact point, and the same fingertip at q = 0 because L_f is fixed. Its value is in framing, not depth.
   - Δ_contact tracks a single point (the one below the fingertip), while the text speaks of the "contact Jacobian", which is a field over the pad. This gap matters for the pad-walking task.

2. **Design studies (Sec. III-D): room and preserved motion.** The crossed four-bar covers more than an at-joint revolute in all 10 feasible conditions, by 2.4–14.6 pp, and preserves contact motion best in all 9 redesign conditions.
   - The evidence is adequate: one optimizer, one coverage metric (inscribed cylinder) and one finger length.
   - Remote actuation matches the four-bar at 90° and at ≤5 mm at 120°. So much of the "room" benefit comes from moving the actuator, not from the pole, which the paper acknowledges.

3. **Main empirical claim (Sec. IV-B1/2): what predicts the error.** When O's impedance setpoint is reused, the error in a measured pad effect follows Δ_contact, not Δ_skel. This holds across two simulators, two contact models, four Isaac Lab tests (Holm-corrected permutation tests), partial correlations controlling Δ_skel (0.86–0.91), and a force-deviation control (partial ρ ≈ 0.8).
   - **Strengths of the evidence.**
     - The controls are thoughtful: a preload control, setpoint re-referencing, a pre-registered statistic, and a self-error floor of 6%.
     - The Skeleton hand is a good counterfactual against the "keep the skeleton" intuition.
   - **The reference choice drives the result.** "Truth" is O on its own geometry carrying the new pad's friction or contact stiffness. That pad is not physically realizable, since a thicker pad changes geometry. Under this reference, the finding that error tracks Δ_contact is close to expected by construction: a hand whose contact points move like O's will respond like O to a contact-parameter change. The non-obvious part is the Skeleton result.
   - **Δ_contact and Δ_skel are confounded by design.** Eq. (4) forces them to trade off at fixed Δt, so the raw ρ comparisons in Fig. 4 are not independent evidence. The partial correlations help, but the number of redesigns behind each ρ (correlation set, grid) is never stated. ρ = 0.94 without n is hard to weigh.
   - **The per-task correlations in Fig. 4 are weak for two of the four tasks** (dial 0.53, box 0.43). These would not be significant with a small number of redesigns. The text says only that "the ordering" is kept.
   - **Table I contradicts or complicates the prose.**
     - Under a reused torque, Coverage's median error is 0% against CoDe's +8%. This is presumably the one exception in "eleven of twelve". It also sits awkwardly with the claim that Δ_contact predicts the torque error.
     - Fig. 1(d) reports +48% for the conventional redesign (median of loads), while Table I reports +125% (median of per-placement errors). The difference is explained, but it reads as inconsistent.
   - **The motivating case is not modelled.** No pad deforms, but the motivation (thick, compliant pads and skins) is exactly the case where deformation moves the contact point and may dominate the kinematic effect.

4. **Policy transfer (Fig. 5) is the weakest leg.**
   - It uses two seeds.
   - The relay-dial task favours Skeleton, so "contact at the fingertip's end" is ruled outside the claim after the fact.
   - The cube result is explained by a third quantity, palm shift (Δ_MCP-like), not by Δ_contact.
   - Pad walking does not transfer to Drake even on O.
   - The defensible message is "the quantity to preserve depends on where the task touches". That is reasonable but much less crisp than the headline "keep the contact-surface motion".

Basis of the scores:
- The paper is clearly argued and careful in its controls. The pole analysis is a neat framing.
- For a paper titled "Design of a ... Robot Hand", three things weaken it:
  - Everything is in simulation (no hardware).
  - Only a planar PIP is redesigned, with the MCP fixed.
  - The ground-truth reference is unrealizable, and the result is close to definitional under it.
- The generality of the main principle is already qualified by the policy experiments.
- Originality is moderate. The insight that the contact kinematics, not the joint skeleton, set what a reused command does is intuitive once stated, although stating it formally and testing it has value.
- I lean to reject but could be persuaded by a hardware demonstration or a deformable-pad study.
