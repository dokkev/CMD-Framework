ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The paper makes three claims about one planar finger (PIP joint only, MCP held).

Claim 1, room: a servo moved off the joint plus a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad "coverage" than a servo-at-joint finger. The support is a pole-based clearance/coverage analysis (Props. 1-2, which I checked: the gap law 2[t - z]tan(q/2) and the 3.46 mm/mm figure at 120 deg are correct) plus a constrained optimization over four joint types, 4 thicknesses and 3 flexions. The CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 conditions where both are feasible, by 2.4-14.6 points. This is credible but rests on a self-defined coverage metric (cylinder inscribed in the V of the two faces) for which Prop. 2 already shows a face-level virtual pole is optimal, on one finger length (90 mm), and on unstated servo dimensions. The margin over 2R-remote and over RCJ at 90 deg is small in Fig. 3a; the clear win is against the servo-at-joint layout and at deep flexion. The abstract's "at every pad thickness and required flexion" silently means "wherever 2R-DD has a design".

Claim 2, redesign: a fixed axis cannot preserve the contact-surface path when the face deepens (Eq. 6-8; the algebra is right: |(I - R_q) dp| = 2 sin(q/2)|dp|, and Eq. 7 follows from the triangle inequality), whereas the CFL reaches 10-51% of the bound at essentially no coverage cost. The bound is correct under its premises, but the premises carry weight: the axis depth is held by the "mounting rule" and the "no lugs with full-width pads" assumption, and c0 is held. The paper itself reports that a fixed axis allowed to move c0 reaches 0.59-0.80 of the bound, and that 2R-DD sits below the bound because its servo already holds the face deep. So "cannot" is a statement about this design rule, not about fixed axes in general. The CFL is lowest in every cell only when the start offset of the other joints is counted; without it, 7 of 9. The CFL change is also not small in absolute terms at 9 mm (up to 4.31 mm), and the title's "keep the contact Jacobian" overstates a result that is "reduce the change of one column's path, at one point, in the plane".

Claim 3, consequence: under a reused impedance setpoint, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect tracks Delta_contact (Spearman 0.80-0.86 in Isaac Lab, 0.67-0.87 in Drake across point and hydroelastic contact and three open-loop tasks), not Delta_skel. This is the most interesting part and the evidence is broad for an extended abstract: two simulators, two contact models, three tasks, partial correlations, Holm correction, a pre-registration statement. My reservations: (i) the mechanism is close to built in. A stiffness-on-position setpoint with a face displaced by millimetres against a 1-2.5 kN/m pad necessarily changes the preload, so a rank correlation with a face-displacement measure is expected; what is non-obvious is only that it beats the skeleton measure, and the hand set was constructed by the authors to decorrelate the two. (ii) The first-order preload model predicts 230% where 124% is measured, so the stated mechanism is only half quantitative, and the partial rho of 0.69 "with the face's offset at the grasp held fixed" suggests something other than the preload offset carries much of the signal; this is not explained. (iii) The result is protocol-specific and the paper says so: with touch re-zeroing, which is what practitioners actually do, the error follows the skeleton (0.88 vs 0.44) and remains 6-61%; with torque control the two candidates are tied (0.69 vs 0.71), so "both of these errors follow the skeleton" is overstated for torque. This narrows the practical reach of the central thesis ("dispense with the skeleton as the redesign target") to hands commanded with un-re-zeroed absolute position targets. (iv) The headline "48% more when it holds 41% less" comes from U, a coverage-only design never intended as a redesign of O; the fairer baseline, the skeleton-preserving revolute (95-228%), is in the text but not in the abstract. (v) Everything is simulation; there is no hardware, and the pre-registration cannot be verified from the submission.

Presentation: the logic is tight and the paper is unusually falsification-minded, but it is very dense. "CFL" is used in the contribution list and never expanded anywhere; "2R-DD" likewise. Sec. IV-A compresses a complex protocol (O, R, U, M, "the grid", "O's placements", 18 + 27 redesigns, gain scaling) into one paragraph that I had to read several times. Fig. 2b and Fig. 3 are hard to read at print size. The costs of the CFL (mechanical advantage 0.41-0.58, link packaging beside full-width pads, backlash, lateral stiffness, part count) get one sentence.

Scores rest on: correct and neat kinematic analysis with a genuinely useful framing (redesign target = contact-surface motion); a complete, reasonably broad but simulation-only validation whose main effect is partly expected by construction and holds for one protocol; and a compressed presentation. For a 5-page extended abstract in which preliminary experiments are admissible and a concrete, refutable hardware test is stated, this is above the bar, but not by a wide margin.
