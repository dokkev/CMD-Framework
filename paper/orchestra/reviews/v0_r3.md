THOUGHT:
The paper argues that comparisons of contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, tactile modules) on robot fingers are confounded by the finger redesign each interface needs. It proposes the "contact-centric kinematics" (the path of a distal-face point seen from the proximal face) as the quantity to hold fixed, and analyses joint–pad conflicts through the finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement. The analysis yields a symmetric clearance law (Eq. 2), a coverage law (Eq. 3), a contact-path law (Eq. 4) and a fixed-axis lower bound on contact-path change (Eq. 5). A planar computational study of four PIP architectures (2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, CFL) over thickness x required flexion tests H1 (architecture bounds coverage) and H2 (only a pole-moving joint can absorb a thicker pad while keeping the contact path). H3, physical interface exchange, is deferred to Stage 2.

My overall view: the finite-pole lens is clean and genuinely useful for designers. The tan(q/2) charge on a fixed axis, the mirror-line argument for clearance, and the 2 sin(q/2)|Δp| contact-path law are simple, checkable, and make the trade-off explicit. The authors also report results where their design loses (2R-remote leads at 90 deg; RCJ ideal is competitive), validate the analytic laws against the numerical evaluator, and state for each hypothesis what a negative result would mean. That fits the Stage-1 format well.

My concerns are about whether the evidence and framing support the headline claim, which is "comparable contact-interface studies":
1. The invariant is never tied to an interface-study outcome. The paper asserts that a Dc of a few mm confounds grasp or tactile comparisons but never shows how much Dc matters, and never sets a threshold for "preserved". CFL still moves the contact path by 2.3-4.3 mm at 9 mm. Whether that is negligible or larger than the interface effect being measured is unknown, so "partially supports H2" has no yardstick.
2. The framing is internally inconsistent. R = (H, C) says the hand H stays fixed while C varies, yet Study 2 and the H3 protocol both change the linkage (articulation geometry Q, which is part of H) to fit thicker pads. What is actually held fixed is c(q), not the hand. Mechanical advantage and transmission (which also determine the contact-point force mapping the introduction lists as a confound) are explicitly unconstrained and go unreported in Study 2. So the redesign may keep the path while changing force transmission, which is itself an embodiment confound.
3. Corollary 1 contains Δd (a reduction in mount depth). If the structure under the pad can be thinned by Δh, the fixed-axis bound drops to zero. The H2 test therefore hinges on how d is constrained in Study 2, and the paper does not say. This is also why 2R-DD and RCJ "fall below the bound" (the paper itself says mount depth shrinks). As written, the bound is not cleanly comparable across architectures.
4. The conclusions are fragile to search quality. CFL runs spread by 1.3-3.4 points and continuation raised cells by up to 2.1 points, while 2R-remote's lead at 90 deg is 1.9-4.1 points and the 120 deg crossover sits near 5.3-6 mm. Several "None found" cells and degenerate 2R-remote designs at 150 deg are statements about the optimizer. A bias also arises because CFL received extra restarts and warm-starts that the other architectures did not. The 2R-remote actuator pose is fixed by hand (10 mm proximal, 15 mm dorsal, 15 deg), which could penalize it at high flexion.
5. The theory is narrower than "exact laws for any finger joint" suggests. Lemma 1 is textbook (Bottema & Roth). Props 2-3 require S1/S2 symmetry and equal pads, yet the optimized designs need not satisfy S2. Corollary 1 has no proof sketch, and the novelty is in the application rather than in the kinematics.
6. H1 is weakly falsifiable ("spread grows") and has no quantitative criterion. Its negative branch is partly realized (RCJ loss = packaging), yet the paper still says H1 is supported.
7. H3 is the claim closest to the title, and it is only sketched. No interfaces are fixed, there are no success thresholds, the measurement accuracy of face tracking (it must resolve ~1 mm Dc) is not given, there is no trial count, and the grasp protocol is undefined. For Stage 1, where methodology must be fully specified, this is thin.
8. Presentation issues. Fig. 1 prints (ℓ1+ℓ2)/Lf (77/52/52/54%) while the caption and objective use C̄pad (0.739/0.593/...), and two coverage measures side by side invite confusion. In Fig. 4(c), the off-scale 2R-remote markers are triangles (2R-DD's symbol). There are text typos ("clearance, clearance"; MA "21" for 1/2). The notation is dense for 5 pages. Related work omits polycentric/centrode joint design (prosthetic knees, finger centrodes) and modular or swappable fingertip/tactile hands, which are directly relevant to the "module swap" claim.

On balance: this is a solid, honest design-analysis core with a well-posed problem, but the central motivation (confound removal) is not operationalized, and the Stage-2 plan is underspecified. I lean reject, at borderline. I would move up if the authors (a) define a Dc tolerance tied to the contact patch or to grasp metrics, (b) constrain or report d and MA in Study 2, and (c) give H3 concrete thresholds and measurement specs.
