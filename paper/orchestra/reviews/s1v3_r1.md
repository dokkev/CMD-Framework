ASSESSMENT NOTES:

The hypotheses as I read them. The paper's central claim is a design principle. When a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface and a command learned on the original hand is reused, the redesign should keep the motion of the contact surfaces (the distal pad's path seen from the proximal pad, Δ^prox_distal). It should not keep the skeleton (joint axes and fingertip path, Δ_skel). A finite-displacement pole argument supports this. Eq. (2) says a pole shift moves every distal-surface point by 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. Eq. (3) says Δ^prox_distal + Δ_skel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|, so a redesign that deepens the surfaces cannot keep both motions. Eq. (4) says a fixed axis whose pin sits above the interface mount cannot keep the contact motion. A crossed four-bar (CFL) at the PIP, driven by an off-joint servo, can place its pole near the contact faces. The paper states three hypotheses and says they were fixed before any redesign was run:
- H1: the effect error e_H (Eq. 9) of a redesign under O's reused impedance setpoint grows with Δ^prox_distal, not with Δ_skel.
- H2: this is not just a change of preload; Δ^prox_distal still predicts the error once the contact force is matched.
- H3: a policy trained on O keeps the most performance on the redesign that preserves the motion its task uses.
There is also a secondary design claim (call it D): the CFL fits thicker interfaces with more coverage than at-joint, remote and RCJ joints, and keeps contact motion best (Fig. 3).

Evidence for each.
- D: optimized designs for 4 architectures × 4 thicknesses × 3 flexion ranges. The CFL leads coverage by 2.4–14.6 pp where both are feasible. At 120°/7 mm the gains are modest (73% vs 68–70%), and "remote" matches the CFL at 90° and up to 5 mm at 120°. The CFL keeps contact motion best in all nine redesign conditions. This is credible, but the margins are small at low thickness and flexion.
- H1: this is the strongest part. Isaac Lab (MuJoCo-Warp) pull-out shows significance in four tests (Holm-corrected p<0.001), partial ρ 0.86–0.91 with Δ_skel held fixed, and ρ=0.94 over 27 grid redesigns. These grid redesigns were built to decouple Δ^prox_distal from Δ_skel. That is the right design, and Skeleton matching O's tip as closely as CoDe matches O's pads answers the obvious circularity worry. Drake repeats the result in four tasks under point and hydroelastic contact. However, ρ drops to 0.43–0.53 in two of the four Drake tasks (dial, pivot), and the hydroelastic numbers for Skeleton vary wildly (86→388% in Hold).
- The headline sign-reversal example makes a vivid case. On O, the compliant interface lowers pull-out by 41%; CoDe measures 39%; Coverage reports a 48% rise. It is a single condition.
- H2: under a reused joint torque, ∆^prox_distal still predicts the error with the force difference held fixed (partial ρ=0.80). Re-referencing the setpoint cuts the error from 42 to 14%. This is reasonable, but the "matched force" condition is only approximated by reusing the torque command, not by matching forces at the contact.
- H3: the weakest part. There are three PPO tasks and two training seeds. The result is mixed by design: CoDe wins finger-walk and cube rotation, and Skeleton wins the fingertip-end dial. The "motion the task uses" is chosen per task after the fact: pad relative motion, tip path, or proximal pad vs palm. The cube ρ=−0.98 over 14 redesigns uses yet another metric. As stated, H3 risks being unfalsifiable: any outcome can be explained by choosing the matching motion. Its pre-registration does not visibly fix which motion each task uses.

Confounds and alternative explanations still open.
(i) The ground truth is definitional. O's "own effect" for a thicker or compliant interface is simulated with O's geometry and only the new contact stiffness. So the thickness change is excluded by definition from the "true" effect, which favors a redesign that hides the thickness change. That is defensible for friction changes but less so for the compliant interface.
(ii) Object placement decides the frame. Placing the object relative to the proximal pad makes Δ^prox_distal the relevant metric almost by construction. The authors admit this in IV-B-2, where palm-fixed placement makes Δ^base_distal relevant. The general claim "keep contact motion" then needs the frame to be task-specified, which weakens its prescriptive value.
(iii) CoDe at 7 mm places its surfaces only 1 mm deeper than O by raising the pivots and making the mount shallower. In effect, CoDe keeps the contact surfaces near their original location. Whether such a mount is physically realizable with real 7 mm pads, pins and lugs is unverified.
(iv) Fairness of the search. The paper says F′ frees pivot heights for every architecture. It is unclear whether Skeleton and Coverage were optimized over the same F′, with the same budget and seeds.
(v) No baseline tests a cheaper fix: a command remap such as joint-space re-referencing or gain retuning, or for policies, brief fine-tuning. Such a baseline would show whether redesign is needed at all.
(vi) Everything is in simulation: rigid pads, a planar finger, the MCP fixed, only the PIP redesigned.

What my scores rest on.
- Value 3: the question "which kinematic property must be preserved when the interface changes" is new and well posed, and the pole argument gives it a clean analytical backbone. It also matters to interface and tactile studies and to policy reuse. I hold back from 4 because the scope is narrow (reused joint-impedance commands, a single PIP joint, planar). In addition, H1 is close to intuitive once the object is fixed to the proximal pad.
- Feasibility 3: H1 and H2 are already supported in simulation with a pre-registered design, two simulators, two contact models and a decorrelated grid. The hardware plan (CoDe, Skeleton and Coverage fingers with 3 and 7 mm pads; pull-out and cube transfer) is concrete and doable. It is not 4 because there is no hardware, H3 rests on two seeds and per-task post-hoc metrics, two Drake tasks show weak correlation, and confounds (i)–(v) remain.
