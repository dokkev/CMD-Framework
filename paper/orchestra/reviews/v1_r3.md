THOUGHT:
The paper argues that comparisons of finger contact interfaces (thick compliant pads, vision-based tactile modules) are confounded by the finger redesign each interface needs. It proposes holding the "contact-centric kinematics" c(q) (the path of a distal-face point seen from the proximal face) fixed across such comparisons. It analyses any planar PIP joint through the finite pole of the distal-phalanx displacement and derives a clearance law (Eq. 2), a coverage law (Eq. 3), a contact-path law (Eq. 4) and a lower bound on the contact-path change of fixed-axis redesigns (Eq. 5). Two simulation studies over four architectures (2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, CFL), four pad thicknesses and three flexion requirements test H1 (architecture bounds coverage) and H2 (only a pole-moving joint can absorb a thicker pad while keeping c(q) and coverage). H3 (hardware exchange) is only a Stage-2 plan.

Intuitions. For a Stage-1 extended abstract the problem framing is clear and unusual. The idea that interface studies confound interface and embodiment is real and under-discussed in tactile-hand work. The paper is unusually honest: it reports where CFL loses (2R-remote leads at 90 deg), gives H-false branches for each hypothesis, and reports run spreads. The finite-pole lens is classical (Bottema and Roth), but using it to unify the joint-pad conflict across architectures is a neat, transferable piece of analysis. Eq. 4 (|dc| = 2 sin(q/2)|dp|) and the remark that "keeping the contact kinematics and keeping the pads are one condition" are the most insightful parts.

My main concerns are about whether the hypotheses as stated are testable and whether the evidence supports the claims at the strength used in the abstract and conclusion.

(1) H2 has no falsification criterion. No absolute Dc tolerance is set, and the "bound" is the fixed-axis bound at dd = 0. CFL reaching 10-51% of the bound is reported as support. But 2R-DD and RCJ also fall below the bound by changing mount depth. And at 90 deg / 9 mm CFL moves the contact path by 4.3 mm, about half the bound and about 5% of finger length. It is unclear what result would have counted against H2, short of CFL exceeding the bound. The "without reserving depth in advance" qualifier makes H2 partly definitional, since recessing or spacers trivially satisfy it and are excluded by fiat. Whether 1-4 mm of contact-path change matters for any interface measurement is deferred to H3, so the practical meaning of "keeping" c(q) is unsettled.

(2) The confound moves rather than disappears. CFL keeps c(q) by changing its skeleton and transmission: MA_wc changes by -7 to +16%. For force- and stiffness-related interface studies, a 16% change in transmission is arguably as serious a confound as a few mm of contact-path change. The paper records it and suggests reporting it as a covariate, but this weakens the central pitch that CoDe makes interfaces "comparable".

(3) H1 evidence and fairness of comparison. The H1 differences are often a few points of C̄_pad: the 2R-remote lead at 90 deg falls from 4.1 to 1.9 points, and the 120 deg crossover lies within the CFL run spread. The CFL run spread is 1.3-3.4 points, and only CFL receives extra seeding from neighbouring cells and Study-2 designs ("extra effort can only raise CFL"). This asymmetric search budget biases the comparison toward the proposed design. Several modelling choices also matter a lot: the fixed 2R-remote actuator pose, no belt or link occlusion, no checks between lateral layers, and the joint-actuator bodies. RCJ ideal reaches CFL's level, so for RCJ the gap is packaging, by the authors' own admission. H1's "architecture bounds" claim is therefore partly "architecture plus packaging model" and is sensitive to choices not ablated.

(4) Theory-to-optimum gap. Props. 2-3 rely on S1-S2, which the optima satisfy only approximately (G - G_sym up to -3.4 mm at 150 deg). The laws are thus explanatory estimates. That is acceptable, but the abstract states the 2.00 / 3.46 / 7.46 mm/mm slopes as if they were properties of real designs.

(5) Metric. The cylinder-inscribed coverage C̄_pad, evaluated at three poses and normalised by the flexed contour length, is idiosyncratic. Fig. 1 shows (ℓ1+ℓ2)/L_f while the text uses C̄_pad, two different numbers for the same designs (CFL 77% vs 0.739), which invites confusion. The claim that this objective captures "space available to interfaces" (Q1) needs justification. Why a cylinder? Why those three poses? Why equal-thickness pads only?

(6) Stage-2 plan. The H3 protocol is reasonable but thin. The pass criterion (motion within the repeatability of the reference) is good. But there is no statement of which interfaces, which grasp tasks, what sample sizes, or how Dc and MA_wc enter the analysis as covariates. For an RSS Stage-1 submission, where methodology must be fully fleshed out, this is the weakest section. The ultimate claim (fewer confounds in interface studies) is never tested, even in simulation: there is no simulated grasp metric showing that Dc or MA differences change outcomes.

(7) Related work. The citations are focused, as the venue asks. But contact kinematics in the Montana sense, which bears directly on the term "contact-centric kinematics", is not cited, and neither are modular or swappable fingertip and gripper-finger designs used for interface comparison. Including them would sharpen novelty.

(8) Clarity. The text is dense, with many symbols and acronyms (CFL, RCJ, 2R-DD, 2R-remote, MA_wc, Dc, Ds, S1, S2, P1-P4). Ds is defined but never used. Some sentences need re-reading, e.g. "a gap that shrinks with flexion is credited and a contour that lengthens is charged". Table I is hard to parse.

Decision reasoning. The framing and the pole-based analysis are original and well executed for a planar model, and the negative results are well reported. But the central H2 claim lacks a falsifiable criterion. The MA change undercuts "same hand". The H1 comparison has an acknowledged search asymmetry favouring the proposed design. And the actual purpose (better interface experiments) is entirely deferred. I lean reject (5), persuadable if co-reviewers weigh the Stage-1 leniency on experiments more heavily.
