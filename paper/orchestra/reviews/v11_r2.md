THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a finger must be redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface (compliant pad, tactile module), the property to preserve is the relative motion of the two contact faces (one column of a relative "contact Jacobian" written in the proximal-phalanx frame, equivalently the finite pole of the distal phalanx measured from the proximal face), not the skeleton/fingertip motion. It supports this with (i) elementary planar-kinematics results (finite-pole clearance law, coverage law, chord law ||Δc|| = 2 sin(q/2)||Δp||, a mixed inequality Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(q_req/2)|Δt|, and a fixed-axis lower bound), (ii) a design-optimization comparison of four PIP joint types (2R-DD, 2R-remote, RCJ, crossed four-bar CFL) over pad thickness and flexion range, and (iii) an Isaac Lab / MuJoCo-Warp study in which reused flexion commands or torques are applied to 15-45 redesigns and the error in measured friction/stiffness/shape effects is rank-correlated with five candidate invariants.

My intuitions after reading:

1. The core idea is clean and, to my knowledge, new as a framing: "interface comparisons are confounded by the redesign the interface forces; choose the invariant of the redesign deliberately." The pole-based analysis is mathematically correct as far as I can check (Lemma 1 is textbook, Prop. 3 is the chord of a rotation about a per-q finite pole, Eq. (7) is a triangle inequality between the pole shift in two frames that differ by the face depth, Cor. 1 follows). The numbers in Fig. 2 (5 tan 60° = 8.7 mm, 3.46 mm/mm at 120°) and the fixed-axis contrast (Δcontact = √3 Δh = 2 sin 60° Δh) are internally consistent. The theory is modest in depth but appropriate and well-integrated.

2. My largest concern is partial circularity in Study 3. The cylinder is placed "where O touches it with both pads at q0," along the proximal face, and the flexion command is reused through each hand's transmission table so that φ = q is matched. Under that protocol, Δcontact is very nearly a direct measure of how differently the two faces close on an object fixed in the proximal-face frame, i.e., of over-/under-closure, which under position control with K0 = 5 N m/rad maps straight to contact force and hence to the measured limit load. That Δcontact predicts error under this protocol is therefore close to built in. Conversely Δskel is measured from the link line, which is not the frame in which the object is placed, and Eq. (7) forces Δskel and Δcontact to trade off at fixed thickness, so the negative ρ for Δskel (−0.40, −0.51) is largely a property of how the redesign set was constructed. The authors partially address this (partial correlation given Δskel = 0.88; the hand-independent grid; the reused-torque protocol in which over-closure is removed and ρ = 0.75 persists). The torque result is the most informative evidence in the paper, but it rests on 16 hands, R vs U differences are modest (5/17/12% vs 9/28/23%), and the grid is still defined along the proximal face. I would like the authors to state plainly which part of C4 is near-tautological given the protocol and which part is an empirical finding.

3. Positive/negative controls are missing where they would be cheapest. Spacers and a recessed fixed axis (Δd = Δh) achieve Δcontact = 0 trivially; in simulation these are exactly the positive control that would show whether Δcontact = 0 drives the error to ~0 regardless of joint type, coverage or mass distribution. They are excluded on practical grounds, but practicality is irrelevant for a causal test in simulation. Likewise, the fixed-axis contrast family (95-228% error) is reported only under the reused-command protocol and is not placed on the Fig. 5 axes; its pads are also shortened to the Eq. (4) gap, confounding pad length with Δcontact. Study 3 otherwise uses only CFL redesigns, so "Δcontact predicts error" is shown essentially within one joint family.

4. Falsification criteria are a nice touch but are self-lenient. C2 is tested against 2R-DD, the weakest physical baseline; the closer competitor, 2R-remote, beats the CFL on the paper's own objective at q_req = 90° for all thicknesses and at 120° for thin pads, and the text then switches metric (pad length over fixed L_f) to recover a win. C3 "fails if it reaches the bound," but the bound is for a different joint class and is only a reference line for the CFL; 2R-DD even falls below it. C4 "fails if another quantity predicts the error in every setting" is a weak bar given four settings with 14-16 hands each and five candidates with no multiplicity correction. The abstract's "ρ = 0.60-0.94" upper end comes from the shape effect over six hands.

5. Physical realism of the interface. The motivation is thick compliant pads and camera-based tactile modules, yet the pads are rigid geometry with a penalty contact model ("pads represented by a contact model"). A 7-9 mm compliant pad deforms by millimetres under the reported 3-17 N forces, which is the same order as the Δcontact differences being studied (0.35-4.3 mm). Whether rigid-face kinematics remains the right invariant once the face itself moves under load is the central question Stage 2 must answer, and it is not discussed.

6. Practical significance. The paper's own numbers say that contact-aware protocols (force matching: 3-7%; closing from own touch: 20%) remove most of the error, while R still errs 41% at 9 mm under the reused command. That reframes the contribution: hardware invariance helps under open-loop protocols but does not make a "same hand." The title ("Same Hand, Different Contact") overstates this. The paper should argue more concretely which interface studies cannot use contact-aware protocols (e.g., frozen learned policies) and quantify the share of practice that this covers.

7. Venue fit. For an RSS Stage-1 abstract the problem, hypotheses and methodology are mostly fleshed out, and the preliminary results are already substantial. However, the Stage-2 plan (C5) is two sentences: no measurement protocol for the physical friction/stiffness effects, no trial counts, no failure criterion, and no statement of what would be learned if R and U do not differ physically. The guidelines explicitly ask for insights under negative results.

8. Writing. The prose is short-sentence and confident, but extremely dense: many symbols are introduced in passing or not defined (x0, "start offset", "transmission" as a scalar, "impedance ratio", "set Δcontact", "moved least or most", the "O's contacts" column). The abstract's "the path of a single point sets it: the pole about which the distal phalanx turns" conflates a point path with a pole. Table I is hard to parse (column groups "Reused command / O's contacts / grid / Torque" and which entries are bold). Fig. 1(d) legend shows filled circles for thicknesses while data use open circles and squares. The title is two titles joined by a dash. Sec. IV-C and V pack many numbers into each sentence; a reader cannot easily tell which result is primary.

Desired outcome: the idea is worth presenting at Stage 1; I would ask for (a) an honest separation of what is tautological under the reused-command protocol, (b) Δcontact = 0 positive controls and cross-architecture points in Fig. 5, (c) a fairer C2 statement against 2R-remote, (d) a discussion of pad compliance, and (e) a concrete Stage-2 protocol with failure criteria. I lean accept at a borderline level.
