THOUGHT:

The paper argues that when a finger is redesigned to carry a thicker contact interface, the designer should keep the relative motion of the two contact faces, c(q) in the proximal-phalanx frame, rather than the skeleton motion. It ties c(q) to the finite pole of the distal phalanx. It gives clearance and coverage laws (Prop. 1, Prop. 2), a pole-shift law (Prop. 3, |Δc| = 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|), a trade-off (Eq. 7, Δcontact + Δskel ≥ 2 sin(qreq/2)|Δt|) and a fixed-axis lower bound (Cor. 1). Three studies follow. Study 1 compares coverage across four PIP architectures. In Study 2, the CFL changes c(q) by only 10–51% of the fixed-axis bound. Study 3 runs a simulated grasp-and-pull test with a reused flexion command, where the error in a measured friction, stiffness or shape effect follows Δcontact (ρ = 0.83, n = 15).

My intuitions:

1. The geometry is clean and, as far as I can check, correct. Lemma 1 is the standard fixed-point formula. Prop. 3 follows directly from c = p + R(c0 − p) with c0 shared: Δc = (I − R)Δp, whose norm is 2 sin(q/2)|Δp|. Eq. 7 is the triangle inequality on two pole offsets that differ by the face depth change, evaluated at qreq. Cor. 1 follows because a fixed axis is its own pole. None of this is deep. It is classical planar kinematics (Bottema & Roth, Burmester-type pole and centrode reasoning). The novelty is in how it is framed for interface studies, not in the math. Eq. 7 is close to self-evident: the face moved by Δt relative to the link line, so you cannot keep both. The paper does present it modestly, and that is good.

2. The most important question is whether Study 3 shows something non-trivial. Under a reused position command with stiffness K0, the squeeze on the object depends on how far past first contact the command goes. That depends on where the faces are at the commanded q, which is exactly c(q). The limit load in a friction test scales roughly as μN, so the error in Δ(limit load) is roughly the relative mismatch in normal force. The correlation with Δcontact is therefore close to built in by the protocol. The simulation confirms a mechanism that the authors themselves state is obvious ("a reused command closes each hand past its own first contact"). It is still useful as a sanity check with numbers attached, but it should not be sold as an independent empirical finding. The "skeleton does not predict it" result is also partly structural. By Eq. 7, at fixed thickness Δskel is pushed up when Δcontact is pushed down, so a negative or null correlation with Δskel (Fig. 1d reports ρ = −0.40) is expected. The partial correlation controlling for Δskel (0.88) helps, but the CI [0.64, 1.00] over 15 author-chosen hands is wide.

3. The hands in Study 3 were constructed "at set values of Δcontact", some keeping the skeleton and some changing it maximally. Hands sampled by the experimenter along the predictor axis are not a random sample of redesigns. The ρ and its CI depend on that design. The hands are also not independent: they share a reference and come in thickness families.

4. Significance depends on the claim that interface studies reuse joint commands, and that touch- or force-based closing is unclean. That claim has no citation. The paper's own numbers also cut against it. Force-matched closing gives 3–7% error, which beats R at 7 mm (21%) and 9 mm (41%). The argument that force sensing is "changed by the interface" is weak when the force comes from motor current or joint torque, though contact location does affect torque-to-force mapping. More importantly, an obvious software baseline is missing: model-based retargeting of the command. The authors already compensate the transmission in software ("control compensates it") using each hand's model. Why not also compensate the contact-surface motion, by commanding each hand to the flexion at which its faces reach O's face configuration for the placed object? That requires no sensing, only the CAD model, which is already in use. If such retargeting reaches a few percent error, the geometric redesign is mainly a convenience for object-agnostic replay (e.g., frozen policies). The paper should frame it that way and test it.

5. Dismissing spacers is also weak. Mounting every interface so its outer surface is at the same depth is standard experimental practice. The authors reject it because it costs 8.6–18.2 points of their own coverage objective. For an interface study, whether a few points of coverage matter more than a guaranteed Δcontact = 0 deserves argument, not assertion.

6. Study 1 / C2. CFL beats 2R-DD everywhere but loses to 2R-remote at 90° and at 120° for thin pads on the paper's own objective. The authors then switch to a second measure, pad length over Lf, on which CFL wins. That looks like moving the goalposts. C2 is stated against 2R-DD only, which is accurate but narrow. The abstract's "near-best contact surface" is fair.

7. Study 3 uses only CFL hands. The claim that the error follows Δcontact would be much stronger with fixed-axis (2R-remote) redesigns, where Δcontact is forced to the bound, and RCJ redesigns in the same plot. That would also test C1's practical consequence directly.

8. The shape result (ρ = 0.94) rests on n = 6. Yet the abstract says shape and stiffness effects "repeat this". With n = 6, Spearman ρ has a huge CI. That needs to be reported, or the claim softened.

9. Even the best redesign leaves 41% error at 9 mm. Keeping c(q) as well as the CFL can does not make reused-command studies clean. It reduces error by roughly 3x versus U. The paper should state this plainly, because it bounds the practical value.

10. Writing. The prose is terse, which is a deliberate style. But density makes several parts hard to follow: Prop. 1's S2 assumption, x0, "vacant length", contour length Lf(q), the "(0.21, 0)" in the Fig. 3 caption, and the meaning of "degenerate" designs. The title has two clauses and a subtitle and is hard to parse. "CoDe Hand" in the title suggests a built hand, but the paper analyses one planar finger in simulation with the MCP fixed. The Stage-2 plan is concrete and good, and it states what is learned from either outcome, as the venue asks. Related work leaves out the classical motion-generation / rigid-body-guidance synthesis literature (Burmester theory, centrodes). Δcontact minimization for a four-bar is essentially that problem. It also leaves out the literature on calibrated/kinematic retargeting of grasps across hands, which is the natural competitor.

11. Smaller technical points. Δcontact is a max over q ≤ qreq, but grasps are tested at q0 from 20° to 120°. Is a per-q0 local Δc a better predictor than the global max? Fig. 1 says 3.9× (one placement) while the text says 3.3× (median); this is consistent, but should be made explicit. In Cor. 1, "the same holds for a rolling joint of fixed roller radius" is asserted with a one-line proof sketch, while Fig. 4 shows RCJ below the bound in places, explained by remounting. That needs a clearer statement of which Δd applies. The MCP column is not kept, and the paper says so. But real grasps use the MCP, so the practical scope is a PIP-only finger.

Overall: a coherent, well-scoped Stage-1 paper with correct (if elementary) geometry, a careful optimization study and a simulation that confirms the stated mechanism. The main weaknesses: the empirical "why it matters" result is close to tautological under the chosen protocol, the strongest alternative protocols (force matching, model-based command retargeting, spacers) are dismissed rather than compared, and the evidence for generality (n = 6 for shape, CFL-only hands, author-chosen Δcontact levels) is thin. Methodology and hypotheses are fully laid out, as the venue requires, but the significance argument needs strengthening. I lean towards reject, and could be persuaded given the Stage-1 allowance for preliminary results.
