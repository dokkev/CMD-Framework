ASSESSMENT NOTES:
Perspective: contact mechanics, contact simulation and experimental methodology. I check whether each experiment tests what it claims and whether the statistics are adequate.

The paper makes three claims. (1) A planar finger with remote actuation and a crossed four-bar (CFL) PIP keeps more pad coverage than a servo-at-joint revolute finger at every pad thickness and required flexion. (2) A fixed axis cannot keep the contact-surface motion c(q) when the pad thickens, which gives a closed-form lower bound (Eq. 9). The CFL can be redesigned to 10-51% of that bound at near-best coverage. (3) In simulation, when an impedance setpoint is reused across redesigns, the error a redesign adds to a measured interface effect (friction or stiffness change in pull-out load) follows Delta_contact and not Delta_skel. The result replicates in a second simulator, with hydroelastic pads and in two further tasks, and it can reverse a conclusion.

Claim 1 rests on the optimization in Fig. 3a-c. The CFL beats 2R-DD in all 10 shared conditions, by 2.4-14.6 points. The margins are large compared with what an optimizer typically varies, but no seed spread is reported (three seeds, GA and NSGA-II), and the 2R-DD servo envelope model, which drives the result, is not specified. 2R-remote beats the CFL at 90 degrees and at thin pads at 120 degrees. The text acknowledges this, and the claim is correctly scoped to servo-at-joint. Evidence: adequate for an abstract.

Claim 2 is a clean geometric argument: Lemma 1, Prop. 3, Eq. 8 and Corollary 1. The bound is correct as stated: a fixed axis is its own pole, and the face depth change sets a pole offset. One caveat: Corollary 1 assumes c0 is kept, but Eq. 11 does not enforce c0. That is why 2R-DD and RCJ carry a start offset at q = 0 (Fig. 3g). The comparison with the bound is therefore partly apples to oranges, which the text half-acknowledges ("without which the CFL is least in 7 of 9"). Evidence: strong.

Claim 3 is the empirical core and the one I scrutinize.

(a) What the experiment tests. Sec. IV-B1 itself identifies the mechanism. Under a reused position-impedance setpoint, the error enters through preload: a face that sits elsewhere is pressed harder or softer, and E is about |N_H/N_O - 1|. Delta_contact is close to a direct measure of where the face sits relative to the object at q0. So the strong correlation largely confirms that a compliant position controller converts geometric interference into force. That is expected, not a discovery about the contact Jacobian as such. The decisive tests are the alternatives. Force matching leaves 3-7%. Re-zeroing at touch, which is common practice, makes the error follow the skeleton instead. The paper reports both honestly, but they narrow the claim to "under a reused position setpoint without contact-triggered zeroing". The abstract and conclusion state it more generally.

(b) Statistical discrimination. The 15/14-hand sets are author-constructed. Per thickness they contain R, U and redesigns chosen to reach a set Delta_contact "while keeping or changing the skeleton most". The correlation between Delta_contact and Delta_skel across hands is set by this construction. Accordingly, Delta_skel's rho changes sign across columns of Table I (-0.40, -0.51, 0.22, 0.71, 0.88). The paper says itself that at 14-16 hands Delta_contact's lead over Delta_skel is not significant; only the 45-hand grid, at a single thickness (7 mm), separates them. Hands share a design pipeline and a reference, and the error is a median over placements and directions where both hands hold, so the effective sample size is smaller than the hand count. Holm correction, the hand bootstrap, partial correlations (0.88/0.91 with Delta_skel held) and pre-registration are good practice and lift the work above typical simulation studies. The "near 20% below about 1 mm" threshold rests on about three hands (R at 5, 7 and 9 mm). Fig. 4b-d show a saturating relation. Above about 1.5 mm, E scatters over 30-300% with little dependence on Delta_contact, so rho is driven mostly by the few low-Delta_contact hands.

(c) Contact modeling. The Isaac Lab runs use point contact for 5-9 mm "soft" pads. The stiffness manipulation (2.5 -> 1.0 kN/m) is a contact-spring change, not a distributed compliant pad. The hydroelastic check uses 0.1 MPa over 3 mm on every hand, so pad thickness and the growth of contact area with compliance are deliberately removed. That isolates kinematics, but it also means no experiment models what a thick compliant pad does to the contact patch, torsional friction or rolling. The "reversed conclusion" (Sec. IV-B3) therefore rests on point contact. Its ground truth is also unclear. O cannot carry a 7 mm pad (claim 1), so how is the "true" effect defined? The text gives both -37% and "41% less" for the soft 7 mm pad, which needs reconciling.

(d) Stage 2 plan. Four physical hands, a Page trend test over 20 blocks with 5 repetitions, and a load cell for preload: reasonable. With Delta_skel falling while Delta_contact rises, the design does discriminate. However, CFL backlash and pad thickness tolerances of a few tenths of a mm are comparable to R's 0.9 mm, and no power analysis is given.

Overall, the theory is sound and the simulation methodology is unusually careful: multiple simulators, pre-registration and correction for multiplicity. My scores rest on three points. The central empirical relation is close to a consequence of the protocol. The discrimination against the skeleton is not significant at the main sample sizes. The contact models do not represent thick compliant pads. Presentation is compact, but the experimental setup paragraph packs the hand sets, placements, the grid, E and the protocols into a few sentences, and terms such as O/R/U/M, q0 and "the grid" are hard to recover.
